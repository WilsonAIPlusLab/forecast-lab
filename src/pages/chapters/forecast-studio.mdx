<p class="lede">Your manager stops by your desk on a Friday: “What will retail sales look like next September, and how sure are you? I need something for Monday's planning meeting.” She does not want a residual plot or an AICc table. She wants one number, a range she can plan around, a sentence on why she should trust it, and a warning if something could go badly wrong. This chapter is about producing exactly that, quickly and honestly.</p>

You already have every tool you need. In the earlier chapters you built benchmarks, smoothing models, ARIMA, regressions, tree ensembles, intervals and a scorecard. What separates a class exercise from a forecast an employer will actually use is the **workflow** around those tools: a fixed sequence of steps, honest evaluation, and communication aimed at the person making the decision. In this closing chapter you will run that workflow end to end, first in a dashboard, then in code.

<KeyIdea>A professional forecast is a number, a range, a track record and a risk, delivered to someone who has to make a decision. The model is only one ingredient.</KeyIdea>

Two steps deserve a second look because students skip them most often:

- **Step 1, the decision and the loss.** A forecast of grocery sales for a store manager ordering milk and a forecast for an investor valuing the chain are different products, even on the same data. The first cares about next week and hates stock-outs; the second cares about next year and hates bias. Write the decision down before you open R.
- **Step 2, the data.** In this course alone you have met seasonally adjusted and unadjusted versions of the same series, $ millions next to $ billions, weekly data that had to be averaged to months, and a pandemic that broke every pattern in 2020. A quick plot and a one-line data note (source, units, SA or NSA) prevents most embarrassing mistakes.

## The Forecast Studio

Below is a complete forecasting dashboard. It runs entirely in your browser in a fraction of a second, without R. It follows steps 3 to 9 of the pipeline:

- **Benchmark and candidate models.** Three benchmarks (naive, seasonal naive, drift) and four exponential smoothing models from [Chapter 6](/chapters/exponential-smoothing/), plus an equal-weight combination.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup>
- **Rolling-origin evaluation.** Every method is re-fitted at each of the last 48 months before the cut-off and asked to forecast 1 to *h* months ahead ([Chapter 3](/chapters/benchmarks/)).<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> The scorecard averages the errors over horizons; the lower chart shows them horizon by horizon.
- **Intervals.** The fan chart's ranges are built from the charted method's own past errors (more on this below).
- **Communication.** An executive summary writes itself from the numbers, with buttons to copy it, download the chart as a slide-ready PNG, or copy a link to the exact view.

### Reading the dashboard

With the default settings the Studio tells you that **Holt-Winters multiplicative** is the best of the eight methods: its typical miss is \$8.8 billion one month ahead and \$12.8 billion twelve months ahead, 35% smaller than the seasonal naive rule's. It expects retail sales of about **\$652.7 billion in September 2027**, with an 80% range of \$626.1 to \$658.2 billion.

Notice two things about that range. It is **lopsided**: it reaches \$26.5 billion below the forecast but only \$5.6 billion above. And the summary flags the reason as its key risk: over the past four years this method has tended to *over*-forecast, by about \$4.9 billion on average. A range built from the method's real errors carries that bias along, which is exactly what you want a decision-maker to see.

<Callout type="math" title="How the Studio builds its ranges">
Let $e_{o,h} = y_{o+h} - \hat y_{o+h \mid o}$ be the error the method made when it forecast $h$ months ahead from origin $o$, for each of the last 48 origins (fewer at long horizons, because the actual value must already be known). The 80% range for the real forecast $h$ months ahead is

$$
\Big[\;\hat y_{T+h\mid T} + q_{0.10}(e_{\cdot,h}),\;\; \hat y_{T+h\mid T} + q_{0.90}(e_{\cdot,h})\;\Big],
$$

where $q_p$ is the $p$-th sample quantile (R's default, type 7), and the 50% range uses $q_{0.25}$ and $q_{0.75}$. Nothing here assumes normal errors or a correct model, which is the appeal. The price: it assumes the next year's errors look like the last four years' errors, and with 25 to 48 errors per horizon the band edges are rough. That is why the Studio shows no 95% range: it would rest on the one or two most extreme misses. [Chapter 10](/chapters/uncertainty/) develops the same idea further as *conformal* prediction.

Each smoothing model's parameters are re-estimated at every origin by minimizing one-step squared errors with the Nelder–Mead method, so no origin ever sees data from its own future. The parameter limits match R's `ets()` ($\beta \le \alpha$, $\gamma \le 1 - \alpha$, damping between 0.8 and 0.98), but `ets()` maximizes a likelihood rather than minimizing squared errors, so its estimates can differ a little. The MASE column divides each method's MAE by the in-sample seasonal naive MAE, as R's `accuracy()` does.
</Callout>

### What surprised you: forecasts made just before a shock

The dashboard becomes a time machine when you drag **Data through** backward. The gold line then shows what actually happened after the cut-off, and the note below the chart counts how many months landed inside the 80% range. Try two dates on retail sales:

- **September 2019** ([open this view](/chapters/forecast-studio/?s=RSXFSN&end=2019-09&h=12&f=best)). Holt-Winters additive wins the scorecard with a typical one-month miss of only \$6.5 billion, and its ranges are narrow. Then March and April 2020 arrive: only **4 of the 12** actual months land inside the 80% range. Nothing in four calm years of errors could have warned you about a pandemic.
- **June 2021** ([open this view](/chapters/forecast-studio/?s=RSXFSN&end=2021-06&h=12&f=best)). Now the track record *includes* 2020, and the summary's key risk says so. The best model has latched onto the stimulus-fueled boom of early 2021 and extrapolates it: it expects \$670.7 billion in June 2022. The actual figure was \$600.7 billion, and **0 of 12** months landed inside the range.

Neither failure is a bug. They are the honest limits of forecasting from the past, and they are why steps 7 (coverage) and the monitoring section below exist. Your executive summary should always say what the forecast assumes, so that when the world changes, your reader knows the forecast has expired.

## Reproduce it in code

A dashboard is for exploring; a script is what you ship. The lab below rebuilds the Studio's default view in about 40 lines of R, with two upgrades the Studio cannot do instantly in a browser: R's automatic `ets()` model selection and `auto.arima()`. Both choose their model form using only the data **before the first forecast origin**, and every model is **re-estimated from scratch at every origin** on the data available at that date. Re-fitting ARIMA 48 times would take too long in your browser, so the lab uses 12 origins, one every four months over the same four years. It then builds the same kind of empirical fan chart and writes the executive summary with `sprintf()`.

When you run it, compare with the dashboard:

- **The benchmark agrees.** Seasonal naive scores an MAE of \$16.41 billion on the lab's 12 origins and \$16.47 billion on the Studio's 48. Same data, same rule, nearly the same number: a useful check that the two pipelines are set up alike.
- **Using only data through September 2022 (the first origin), `ets()` picks ETS(M,Ad,M) and `auto.arima()` picks ARIMA(0,1,2)(0,1,1)[12].** Re-fitted at each origin, ETS wins with an MAE of \$12.23 billion (MASE 0.56). ARIMA scores \$14.16 billion, and their combination \$12.55 billion: here the average does *not* beat the better ingredient, because ARIMA has tended to over-forecast (bias −\$6.9 billion) while ETS is almost unbiased. Notice also how the edge over seasonal naive shrinks with the horizon: ETS misses by \$9.6 billion one month ahead but \$15.7 billion twelve months ahead, against about \$17 billion for seasonal naive.
- **The headline is similar.** R expects \$641.8 billion in September 2027, with an 80% range of \$624.7 to \$663.9 billion; the Studio said \$652.7 billion (\$626.1 to \$658.2 billion). Two honest pipelines, same story: roughly \$640 to \$655 billion, give or take \$15 to \$25 billion. The Python version re-fits statsmodels' damped Holt-Winters and `SARIMAX` at the same 12 origins: its ARIMA MAE matches R's to two decimals (\$14.16 billion), and its Holt-Winters model, close to but not identical to ETS(M,Ad,M), forecasts \$638.2 billion.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Why the lab re-fits at every origin">
A shortcut you will see online re-uses the parameters estimated on the full sample and only re-runs the model at each past origin. It is much faster, but those parameters were estimated with data from *after* the origin: look-ahead leakage, the sin from [Chapter 3](/chapters/benchmarks/). The same goes for choosing the model form on the full sample and then scoring it on part of that sample. That is why this lab selects forms on data before the first origin and re-estimates everything at each origin. When time is short, use fewer origins, not leaky ones.
</Callout>

## Writing for decision-makers

The executive summary is the only part of your work most readers will see. The Studio's generator follows five rules that you should follow whenever you write one by hand:

1. **Bottom line up front.** The first sentence is the forecast: a number, a date and units. Not the method, not the data, not a caveat.
2. **A range, not just a point.** “About \$653 billion, 80% range \$626 to \$658 billion” lets a planner size inventory or a budget buffer. A point forecast alone invites false precision.
3. **A track record in plain words.** “Its typical miss was \$8.8 billion one month ahead” beats “MASE = 0.49”. Translate every metric into the reader's units, and compare with a rule they understand (“35% smaller than using the same month last year”).
4. **One key risk.** Name the single thing most likely to make the forecast wrong, and in which direction: a bias, a shock in the evaluation window, a near-tie between methods.
5. **What would change our view.** A concrete trigger (“two months in a row outside the 80% range”) tells the reader when to come back to you. It also commits you to monitoring.

Plus one chart (the fan chart, titled with the takeaway) and nothing else unless asked. Keep the diagnostics in an appendix.

<Callout type="business" title="Uncertainty does not cost you credibility">
Students often fear that giving a range makes them look unsure. The evidence points the other way. In large experiments, including one run with BBC News, stating the uncertainty around numbers as a range reduced readers' trust in those numbers only slightly.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup> What does destroy credibility is a confident forecast that misses. Overconfidence is common even among professionals: in a decade of surveys, U.S. chief financial officers' 80% intervals for next year's stock-market return contained the actual return only 36% of the time.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> Ranges built from your own past errors, as in the Studio, are a direct defense against that trap.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Judgmental adjustments: when to override the model">
Someone will want to “adjust” your forecast: sales thinks it is too low, finance thinks it is too high. A study of more than 60,000 forecasts at four supply-chain companies found that **large** adjustments, usually based on real information the model lacked (a promotion, a lost customer), tended to improve accuracy, while **small** tweaks often made it worse. **Upward** adjustments were much less likely to help than downward ones, a sign of optimism bias.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> The practical rule: adjust only for specific information the model cannot see, write the reason down, and track whether the adjustments add value, the forecast value added idea from [Chapter 3](/chapters/benchmarks/).
</Callout>

## After you ship: monitoring

A forecast is not finished when you send it. Every month a new actual value arrives, and you need to know whether the model is still working. The standard tool is the <Term def="The running sum of forecast errors divided by the mean absolute error (MAD). It stays near zero when errors are random and drifts away when they pile up on one side, signaling bias.">tracking signal</Term>, in use for exactly this purpose since at least the 1960s.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup> Enterprise planning software uses it too: SAP's materials-planning module, for example, flags a forecast for review when the absolute tracking signal exceeds a default limit of 4.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>

Three lessons from the monitor:

- **Bias shows up fast.** On e-commerce sales, the model deployed in January 2019 under-forecast every month from January to May, so the alarm fired in **May 2019**, long before COVID. Online sales were simply growing faster than the 2010–2018 trend the model had learned.
- **Re-fitting on alarm helps, modestly.** For e-commerce, re-estimating the parameters whenever the alarm fires cuts the average one-month miss from \$1.62 billion to \$1.41 billion, about 13%. For restaurant and bar sales it cuts \$3.00 billion to \$2.79 billion, about 7%.
- **The limit is a trade-off.** Drag the limit down to 2 and alarms fire constantly, most of them false. Push it to 8 and you learn about real breaks late. Pick the limit the way you pick a metric: by the cost of each kind of mistake.

## Reproducible and honest

Two habits separate forecasts people trust from forecasts that quietly cause damage.

**Reproducibility.** Anyone, including you in six months, should be able to rerun your forecast and get the same numbers. In practice that means: one script from raw data to final chart (no copy-pasting into spreadsheets); the series ID and the date you pulled the data written into the output, because economic data get revised; package versions recorded (the stamp in the lab above); and `set.seed()` before anything random, such as a random forest or simulated paths ([Chapter 11](/chapters/random-forests/)). Save the data file you used next to the script.

**Honesty about uncertainty.** A forecast influences real decisions about inventory, hiring and budgets. Three rules keep you on the right side:

- Never report accuracy on data you used to choose the model. Report rolling-origin results, and report the benchmark next to your model, even when the benchmark wins (it does on the unemployment rate: try it in the Studio).
- Never trim the range to make a forecast look sharper. If the range is too wide to be useful, say so; that is information too.
- Keep score of your own forecasts. Philip Tetlock's forecasting tournaments found that the most accurate forecasters were not the most confident ones but those who stated probabilities, tracked their record and updated often.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The tempting shortcuts">
Choosing the cut-off date that makes your model look best. Dropping 2020 without saying so. Quoting MAPE for a series near zero. Presenting a model's in-sample fit as its accuracy. Each makes the forecast look better today and costs you credibility the first time reality disagrees.
</Callout>

Good discussion points: the unemployment and e-commerce teams must defend a *benchmark* as their recommendation (a valuable lesson in itself); the housing team has the widest relative range and should talk scenarios; the payrolls and CPI teams should notice that their ranges sit mostly above the forecast because of past under-forecasting.
