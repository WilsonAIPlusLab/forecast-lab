<p class="lede">Your manager stops by your desk on a Friday: “What will retail sales look like next September, and how sure are you? I need something for Monday's planning meeting.” She does not want a residual plot or an AICc table. She wants one number, a range she can plan around, a sentence on why she should trust it, and a warning if something could go badly wrong. This chapter is about producing exactly that, quickly and honestly.</p>

You already have every tool you need. What turns a class exercise into a forecast an employer will use is the **workflow** around those tools: a fixed sequence of steps, a checklist of judgement calls, an honest test, and plain words for the person making the decision. In this closing chapter you run that workflow end to end, in a dashboard, in code, and on a series of your choice.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Write the data note before the first model">
One line, at the top of every script and every report: *series, FRED ID, units, frequency, SA/NSA, sample.* For the Studio's default series: “U.S. retail sales excluding food services, `RSXFSN`, \$ millions per month (we report \$ billions), monthly, not seasonally adjusted, January 2010 to September 2026.” It catches classic traps: `HOUST` (an annual rate: 1,221 thousand in September 2026) and `HOUSTNSA` (the monthly count: 103.8 thousand) measure the same thing but differ by a factor of about 12.
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Choose the transformation for a reason you can say out loud">
Each transformation changes what your forecast *means*, so pick it for a reason you could explain to your manager:
<ul>
<li><b>Log</b>: “the swings grow with the level, and I want to talk in percentages.” (Multiplicative Holt-Winters does the same job.)</li>
<li><b>Growth rate or difference</b>: “the level wanders, so I model the changes” (Chapter 7).</li>
<li><b>Year-over-year change</b>: “the data are not seasonally adjusted.”</li>
<li><b>Real (deflated)</b>: “the question is about quantities, not dollars.”</li>
</ul>
If you cannot finish the sentence, stay in levels. Then test the choice: on retail sales, proportional seasonal swings lower the Studio's rolling-origin error from \$12.6 to \$10.7 billion, but on light vehicle sales they raise it from 63.0 to 70.6 thousand vehicles.
</Callout>

## The Forecast Studio

Below is a complete forecasting dashboard. It runs in your browser in a fraction of a second, without R, and follows steps 3 to 9 of the pipeline:

- **Benchmarks and models.** Naive, seasonal naive and drift, four exponential smoothing models from [Chapter 6](/chapters/exponential-smoothing/), and an equal-weight combination.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup>
- **Rolling-origin evaluation.** Every method is re-fitted at each of the last 48 months before the cut-off and forecasts 1 to *h* months ahead ([Chapter 3](/chapters/benchmarks/)).<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> The scorecard averages over horizons; the lower chart shows each horizon.
- **Intervals.** The fan chart's ranges come from the charted method's own past errors, with no assumption of normal errors (details in Beyond the basics below).
- **Communication.** An executive summary writes itself, with buttons to copy it, download the chart as a PNG, or copy a link to the exact view.

### Reading the dashboard

With the default settings the Studio tells you that **Holt-Winters multiplicative** is the best of the eight methods: its typical miss is \$8.8 billion one month ahead and \$12.8 billion twelve months ahead, 35% smaller than the seasonal naive rule's. It expects retail sales of about **\$652.7 billion in September 2027**, with an 80% range of \$626.1 to \$658.2 billion.

That range is **lopsided**: it reaches \$26.5 billion below the forecast but only \$5.6 billion above. The summary flags the reason as its key risk: over the past four years this method has tended to *over*-forecast, by about \$4.9 billion. A range built from real errors carries that bias along, which is exactly what a decision-maker should see.

### What surprised you: forecasts made just before a shock

Drag **Data through** backward and the gold line shows what actually happened; the note below the chart counts the months inside the 80% range. Try retail sales:

- **September 2019** ([open this view](/chapters/forecast-studio/?s=RSXFSN&end=2019-09&h=12&f=best)). The ranges are narrow, yet only **4 of the 12** months land inside. Four calm years of errors could not warn of a pandemic.
- **June 2021** ([open this view](/chapters/forecast-studio/?s=RSXFSN&end=2021-06&h=12&f=best)). The best model extrapolates the stimulus boom: \$670.7 billion for June 2022, against an actual \$600.7 billion. **0 of 12** months land inside.

Neither failure is a bug; they are the honest limits of forecasting from the past. That is why your summary must say what the forecast assumes.

## One change at a time

The scorecard tells you *which* method wins, not *why*. To find out, build up from the benchmark one ingredient at a time and re-score after each step on the same test. Change three things at once and you cannot say which one did the work.

The ladder below starts at the naive rule and adds one ingredient per rung: smoothing, a damped trend, seasonality, then seasonality that scales with the level. Every rung is scored on the Studio's 48 rolling origins.

Three lessons from the ladder:

- **The ingredient that matters depends on the series.** On retail sales seasonality is everything. On consumer prices (seasonally adjusted) the trend is: it cuts the error from 5.2 to 1.9 index points, and seasonality on top makes things worse (2.5).
- **An ingredient can hurt.** On payroll jobs, proportional seasonal swings raise the error by 10%.
- **Compare with the best benchmark.** On payroll jobs and consumer prices the plain drift rule beats every rung. If nothing beats the benchmark, ship the benchmark and say so.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Hold the test fixed while you compare">
“One change at a time” has a twin: **one test for every candidate.** Keep the origins, horizons, sample start and metric identical, and decide the test *before* you look at results; otherwise a model can win just because its lucky months fell inside the window. And a small gain on a small test is a tie: with 8 origins, a 3% improvement is noise; with 48 origins and a 15% gain, you can believe it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Percent or percentage points?">
For rates (unemployment, interest rates, inflation), say **percentage points** for the change. If unemployment goes from 4.1% to 4.3%, it rose by **0.2 percentage points**, which is about a 5% rise *relative* to its level. “Unemployment rose 0.2%” is ambiguous at best and wrong at worst. The same goes for forecast misses: the Studio reports the unemployment rate's typical miss as 0.09 percentage points one month ahead, not “0.09%”.
</Callout>

## Writing for decision-makers

The executive summary is the only part of your work most readers will see. The Studio's generator follows five rules; follow them when you write one by hand:

1. **Bottom line up front.** The first sentence is the forecast: a number, a date and units. Not the method, not a caveat.
2. **A range, not just a point.** “About \$653 billion, 80% range \$626 to \$658 billion” lets a planner size a buffer.
3. **A track record in plain words.** “Its typical miss was \$8.8 billion one month ahead, 35% smaller than using the same month last year” beats “MASE = 0.49”.
4. **One key risk,** with its direction: a bias, a shock in the evaluation window, a near-tie.
5. **What would change our view.** A concrete trigger (“two months in a row outside the 80% range”).

Strong sentences share four habits: a number **with units**, a **comparison** the reader understands, the **direction** of any risk, and honest words for uncertainty (“we expect”, “about”), never “will”; for drivers, “goes with”, never “causes”. ## Chapter checklist

- Follow the nine-step pipeline, from the decision and the loss to communication.
- **Before modelling**, write the data note (units, frequency, SA/NSA, real or nominal) and choose a transformation for a reason you can say out loud.
- **While modelling**, start from the benchmark, keep one honest test for every candidate, and change one thing at a time.
- **After modelling**, return to the reader's units, compare **like with like**, and state a range and what it assumes.
- Write the summary **bottom line first**: one number, one range, one track record, one risk, one trigger. Say “we expect” and “goes with”, never “will be” and “causes”.
- Report honestly: out-of-sample accuracy, the benchmark next to your model (even when it wins), and an untrimmed range.

#### How the Studio builds its ranges

<Callout type="math" title="How the Studio builds its ranges">
Let $e_{o,h} = y_{o+h} - \hat y_{o+h \mid o}$ be the error the method made when it forecast $h$ months ahead from origin $o$, for each of the last 48 origins (fewer at long horizons, because the actual value must already be known). The 80% range for the real forecast $h$ months ahead is

$$
\Big[\;\hat y_{T+h\mid T} + q_{0.10}(e_{\cdot,h}),\;\; \hat y_{T+h\mid T} + q_{0.90}(e_{\cdot,h})\;\Big],
$$

where $q_p$ is the $p$-th sample quantile (R's default, type 7), and the 50% range uses $q_{0.25}$ and $q_{0.75}$. Nothing here assumes normal errors or a correct model, which is the appeal. The price: it assumes the next year's errors look like the last four years' errors, and with 25 to 48 errors per horizon the band edges are rough. That is why the Studio shows no 95% range: it would rest on the one or two most extreme misses. [Chapter 10](/chapters/uncertainty/#intervals-for-any-model-split-conformal-prediction) developed the same idea as *conformal* prediction.

Each smoothing model's parameters are re-estimated at every origin by minimizing one-step squared errors with the Nelder–Mead method, so no origin ever sees data from its own future. The parameter limits match R's `ets()` ($\beta \le \alpha$, $\gamma \le 1 - \alpha$, damping between 0.8 and 0.98), but `ets()` maximizes a likelihood rather than minimizing squared errors, so its estimates can differ a little. The MASE column divides each method's MAE by the in-sample seasonal naive MAE, as R's `accuracy()` does.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Why the lab re-fits at every origin">
A shortcut you will see online re-uses the parameters estimated on the full sample and only re-runs the model at each past origin. It is much faster, but those parameters were estimated with data from *after* the origin: look-ahead leakage, the sin from [Chapter 3](/chapters/benchmarks/). The same goes for choosing the model form on the full sample and then scoring it on part of that sample. That is why the lab selects forms on data before the first origin and re-estimates everything at each origin. When time is short, use fewer origins, not leaky ones.
</Callout>

#### Report in levels, even if you modelled changes

If your model works with logs, growth rates or year-over-year changes, translate the result back into the level your reader cares about before you write a word. A forecast of “+5.0% on last December” for retail sales becomes \$694.1 billion × 1.050 ≈ **\$728.8 billion**, close to the model's level forecast of \$728.6 billion (the growth rate is rounded). For forecasts made in logs, exponentiate the forecast and the range ends, as in [Chapter 10](/chapters/uncertainty/#forecasting-in-logs-and-coming-back-to-dollars); the result is a median, and the range stays lopsided, as it should.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup> Also remember that retail sales are **nominal**: if prices rise about 3% over the same year, a 2.8% rise in dollar sales means roughly flat volumes ([Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#real-vs-nominal-more-stuff-or-higher-prices)).

#### Model sentences: weak and strong

A phrasebook that extends the wording workshop to more kinds of claim:

| What you report | Weak | Strong | Why it is better |
|---|---|---|---|
| A point forecast | “Sales will be \$652.66 billion.” | “We expect retail sales (not seasonally adjusted) to be about \$653 billion in September 2027, 2.8% above September 2026.” | Units, date, SA/NSA, like-with-like comparison; “expect” and “about” signal uncertainty |
| An 80% range | “We are 80% sure the model is right.” | “Our 80% range is \$626 to \$658 billion: we expect the outcome to land in it about 8 times out of 10.” | The range is about the outcome, not the model |
| A lopsided range | “±\$16 billion” | “\$27 billion below to \$6 billion above our forecast” | Says where the risk lies |
| A track record | “MASE = 0.49.” | “Its typical miss was \$8.8 billion one month ahead, 35% smaller than using the same month last year.” | The reader's units and a benchmark they understand |
| A model that loses | (leave the benchmark out) | “No model beat simply carrying forward the latest value, so we recommend that rule.” | Honest, and still useful |
| A change in a rate | “Unemployment will rise 0.2%.” | “We expect unemployment to rise by about 0.2 percentage points.” | Points, not percent |
| A driver (Chapter 4) | “Unemployment causes car sales to fall by 0.90.” | “In months when unemployment was 1 point higher, car sales ran about 900,000 a year lower, holding the model's other drivers fixed.” | Units on both sides; association, not cause |
| A limitation | “Results should be interpreted with caution.” | “The forecast assumes no recession; in 2020 a shock pushed 8 of 12 months outside a range like this.” | Specific, with evidence |

The last row's evidence comes from the Studio's September 2019 view (4 of 12 months inside the range, so 8 outside; see [What surprised you](#what-surprised-you-forecasts-made-just-before-a-shock) above).

#### Uncertainty does not cost you credibility

Students often fear that giving a range makes them look unsure. The evidence points the other way. In large experiments, including one run with BBC News, stating the uncertainty around numbers as a range reduced readers' trust in those numbers only slightly.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup> What does destroy credibility is a confident forecast that misses. Overconfidence is common even among professionals: in a decade of surveys, U.S. chief financial officers' 80% intervals for next year's stock-market return contained the actual return only 36% of the time.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> Ranges built from your own past errors, as in the Studio, are a direct defense against that trap.

#### Judgmental adjustments: when to override the model

Someone will want to “adjust” your forecast: sales thinks it is too low, finance thinks it is too high. A study of more than 60,000 forecasts at four supply-chain companies found that **large** adjustments, usually based on real information the model lacked (a promotion, a lost customer), tended to improve accuracy, while **small** tweaks often made it worse. **Upward** adjustments were much less likely to help than downward ones, a sign of optimism bias.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> The practical rule: adjust only for specific information the model cannot see, write the reason down, and track whether the adjustments add value, the forecast value added idea from [Chapter 3](/chapters/benchmarks/).

#### After you ship: monitoring

A forecast is not finished when you send it. Every month a new actual value arrives, and you need to know whether the model is still working. The standard tool is the <Term def="The running sum of forecast errors divided by the mean absolute error (MAD). It stays near zero when errors are random and drifts away when they pile up on one side, signaling bias.">tracking signal</Term>, in use for exactly this purpose since at least the 1960s.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup> Enterprise planning software uses it too: SAP's materials-planning module, for example, flags a forecast for review when the absolute tracking signal exceeds a default limit of 4.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>

#### Reproducible and honest

Two habits separate forecasts people trust from forecasts that quietly cause damage.

**Reproducibility.** Anyone, including you in six months, should be able to rerun your forecast and get the same numbers. In practice that means: one script from raw data to final chart (no copy-pasting into spreadsheets); the series ID and the date you pulled the data written into the output, because economic data get revised ([Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#data-get-revised-vintages-and-alfred)); package versions recorded (the stamp in the monitoring lab above); and `set.seed()` before anything random, such as a random forest or simulated paths ([Chapter 11](/chapters/random-forests/)). Save the data file you used next to the script.

**Honesty about uncertainty.** A forecast influences real decisions about inventory, hiring and budgets. Three rules keep you on the right side:

- Never report accuracy on data you used to choose the model. Report rolling-origin results, and report the benchmark next to your model, even when the benchmark wins (it does on the unemployment rate: try it in the Studio).
- Never trim the range to make a forecast look sharper. If the range is too wide to be useful, say so; that is information too.
- Keep score of your own forecasts. Philip Tetlock's forecasting tournaments found that the most accurate forecasters were not the most confident ones but those who stated probabilities, tracked their record and updated often.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup>
