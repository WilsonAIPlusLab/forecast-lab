<p class="lede">Your new manager has read that "AI beats traditional forecasting" and asks you to replace the exponential-smoothing model behind the retail sales forecast with machine learning. You could just say yes. Or you could build the machine-learning model properly, put it on the same scorecard as everything else, and report what actually wins. This chapter does the second.</p>

Machine learning has a mixed record in forecasting. In a 2018 study, eight popular machine-learning methods were less accurate than simple statistical methods on 1,045 monthly series from the M3 competition, at every horizon.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> Two years later the M5 competition, built on Walmart sales data, was won by tree-based models (LightGBM), which beat the statistical benchmarks.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> So the honest answer to "can ML beat our statistical models?" is: **sometimes, if you build it carefully.** By the end of this chapter you will know what "carefully" means, and you will have run the race yourself.

<KeyIdea>A machine-learning model doesn't see a time series. It sees a table. Forecasting with ML is mostly the craft of building that table honestly.</KeyIdea>

## From a series to a table

Standard machine-learning models such as trees and forests learn a mapping from **features** (inputs, the columns you give it) to a **target** (the number to predict). They have no built-in idea of time. So the first job is to rewrite the series as rows: each month becomes one row whose target is that month's value, and whose features are things you already knew *before* the month began.

<div class="breakout">
<SeriesToTable client:load />
</div>

The standard features for a monthly series:

| Feature | Example | Captures |
|---|---|---|
| **Lags** $y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}, \dots$ | sales last month, two months ago | momentum, persistence |
| **Seasonal lag** $y_{t-12}$ | sales in the same month last year | seasonality |
| **Rolling statistics** | mean or standard deviation of the last 3 or 12 months, *ending at t − 1* | local level, recent volatility |
| **Calendar** | month of the year as a category (11 dummy variables), number of weekends, holidays | fixed seasonal effects |
| **Trend index** | 1, 2, 3, … | slow drift (but see the warning in the extrapolation section below) |
| **Exogenous drivers, lagged** | building permits last month, consumer sentiment last month | outside information |

The last row needs care. A driver is only useful if its value is **known when you make the forecast**. Building permits for March are published in mid-April, together with March housing starts,<sup>[7](#refs)</sup> so to forecast April starts on April 1 you can use February's permits and starts but not March's. Lag every driver by at least the publication delay.

### Why these features? Each one answers a question you would ask anyway

Feature engineering is where most of the real work in ML forecasting happens. A forest can only find patterns that some column makes visible. So think of each feature as a question an experienced analyst would ask before guessing next month:

- **"What happened recently?"** Lags $y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}$. If growth was strong last month, is it likely to stay strong? Lags let the model learn momentum (or bounce-back).
- **"What usually happens in this month?"** The seasonal lag $y_{t-12}$ and the calendar month. A December is not a February. These two features are the forest's way of knowing the calendar, and on NSA retail sales they turn out to matter most.
- **"What is the recent level, without the noise?"** A rolling mean of the last 3 or 12 months smooths out one-off spikes, so the model can tell a real shift from a single odd month.
- **"Is anything outside the series pointing up or down?"** Lagged drivers such as permits or sentiment, used only with the values that are already published.

A model cannot invent a question you never asked. If December sales depend on how many shopping weekends fall before Christmas, and no column says so, no amount of tuning will find it.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Build features in the units you will forecast, and look at them first">
Retail sales here are in \$ millions, **not seasonally adjusted**, and they trend upward. So the lab forecasts the monthly growth rate in percent, `100 * diff(log(sales))`, and builds every lag from that same growth series (see [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#transformations-every-analyst-uses) for why a log difference is close to a percent change). Two habits pay off. First, **plot each feature against the target** before modeling: in the 2011–2022 training rows, this month's growth has a correlation of 0.91 with growth in the same month last year, but −0.33 with last month's growth (a strong month tends to be followed by a weaker one). That tells you seasonality will dominate before you fit anything. Second, **look for odd months**: in 2020, April fell by about 12% and May jumped by about 20% (in log points), swings that no normal April or May shows. A tree will happily give such months a leaf of their own.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Three ways to leak the future into your features">
<ul>
<li><strong>A rolling window that includes the target.</strong> A "3-month average" computed over months t − 2, t − 1 and t contains the answer. Switch on the leak in the explorer above to see it.</li>
<li><strong>A driver that isn't published yet.</strong> This month's unemployment rate cannot help forecast this month's retail sales if it comes out a week after you need the forecast.</li>
<li><strong>A random train/test split.</strong> Machine-learning tutorials shuffle rows before splitting. With time series that lets the model learn from 2024 to "predict" 2023. Always split by date.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="The shift test: every rolling window must end last month">
The most common real-world leak is not a typo; it is a library default. In R, `stats::filter(x, rep(1/3, 3), sides = 1)` averages months t − 2, t − 1 **and t**. In Python, `g.rolling(3).mean()` does the same. Both are fine for describing the past and wrong as forecasting features. The fix is one shift: `lag_k(..., 1)` in R or `.shift(1)` in Python. </Callout>

## Regression trees: twenty questions for numbers

A <Term def="A model that predicts a number by asking a sequence of yes/no questions about the features (is month = December? was growth last month above 2%?) and returning the average target of the training rows that gave the same answers.">regression tree</Term> forecasts by asking yes/no questions. Is it January? Was growth last month above 1.5%? Each answer sends the month down a branch, and at the end of the path, in a **leaf**, the forecast is simply the average of the training months that ended up there.

How does the tree choose its questions? Greedily. For every feature and every possible cut point, it computes how much the cut would reduce the **sum of squared errors** (SSE) inside the group, and it keeps the best one. Then it repeats inside each half. This algorithm is called CART.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> Try it yourself on retail sales, with just two features.

<div class="breakout">
<GrowTree client:load />
</div>

The first cut you find (or that **Best split** finds) separates January from everything else: January sales fall by about 23 log points from December on average (roughly 20% in plain percent; the log shortcut stretches for moves this big), while the other months grow by about 2.5%. The second cut isolates December. The tree has discovered seasonality with no one telling it about calendars. Notice the dots near the top and bottom edges: those are the COVID months of spring 2020, when sales collapsed and rebounded by over 20% in a single month. A deep tree will happily give them a leaf of their own.

Now drag **Leaves** to 40. The training error keeps falling, because each new leaf fits the training months more closely. The test error (2023 onward) stops improving at around 20 leaves and then drifts up. That is <Term def="Fitting the noise in the training data instead of the pattern, so the model looks better in-sample and worse on new data.">overfitting</Term>, and trees are prone to it.

<Callout type="math" title="What a split minimizes">
A node holds training months $i \in S$ with targets $y_i$ and mean $\bar y_S$. Its error is $\text{SSE}(S) = \sum_{i \in S} (y_i - \bar y_S)^2$. A cut on feature $j$ at value $c$ splits $S$ into $L = \lbrace i : x_{ij} \le c \rbrace$ and $R = \lbrace i : x_{ij} > c \rbrace$. CART picks the $(j, c)$ that maximizes the reduction

$$\Delta = \text{SSE}(S) - \text{SSE}(L) - \text{SSE}(R).$$

Only cut points halfway between neighboring observed values need checking, so the search is fast. A leaf predicts $\bar y$ of its months, which is the constant that minimizes squared error inside it. That is why a tree's predictions are **piecewise constant**: a staircase, never a slope.
</Callout>

In R, `rpart()` grows the tree and the lab draws it. With all four features (two lags, the seasonal lag and the month), its first question is not "is it January?" but "was growth in the same month last year below about −15%?" Only Januaries are that weak, so the seasonal lag finds January by itself.

### Reading a tree in plain words

A tree is the easiest model in this course to read aloud, because it *is* a list of if-then rules. Take the lab's depth-3 tree and follow a December down it:

1. "Was growth in the same month last year at least −15.3%?" **Yes.** (Only Januaries fall further, so this question really asks "is it January?")
2. "Is the month March, May or December?" **Yes.** (These are the months that usually jump.)
3. "Was growth two months ago below 5.2%?" **Yes.** (For a December, two months ago is October, which is usually a quiet month.)

The December lands in a leaf of **23 training months**, and the forecast is their average growth: **+13.4%**. In words: *"Across the 23 past March, May and December months that followed a quiet month two months earlier, sales grew 13.4% on average, so the tree expects the same."* To get dollars, apply it to November's level: November sales × $e^{0.134}$, about 14% more.

Three things to notice when you read any tree:

- **A split is a question, not an effect.** "g12 ≥ −15.3" does not say that higher growth last year *causes* anything. The tree found that this cut separates the training months into two groups with very different averages.
- **The leaf size is your sample size.** A forecast from a leaf of 23 months is an average of 23 numbers; a leaf of 5 months is a noisy average of 5. Look at the `n` next to every leaf.
- **The forecast is an average of real past months.** That is why it is easy to explain ("months like this one grew 13% on average") and why it can never go beyond the most extreme past month.

## The extrapolation trap

Here is the property of trees that matters most for economic data, and that most machine-learning tutorials never mention. Look again at how a leaf forecasts: it returns an **average of training targets**. An average can never be larger than the largest value it averages.

Prices, nominal sales, GDP, employment, house prices: most series you will forecast at work trend upward. A tree-based model on their levels will look fine in a backtest that happens to stay inside the historical range, then quietly flat-line the first time the series makes a new high. ### Three ways out: growth rates, differences, detrending

The fix is always the same idea: **give the tree a target that stays inside the range it has already seen**, then convert its forecast back to the units you need. Three versions work, and you already know all three from earlier chapters.

| Model the... | The tree forecasts | Back to dollars | Stays in range because... |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Growth rate** (log difference) | `100 * diff(log(y))`, % per month | last level × exp(cumulative growth / 100) | percentage growth has no trend |
| **Difference** | `diff(y)`, \$ change per month | last level + cumulative sum of changes | monthly changes trend much less than levels |
| **Detrended series** | `log(y)` minus a fitted trend line | trend line + forecast gap, then exp() | the gap from trend wanders around zero |

Taking differences to remove a trend is exactly what ARIMA's "I" does ([Chapter 7](/chapters/arima/#the-cure-differencing)), and a log difference is approximately a percent change ([Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#transformations-every-analyst-uses)). A tree needs the same treatment for a different reason: not because of unit roots, but because it can only repeat averages of what it has seen.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Prefer growth rates for series that grow by percentages">
Differences and growth rates did about equally well here, but they age differently. E-commerce sales rose by \$251 million a month on average in 2011 and by \$475 million in 2022: in dollars, the typical change has doubled, so a forest trained on early changes sees "small" numbers that no longer fit. In percent, the same months grew 1.05% and 0.54% a month: still the same order of magnitude. For anything that grows by percentages (sales, prices, GDP, employment), model the **log difference**, and use plain differences for series that move by similar amounts in their own units, such as an interest rate or an unemployment rate.
</Callout>

## Random forests: many trees, one average

A single deep tree is unstable: change a few training months and you can get a quite different tree. Leo Breiman's fix was to grow **many** trees on perturbed versions of the data and average them.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> <sup>[5](#refs)</sup> The labs use R's `randomForest` package, a port of Breiman and Cutler's original code.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup>

1. **Bootstrap.** Each tree is trained on a <Term def="A sample of the same size as the training set, drawn with replacement, so some months appear twice or more and about a third are left out.">bootstrap sample</Term> of the training rows. Averaging trees grown this way is called **bagging** (bootstrap aggregating).
2. **Random feature subsets.** At every split, a random forest only lets the tree choose among `mtry` randomly picked features. This makes the trees less alike, and averaging less-alike trees cancels more noise.
3. **Average.** The forest's forecast is the mean of all the trees' forecasts.

<ForestOfOpinions client:load />
</div>

A typical single tree misses the retail forecast by about \$15.8 billion a month on average. Averaging 200 trees cuts that to about \$10.8 billion, and most of the gain arrives within the first 20 or so trees. Switch to **Housing starts** for a sharper lesson: a typical single tree misses by about 112 thousand units, worse than simply repeating last month's value (95), while the forest of 200 trees misses by about 83 and beats it.

<Callout type="math" title="Why averaging helps, and why mtry helps more">
If each tree's prediction has variance $\sigma^2$ and any two trees' predictions have correlation $\rho$, the average of $B$ trees has variance

$$\rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2 .$$

The second term vanishes as you add trees. The first does not: correlated trees make correlated mistakes. Restricting each split to a random subset of `mtry` features lowers $\rho$, which is why a random forest usually beats plain bagging. This is also why more trees never cause overfitting in a forest; they just stop helping.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

Two extras come free with bootstrapping. Each tree never sees about a third of the rows, its **out-of-bag** (OOB) rows. Scoring every row with only the trees that did not train on it gives the <Term def="Out-of-bag error: the forest's error on each training row, using only the trees whose bootstrap sample left that row out. A built-in estimate of out-of-sample error.">OOB error</Term>, a validation score that needs no hold-out set. And shuffling one feature in the OOB rows and measuring how much the error grows gives **permutation importance**: how much the forest relies on that feature.

The forest explains about 90% of the variance of monthly growth out-of-bag, with an OOB error of about 3.0 percentage points. Permutation importance puts seasonality far ahead: shuffling the month raises the OOB mean squared error by about 840%, and shuffling the seasonal lag by about 630%, against about 130% and 110% for the two recent lags. (R's default `%IncMSE` column is a scaled score, not a percentage, so the lab asks for the raw increase.) Its forecast from January 2023 onward misses actual sales by \$12,980 million a month on average.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Is OOB error a fair test for time series?">
OOB rows are chosen at random, so a tree may train on 2021 and be scored on 2019. For a model whose features are only lags of the series, this is less of a problem than you might fear: Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo show that ordinary cross-validation is valid for purely autoregressive models when the errors are uncorrelated.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> That guarantee needs a model that captures the dynamics (leftover autocorrelation in the errors breaks it) and a stable series (structural breaks such as COVID, or trending levels, break it too). And OOB scores one-step fits on scattered months, while your real job is a multi-step forecast from today. So treat OOB error as a quick in-sample check, and make the decisions that matter (tuning, model choice) with a time-ordered evaluation, as below.
</Callout>

## Reading variable importance

The importance chart is the slide everyone wants, and the one most often misread. Start with what it **is**, in plain words. In the forest lab, shuffling the calendar month raises the out-of-bag mean squared error by about 840%. Read that as: *"If the forest could no longer tell which month it is, its squared misses would be about 9 times larger (its typical miss about 3 times larger)."* That is a statement about **how much this fitted model leans on that column**. Nothing more.

Now what it is **not**:

- **Not a coefficient.** A regression coefficient has a sign and units: "a 1-point rise in X goes with a 0.4% rise in Y, holding Z fixed" ([Chapter 4](/chapters/regression/#reading-a-regression-like-an-analyst)). Importance has neither. "g12: 630%" does not say whether a strong month last year means a strong or a weak month this year, or by how much. To see direction, look at the leaves (as in the tree section), or use SHAP values, which you will meet in [Chapter 12](/chapters/boosting-shap/): they have a sign and are in the target's units.
- **Not causal.** The calendar month does not *cause* spending. It stands in for holidays, weather, tax refunds and school calendars. Importance ranks what helps *predict*, which is all a forecaster needs, but it is no evidence for a policy or a marketing decision.
- **Not stable when features overlap.** The month and last year's growth in the same month (`g12`) both encode seasonality. With both in the model, they share the credit; remove one, and the other takes almost all of it.<sup>[11](#refs)</sup> <sup>[12](#refs)</sup>
- **Not fixed.** Change the random seed and the bars move. Rank order of large gaps is usually stable; small gaps are noise.

## Forecasting many months ahead: recursive or direct?

A model that predicts *next* month cannot directly give you the month after, because next month's value is one of its features. There are two standard ways around this.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>

In plain words: **recursive** is one forecaster who predicts next month, then treats that guess as if it were data to predict the month after, and so on. **Direct** is a team of twelve specialists. Specialist *h* answers one question only: "given what we know today, what will month *h* look like?" No specialist ever sees another's guess.

At one month ahead the two are identical (it is the same model). Further out, each strategy wins at some horizons; averaged over all twelve, recursive misses by about \$8,900 million a month and direct by about \$10,900 million on this series. The direct model for 6 months ahead is trained on rows like "growth at the origin → growth 6 months later". So its "last month" feature is really *growth six months before the target*, `lag_k(x, 6)`, not `lag_k(x, 1)`. ## Tuning without peeking

A forest has a few settings worth tuning: `mtry` (features per split) and `nodesize` (the smallest leaf; larger leaves give smoother trees). The tempting way to tune is to try several values and keep whichever scores best on the 2023 test period. Don't: then the test score is no longer an honest estimate, because you chose the model *by* it.

The other tempting shortcut is the one in every general machine-learning tutorial: **k-fold cross-validation with shuffled rows**. Deal the months into five random piles, train on four, score the fifth, repeat. For a trending series, that quietly answers the wrong question.

<Callout type="empirical" title="A leakage checklist for time-series validation">
<ul>
<li><strong>Folds go forward in time.</strong> Train on months up to an origin, score only months after it. Never shuffle.</li>
<li><strong>Features are rebuilt from the past only, inside every fold.</strong> Rolling means end at t − 1; a trend line, a scaling (mean and standard deviation) or a seasonal adjustment fitted on the full sample already "knows" the test years, so fit them on the training months of each fold.</li>
<li><strong>Respect publication lags.</strong> A driver enters with the lag at which it is actually published, not the month it describes.</li>
<li><strong>Tune on the folds, report on the test set once.</strong> If you change the model after seeing the test score, that score is no longer a test.</li>
<li><strong>Beware of numbers that are too good.</strong> An R² near 1, or a forest that beats ETS by half, is a reason to hunt for a leak before it is a reason to celebrate.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

## Chapter checklist

- Rebuild the series as a **table**: lags (momentum), a seasonal lag and the calendar month (seasonality), past-only rolling statistics (the recent level) and **lagged** drivers that are known at forecast time. Each feature should answer a question you can say out loud.
- Hunt for **leakage**: rolling windows that include the target (apply the shift test), drivers not yet published, statistics fitted on the full sample, random splits. A test set does not catch leaky features.
- A **regression tree** splits to minimize squared error and predicts leaf averages, so its forecasts are a staircase and it overfits when grown deep. Read a forecast as "the *n* past months that answered these questions the same way averaged *x*", in the target's units.
- Trees and forests **cannot extrapolate**: for a trending series, model the growth rate (log difference), the difference or the gap from a trend, then convert back to dollars. Prefer growth rates for series that grow by percentages.
- A **random forest** averages bootstrap trees with random feature subsets (`mtry`); OOB error and permutation importance come free.
- **Variable importance** says how much this model leans on a feature. It has no sign and no units, it is not causal, and correlated features share (and swap) the credit. Report rankings and the error cost of dropping a group of features.
- For multi-step forecasts choose **recursive** (one model fed its own forecasts) or **direct** (one model per horizon, with features known at the origin) by testing; tune with **time-ordered folds**, never shuffled ones and never on the test set.
- Put ML on the **same scorecard**, check its **seed sensitivity**, and consider **combining** it with a statistical model.
