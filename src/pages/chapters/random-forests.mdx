<p class="lede">Your new manager has read that "AI beats traditional forecasting" and asks you to replace the exponential-smoothing model behind the retail sales forecast with machine learning. You could just say yes. Or you could build the machine-learning model properly, put it on the same scorecard as everything else, and report what actually wins. This chapter does the second.</p>

Machine learning has a mixed record in forecasting. In a 2018 study, eight popular machine-learning methods were less accurate than simple statistical methods on 1,045 monthly series from the M3 competition, at every horizon.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> Two years later the M5 competition, built on Walmart sales data, was won by tree-based models (LightGBM), which beat the statistical benchmarks.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> So the honest answer to "can ML beat our statistical models?" is: **sometimes, if you build it carefully.** By the end of this chapter you will know what "carefully" means, and you will have run the race yourself.

<KeyIdea>A machine-learning model doesn't see a time series. It sees a table. Forecasting with ML is mostly the craft of building that table honestly.</KeyIdea>

## From a series to a table

Standard machine-learning models such as trees and forests learn a mapping from **features** (inputs, the columns you give it) to a **target** (the number to predict). They have no built-in idea of time. So the first job is to rewrite the series as rows: each month becomes one row whose target is that month's value, and whose features are things you already knew *before* the month began.

<div class="breakout">
<SeriesToTable client:load />
</div>

The standard features for a monthly series:

| Feature | Example | Captures |
|---|---|---|
| **Lags** $y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}, \dots$ | sales last month, two months ago | momentum, persistence |
| **Seasonal lag** $y_{t-12}$ | sales in the same month last year | seasonality |
| **Rolling statistics** | mean or standard deviation of the last 3 or 12 months, *ending at t − 1* | local level, recent volatility |
| **Calendar** | month of the year as a category (11 dummy variables), number of weekends, holidays | fixed seasonal effects |
| **Trend index** | 1, 2, 3, … | slow drift (but see the warning in the extrapolation section below) |
| **Exogenous drivers, lagged** | building permits last month, consumer sentiment last month | outside information |

The last row needs care: a driver is only useful if its value is **known when you make the forecast**. Building permits for March are published in mid-April,<sup>[7](#refs)</sup> so a forecast made on April 1 can use February's permits but not March's.

### Why these features? Each one answers a question you would ask anyway

A forest can only find patterns that some column makes visible. So think of each feature as a question an experienced analyst would ask before guessing next month:

- **"What happened recently?"** Lags $y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}$ let the model learn momentum (or bounce-back).
- **"What usually happens in this month?"** The seasonal lag $y_{t-12}$ and the calendar month. A December is not a February. On NSA retail sales these turn out to matter most.
- **"What is the recent level, without the noise?"** A rolling mean of the last 3 or 12 months smooths out one-off spikes.
- **"Is anything outside the series pointing up or down?"** Lagged drivers such as permits or sentiment, used only with values already published.

A model cannot invent a question you never asked: if no column says how many shopping weekends fall before Christmas, no amount of tuning will find that effect.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Build features in the units you will forecast, and look at them first">

So the lab forecasts the monthly growth rate in percent, `100 * diff(log(sales))`, and builds every lag from that same growth series ([Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#transformations-every-analyst-uses)). Then **plot each feature against the target** before modeling: in the 2011–2022 training rows, this month's growth has a correlation of 0.91 with growth in the same month last year, but −0.33 with last month's growth (a strong month tends to be followed by a weaker one). Seasonality will dominate before you fit anything. And **look for odd months**: in 2020, April fell by about 12% and May jumped by about 20% (in log points).
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="The shift test: every rolling window must end last month">
The most common real-world leak is a library default. In R, `stats::filter(x, rep(1/3, 3), sides = 1)` averages months t − 2, t − 1 **and t**. In Python, `g.rolling(3).mean()` does the same. ## Regression trees: twenty questions for numbers

A <Term def="A model that predicts a number by asking a sequence of yes/no questions about the features (is month = December? was growth last month above 2%?) and returning the average target of the training rows that gave the same answers.">regression tree</Term> forecasts by asking yes/no questions. Each answer sends the month down a branch, and at the end of the path, in a **leaf**, the forecast is simply the average of the training months that ended up there.

How does the tree choose its questions? Greedily. For every feature and every possible cut point, it computes how much the cut would reduce the **sum of squared errors** (SSE) inside the group, and it keeps the best one. Then it repeats inside each half. This algorithm is called CART.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> Try it yourself on retail sales, with just two features.

<div class="breakout">
<GrowTree client:load />
</div>

The first cut you find (or that **Best split** finds) separates January from everything else: January sales fall by about 23 log points from December on average (roughly 20% in plain percent), while the other months grow by about 2.5%. The second cut isolates December. The tree has discovered seasonality with no one telling it about calendars.

Now drag **Leaves** to 40. The training error keeps falling, because each new leaf fits the training months more closely. The test error (2023 onward) stops improving at around 20 leaves and then drifts up. That is <Term def="Fitting the noise in the training data instead of the pattern, so the model looks better in-sample and worse on new data.">overfitting</Term>, and trees are prone to it.

In R, `rpart()` grows the tree on all four features (two lags, the seasonal lag and the month). Its first question is "was growth in the same month last year below about −15%?" Only Januaries are that weak, so the seasonal lag finds January by itself.

### Reading a tree in plain words

A tree *is* a list of if-then rules, so it reads aloud easily. Take the lab's depth-3 tree and follow a December down it:

1. "Was growth in the same month last year at least −15.3%?" **Yes.** (Only Januaries fall further, so this question really asks "is it January?")
2. "Is the month March, May or December?" **Yes.** (These are the months that usually jump.)
3. "Was growth two months ago below 5.2%?" **Yes.** (For a December, two months ago is October, which is usually a quiet month.)

The December lands in a leaf of **23 training months**, and the forecast is their average growth: **+13.4%**. In words: *"Across the 23 past March, May and December months that followed a quiet month two months earlier, sales grew 13.4% on average, so the tree expects the same."* To get dollars, apply it to November's level: November sales × $e^{0.134}$, about 14% more.

When you read any tree, remember:

- **A split is a question, not an effect.** "g12 ≥ −15.3" does not say that higher growth last year *causes* anything. The cut separates the training months into two groups with very different averages.
- **The leaf size is your sample size.** A leaf of 23 months is an average of 23 numbers; a leaf of 5 months is a noisy average of 5. Look at the `n` next to every leaf.
- **The forecast is an average of real past months.** That is why it is easy to explain ("months like this one grew 13% on average") and why it can never go beyond the most extreme past month.

## The extrapolation trap

Here is the property of trees that matters most for economic data. A leaf forecasts an **average of training targets**, and an average can never be larger than the largest value it averages.

<KeyIdea>Trees and forests cannot extrapolate. For trending data, never feed them the level. Model growth rates, differences or a detrended series, then convert the forecast back.</KeyIdea>

In R, the forest on levels misses 2023-onward sales by about \$13.6 billion a month; the same forest on growth rates, by about \$5.6 billion. For series that grow by percentages (sales, prices, GDP, employment), the growth rate is the safe default; convert back with last level × exp(growth / 100), month after month.

<Callout type="business" title="Where this bites in practice">
Most series you will forecast at work trend upward. A tree-based model on their levels will look fine in a backtest that happens to stay inside the historical range, then quietly flat-line the first time the series makes a new high. If a vendor's "AI forecast" goes flat at a suspiciously round ceiling, ask whether it was trained on levels.
</Callout>

## Random forests: many trees, one average

A single deep tree is unstable: change a few training months and you get a different tree. Leo Breiman's fix was to grow **many** trees on perturbed versions of the data and average them.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> <sup>[5](#refs)</sup> The labs use R's `randomForest` package.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup>

1. **Bootstrap.** Each tree is trained on a <Term def="A sample of the same size as the training set, drawn with replacement, so some months appear twice or more and about a third are left out.">bootstrap sample</Term> of the training rows. Averaging trees grown this way is called **bagging** (bootstrap aggregating).
2. **Random feature subsets.** At every split, a random forest only lets the tree choose among `mtry` randomly picked features. This makes the trees less alike, and averaging less-alike trees cancels more noise.
3. **Average.** The forest's forecast is the mean of all the trees' forecasts.

<ForestOfOpinions client:load />
</div>

A typical single tree misses the retail forecast by about \$15.8 billion a month on average. Averaging 200 trees cuts that to about \$10.8 billion, with most of the gain in the first 20 or so trees. On **Housing starts**, a single tree (112 thousand units) loses to repeating last month's value (95), while the forest (83) beats it.

Two extras come free with bootstrapping. Each tree never sees about a third of the rows, its **out-of-bag** (OOB) rows. Scoring every row with only the trees that did not train on it gives the <Term def="Out-of-bag error: the forest's error on each training row, using only the trees whose bootstrap sample left that row out. A built-in estimate of out-of-sample error.">OOB error</Term>, a validation score that needs no hold-out set. And shuffling one feature in the OOB rows and measuring how much the error grows gives **permutation importance**: how much the forest relies on that feature.

The forest explains about 90% of the variance of monthly growth out-of-bag, with an OOB error of about 3.0 percentage points. Permutation importance puts seasonality far ahead: shuffling the month raises the OOB mean squared error by about 840%, and shuffling the seasonal lag by about 630%, against about 130% and 110% for the two recent lags. OOB rows are chosen at random, so a tree may train on 2021 and be scored on 2019. Treat OOB error as a quick check, and make decisions with a time-ordered evaluation, as below.

## Reading variable importance

The importance chart is the slide most often misread. Start with what it **is**. In the forest lab, shuffling the calendar month raises the out-of-bag mean squared error by about 840%. Read that as: *"If the forest could no longer tell which month it is, its squared misses would be about 9 times larger (its typical miss about 3 times larger)."* That is a statement about **how much this fitted model leans on that column**. Nothing more.

Now what it is **not**:

- **Not a coefficient.** A regression coefficient has a sign and units ([Chapter 4](/chapters/regression/#reading-a-regression-like-an-analyst)); importance has neither. "g12: 630%" does not say whether a strong month last year means a strong or a weak month this year. To see direction, look at the leaves, or use SHAP values ([Chapter 12](/chapters/boosting-shap/)), which have a sign and are in the target's units.
- **Not causal.** The calendar month does not *cause* spending. It stands in for holidays, weather and tax refunds. Importance ranks what helps *predict*; it is no evidence for a policy decision.
- **Not stable when features overlap.** The month and last year's growth in the same month (`g12`) both encode seasonality, so they share the credit. Drop the month and `g12`'s importance jumps from about 630% to about 1,150%, while the test error barely moves (1.83 to 2.02 percentage points). Drop both and the error nearly doubles, to 3.55.<sup>[11](#refs)</sup> <sup>[12](#refs)</sup>
- **Not fixed.** Change the random seed and the bars move. Rank order of large gaps is usually stable; small gaps are noise.

<Callout type="empirical" title="How to report variable importance honestly">
<ul>
<li><strong>Report a ranking, not a precise number.</strong> "Seasonal features matter most, recent lags much less" survives a new seed; "month = 837%" does not.</li>
<li><strong>Group features that carry the same information</strong> (month and the seasonal lag), and report what removing the group costs on time-ordered test data: here, nearly double the error.</li>
<li><strong>Say which importance you used.</strong> The R lab shows permutation importance on out-of-bag rows; scikit-learn's default is impurity importance, which can favor features with many distinct values. Never put the two on one chart.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

## Honest validation: folds that go forward in time

Choosing a model by its 2023 test score spoils the test: it is no longer an honest estimate. The usual machine-learning shortcut, **k-fold cross-validation with shuffled rows**, is worse for a trending series: it quietly answers the wrong question.

</Callout>

<KeyIdea>Machine learning earns its place the same way every model does: by beating the benchmarks on data it has never seen, in a fair, time-ordered test. Its biggest value is often as a different opinion to combine with, not as a replacement.</KeyIdea>

## Chapter checklist

- Rebuild the series as a **table** of lags, a seasonal lag, the calendar month, past-only rolling statistics and **lagged** drivers known at forecast time.
- Hunt for **leakage** (apply the shift test); a test set does not catch leaky features.
- Read a **tree** forecast as "the *n* past months that answered these questions the same way averaged *x*", in the target's units. Deep trees overfit.
- Trees and forests **cannot extrapolate**: for a trending series, model the growth rate, then convert back to dollars.
- A **random forest** averages bootstrap trees with random feature subsets (`mtry`); OOB error and permutation importance come free.
- **Variable importance** says how much this model leans on a feature: no sign, no units, not causal, shared among correlated features.
- Validate with **time-ordered folds**, never shuffled ones, and use the test set once. Put ML on the **same scorecard**, check its **seed sensitivity**, and consider **combining** it with a statistical model.

#### What a split minimizes

A node holds training months $i \in S$ with targets $y_i$ and mean $\bar y_S$. Its error is $\text{SSE}(S) = \sum_{i \in S} (y_i - \bar y_S)^2$. A cut on feature $j$ at value $c$ splits $S$ into $L = \lbrace i : x_{ij} \le c \rbrace$ and $R = \lbrace i : x_{ij} > c \rbrace$. CART picks the $(j, c)$ that maximizes the reduction

$$\Delta = \text{SSE}(S) - \text{SSE}(L) - \text{SSE}(R).$$

Only cut points halfway between neighboring observed values need checking, so the search is fast. A leaf predicts $\bar y$ of its months, which is the constant that minimizes squared error inside it. That is why a tree's predictions are **piecewise constant**: a staircase, never a slope.

#### Three ways out: growth rates, differences, detrending

The fix for the extrapolation trap is always the same idea: **give the tree a target that stays inside the range it has already seen**, then convert its forecast back to the units you need. Three versions work, and you already know all three from earlier chapters.

| Model the... | The tree forecasts | Back to dollars | Stays in range because... |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Growth rate** (log difference) | `100 * diff(log(y))`, % per month | last level × exp(cumulative growth / 100) | percentage growth has no trend |
| **Difference** | `diff(y)`, \$ change per month | last level + cumulative sum of changes | monthly changes trend much less than levels |
| **Detrended series** | `log(y)` minus a fitted trend line | trend line + forecast gap, then exp() | the gap from trend wanders around zero |

Taking differences to remove a trend is exactly what ARIMA's "I" does ([Chapter 7](/chapters/arima/#the-cure-differencing)), and a log difference is approximately a percent change ([Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#transformations-every-analyst-uses)). A tree needs the same treatment for a different reason: not because of unit roots, but because it can only repeat averages of what it has seen.

Differences and growth rates did about equally well here, but they age differently. E-commerce sales rose by \$251 million a month on average in 2011 and by \$475 million in 2022: in dollars, the typical change has doubled, so a forest trained on early changes sees "small" numbers that no longer fit. In percent, the same months grew 1.05% and 0.54% a month: still the same order of magnitude. For anything that grows by percentages (sales, prices, GDP, employment), model the **log difference**, and use plain differences for series that move by similar amounts in their own units, such as an interest rate or an unemployment rate.

#### Which past months vote?

A tree forecast is an average of past months, and so is a forest forecast: a weighted one. Pick a month to forecast; the purple dots are the training months the forecast is built from.

Switch to the **forest** and the forecast becomes a weighted average of 27 past months, with about 85% of the weight on past Decembers. For every January, about 99% of the weight sits on past Januaries. One surprise in the December list: May 2020, the COVID rebound month, gets almost 9% of the weight. While choosing splits, many trees grouped its +20% jump with the big December jumps, so it shares their leaves and votes on Christmas forecasts. That is how one extreme month can seep into forecasts for quite different months. A forest on these features behaves a lot like a smart seasonal naive: "this month will look like the same month in comparable past years."<sup>[13](#refs)</sup>

#### Why averaging helps

If each tree's prediction has variance $\sigma^2$ and any two trees' predictions have correlation $\rho$, the average of $B$ trees has variance

$$\rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2 .$$

The second term vanishes as you add trees. The first does not: correlated trees make correlated mistakes. Restricting each split to a random subset of `mtry` features lowers $\rho$, which is why a random forest usually beats plain bagging. This is also why more trees never cause overfitting in a forest; they just stop helping.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup>

#### Is OOB error a fair test for time series?

OOB rows are chosen at random, which is less of a problem than you might fear for a model whose features are only lags: Bergmeir, Hyndman and Koo show that ordinary cross-validation is valid for purely autoregressive models when the errors are uncorrelated.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> That guarantee needs a model that captures the dynamics (leftover autocorrelation in the errors breaks it) and a stable series (structural breaks, or trending levels, break it too). And OOB scores one-step fits on scattered months, while the real job is a multi-step forecast from today.

#### Forecasting many months ahead: recursive or direct?

A model that predicts *next* month cannot directly give you the month after, because next month's value is one of its features. There are two standard ways around this.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>

In plain words: **recursive** is one forecaster who predicts next month, then treats that guess as if it were data to predict the month after, and so on (this is what the forest lab does). **Direct** is a team of twelve specialists. Specialist *h* answers one question only: "given what we know today, what will month *h* look like?" No specialist ever sees another's guess.

<Callout type="empirical" title="In a direct model, every feature must be known at the origin">
The direct model for 6 months ahead is trained on rows like "growth at the origin → growth 6 months later". So its "last month" feature is really *growth six months before the target*, `lag_k(x, 6)`, not `lag_k(x, 1)`. Using the one-month lag in a 6-month model is a leak: when you stand in December, you do not know May's growth yet. The lab's `direct_table(g, h)` shifts every recent lag by `h` for exactly this reason, and keeps `g12` only because the same month last year is still known for horizons up to 12.

#### Tuning without peeking

A forest has a few settings worth tuning: `mtry` (features per split) and `nodesize` (the smallest leaf; larger leaves give smoother trees). Tune them with time-series cross-validation inside the training years: stand at the end of each December from 2017 to 2021, train on everything up to that point, forecast the next 12 months, and average the errors. The lab tunes six settings this way, and runs ETS and seasonal naive through the same five rolling years.

<Callout type="empirical" title="A leakage checklist for time-series validation">
<ul>
<li><strong>Folds go forward in time.</strong> Train on months up to an origin, score only months after it. Never shuffle.</li>
<li><strong>Features are rebuilt from the past only, inside every fold.</strong> Rolling means end at t − 1; a trend line, a scaling (mean and standard deviation) or a seasonal adjustment fitted on the full sample already "knows" the test years, so fit them on the training months of each fold.</li>
<li><strong>Respect publication lags.</strong> A driver enters with the lag at which it is actually published, not the month it describes.</li>
<li><strong>Tune on the folds, report on the test set once.</strong> If you change the model after seeing the test score, that score is no longer a test.</li>
<li><strong>Beware of numbers that are too good.</strong> An R² near 1, or a forest that beats ETS by half, is a reason to hunt for a leak before it is a reason to celebrate.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>
