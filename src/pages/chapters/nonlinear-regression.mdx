<p class="lede">Two managers, two forecasts. A bank's marketing lead has a list of clients and a budget for phone calls: <strong>who is likely to say yes to a term deposit, and how far down the list should the team call?</strong> A bike-share operations manager must put bikes and staff in place for tomorrow: <strong>how many rentals should she expect, and how sure can she be?</strong> Neither answer is a number on a smooth scale. The first is yes or no. The second is a count: 0, 1, 2, … bikes. [Chapter 4](/chapters/regression/)'s straight line handles both badly. This chapter bends it.</p>

You will work with two real, openly licensed datasets. The first comes from a Portuguese bank's telephone marketing campaigns, run between May 2008 and November 2010 and published by Moro, Cortez and Rita.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> The second is two years of rentals from Capital Bikeshare in Washington, D.C., 2011–2012, published by Fanaee-T and Gama.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> Logistic regression gets most of the chapter, because yes/no forecasts are everywhere in business: will this customer buy, churn, default, click? Poisson regression then does the same job for counts.

<KeyIdea>Keep the regression's straight line, but put it inside a curve. For a yes/no outcome the line predicts the <strong>log-odds</strong> and the logistic curve turns it into a probability; for a count the line predicts the <strong>log</strong> of the expected count and the exponential turns it into a positive number.</KeyIdea>

## Part 1. Will this client say yes?

The bank file holds **4,119 clients**, a 10% random sample that the dataset's authors drew from the full file of 41,188 contacts.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> For each client you know facts available *before* the call (age, job, the phone type, how many calls this campaign has made to the client, counting this one, the outcome of any previous campaign, and the economic climate that day, such as the 3-month <Term def="The Euro Interbank Offered Rate: the average interest rate at which large European banks lend to one another. The 3-month Euribor is a benchmark for short-term euro interest rates.">Euribor</Term> interest rate) and one outcome, `y`: did the client subscribe to a term deposit? Only **451 of 4,119 (10.9%)** did.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="No dates, so no time split">
Chapter 3 insisted that the test set must come <em>after</em> the training set. This file has no dates: UCI drew the sample at random from a file ordered by date, so the row order carries no information about time.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> The honest substitute is a <strong>stratified</strong> split: set aside 30% of the subscribers and 30% of the non-subscribers as a test set, fit on the rest, and treat the test clients as "the next campaign's call list". That answers <em>"how well will the model rank new clients from the same campaigns?"</em> It is kinder than a real next-campaign forecast, in which the economy (and the bank's script) may have changed. Keep that caveat in mind whenever you read a test score below.
</Callout>

### Why a straight line breaks

The obvious first move is Chapter 4's: code yes as 1 and no as 0 and run ordinary least squares. The fitted value is then read as the probability of a yes. This is the <Term def="An ordinary least-squares regression whose dependent variable is 0 or 1. Its fitted values are read as probabilities.">linear probability model</Term> (LPM). It is simple, and its slopes are easy to read: "one more point of Euribor lowers the chance of a yes by 5 percentage points." So what goes wrong?

The explorer shows the three problems that every econometrics text lists for the LPM:<sup>[6](#refs)</sup>

1. **Forecasts outside [0, 1].** Fitted to all 4,119 clients with the five pre-call facts, the LPM gives 47 of them a negative probability; add the month and weekday of the call and the count jumps to 507. The logit, whatever you give it, never leaves the interval.
2. **Heteroskedasticity by construction.** A 0/1 outcome with probability $p$ has variance $p(1-p)$. Clients with different $p$ have different error variances, so the usual OLS standard errors are wrong (robust ones are needed).
3. **Constant marginal effects.** A straight line says a given change in a predictor moves the probability by the same amount for every client. The gold dots say otherwise: the share of yeses creeps up among low-score clients and climbs steeply among high-score ones, just as the S-curve does.

<Callout type="math" title="The linear probability model and its variance">
For a 0/1 outcome, $E[y \mid x] = P(y = 1 \mid x)$, so OLS of $y$ on $x$ models the probability directly:

$$
P(y_i = 1 \mid x_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ki}.
$$

Because $y_i$ is Bernoulli, $\operatorname{Var}(y_i \mid x_i) = p_i(1 - p_i)$, which changes from client to client: the errors are heteroskedastic by construction. Nothing in the formula stops $\hat p_i$ from leaving $[0, 1]$, and $\partial p / \partial x_j = \beta_j$ is the same at every point.
</Callout>

### Odds, log-odds and the logistic curve

The fix is to let the straight line predict something that *can* run from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$, then translate it back into a probability. That something is the <Term def="log(p / (1 − p)), also called the logit. Probability 0.5 is log-odds 0; probabilities near 0 or 1 have large negative or positive log-odds.">log-odds</Term>. Three scales say the same thing:

- **Probability** $p$: between 0 and 1. A 20% chance.
- **<Term def="p / (1 − p): how many times more likely the event is to happen than not. Probability 0.2 is odds of 0.25, or 1 to 4.">Odds</Term>** $p/(1-p)$: between 0 and $\infty$. A 20% chance is odds of 0.25, "1 to 4". Gamblers and epidemiologists talk this way.
- **Log-odds** $\log\frac{p}{1-p}$: any number. A 20% chance is $\log 0.25 = -1.39$.

The **logistic function** $p = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$ runs the translation backward: it takes any log-odds $z$ and returns a probability between 0 and 1. That is the S-curve.

The logistic regression (or **logit**) model puts the straight line on the log-odds scale. Each coefficient therefore *adds* a fixed amount to the log-odds, which *multiplies* the odds by a fixed factor $e^{b}$, the <Term def="exp(b): the factor by which the odds p/(1 − p) are multiplied when the predictor rises by one unit, holding the others fixed.">odds ratio</Term>. What it does to the probability depends on where you start.

<Callout type="math" title="The logit model and its likelihood">
The logit model says

$$
P(y_i = 1 \mid x_i) = p_i = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ki})}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\log\frac{p_i}{1 - p_i} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ki}.
$$

Raising $x_j$ by one unit adds $\beta_j$ to the log-odds, so it multiplies the odds by the **odds ratio**

$$
\text{OR}_j = \frac{\text{odds}(x_j + 1)}{\text{odds}(x_j)} = e^{\beta_j}.
$$

The coefficients are chosen by **maximum likelihood**: make the observed yeses and noes as probable as possible.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup> The log-likelihood is

$$
\ell(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big[ y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i) \Big].
$$

It has no closed-form maximum. R's `glm()` uses iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS), a version of Newton's method; the explorers on this page run the same algorithm in your browser and match `glm()` to at least six decimals. The **probit** model replaces the logistic curve with the normal cumulative distribution, $p_i = \Phi(x_i'\beta)$; its coefficients are smaller, typically by a factor of about 1.6 to 1.8, and its probabilities are nearly identical.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

### Marginal effects are not constant

Managers ask in percentage points, not odds: *"how much does one more point of Euribor change the chance of a yes?"* For a logit the answer depends on where you stand on the S-curve. Drag the tangent.

<div class="breakout">
<MarginalEffects client:load />
</div>

For a never-contacted 40-year-old on a mobile phone, one more point of Euribor lowers the chance of a yes by about 8 percentage points when rates are near 0.7% (where her probability is 26%), but by only about 2 points near 4.9% (where it is 5.6%). Same coefficient, $b = -0.421$; very different effects.

Two summaries are common, and they can disagree. The <Term def="The average, over all clients in the sample, of each client's own marginal effect b·p(1 − p). The usual way to report a logit effect in percentage points.">average marginal effect</Term> (AME) computes each client's slope and averages them. The <Term def="The marginal effect computed once, for a hypothetical client whose every predictor is at its sample mean.">marginal effect at the mean</Term> (MEM) computes the slope for one hypothetical "average client". For Euribor the AME is **−3.41** percentage points per point and the MEM is **−2.92**; individual clients' effects range from about −10.5 to −0.1. The mean rate, 3.61%, sits in the empty gap between the two clusters of calls (only 2 of 4,119 calls were made at rates between 2% and 4%), and the "average client" is 3.6% "past success". The MEM describes a client who does not exist, which is why most applied work reports the AME.

For a yes/no predictor, compute a *discrete change* instead: switching every training client from "never contacted" to "past success" raises the predicted probability by **25.7 percentage points** on average.

<Callout type="math" title="The marginal effect p(1 − p)β, and the divide-by-4 rule">
Differentiate the logistic curve with respect to $x_j$:

$$
\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial x_{ji}} = \beta_j\, p_i (1 - p_i).
$$

The factor $p(1-p)$ is largest at $p = 0.5$, where it equals $1/4$. So $\beta_j / 4$ is the **largest** possible marginal effect, a quick upper bound known as the divide-by-4 rule.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup> The two summaries are

$$
\text{AME}_j = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \beta_j\, \hat p_i (1 - \hat p_i),
\qquad
\text{MEM}_j = \beta_j\, \bar p (1 - \bar p), \quad \bar p = \Lambda(\bar x'\hat\beta),
$$

where $\Lambda$ is the logistic function. For a 0/1 predictor, use the discrete change $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \big[\hat p_i(x_j = 1) - \hat p_i(x_j = 0)\big]$.
</Callout>

### From probability to decision: the confusion matrix

A probability is not yet a call list. To act, you pick a <Term def="The probability above which a client is classified as a 'yes' (here: gets a phone call).">threshold</Term> and call everyone above it. Each threshold produces a <Term def="A 2×2 table that counts true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives for one threshold.">confusion matrix</Term>:

| | Actually subscribed | Actually said no |
|---|---|---|
| **Predicted yes (called)** | True positive (TP): a reached buyer | False positive (FP): a wasted call |
| **Predicted no (not called)** | False negative (FN): a missed buyer | True negative (TN): a call rightly skipped |

Five metrics summarize it:

| Metric | Formula | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Accuracy** | (TP + TN) / all | What share of clients did we classify correctly? |
| **Precision** | TP / (TP + FP) | Of the clients we called, what share subscribed? |
| **Recall** (sensitivity) | TP / (TP + FN) | Of all the subscribers, what share did we reach? |
| **Specificity** | TN / (TN + FP) | Of all the non-subscribers, what share did we leave alone? |
| **F1** | $2 \cdot \frac{\text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}}$ | One number that is high only if both precision and recall are |

The 0.5 threshold wins on accuracy and precision and loses on the thing the business cares about. The profit column assumes, for illustration, that a call costs \$5 and a new deposit is worth \$50 to the bank. Then a call pays off when $p \times 50 \gt 5$, that is, when $p \gt 0.10$: a threshold set by economics, not by statistics.

<Callout type="business" title="Choose the threshold from costs, not habit">
With a cost $c$ per call and a value $V$ per success, call a client when $p \cdot V \gt c$, so the break-even threshold is $p^\ast = c / V$. The 50% default is right only if a missed buyer and a wasted call cost the same, which is almost never true. [Chapter 9](/chapters/recession-probability/) uses the same logic for recession warnings, where the costs are a false alarm and a missed recession.
</Callout>

### Plan the campaign: ROC, AUC, gains and lift

A threshold is one point. To see every possible threshold at once, rank the clients from most to least promising and walk down the list. Two curves come out of that walk:

- The <Term def="Receiver operating characteristic curve: true-positive rate (recall) against false-positive rate (1 − specificity) for every threshold.">ROC curve</Term> plots the share of buyers reached against the share of non-buyers called. The area under it, the <Term def="Area under the ROC curve: the probability that a randomly chosen subscriber gets a higher score than a randomly chosen non-subscriber. 0.5 = coin flip, 1 = perfect ranking.">AUC</Term>, is the probability that a random subscriber outranks a random non-subscriber.
- The <Term def="Cumulative share of all positives (buyers) found when you act on the top x% of the ranked list. Also called a cumulative gains or lift chart.">gain curve</Term> plots the share of buyers reached against the share of the *whole list* called. That is the chart a marketing manager reads: "call 30% of the list, reach 65% of the buyers". <Term def="Gain divided by the share called: how many times better than random calling. Lift 2 at 30% means the top 30% holds twice its share of buyers.">Lift</Term> divides that gain by the share called.

What the planner shows for the honest, before-the-call model:

- **A call budget is a threshold.** "Call the top 20%" is the same decision as "call everyone with a predicted probability of at least 17.8%".
- **Gains.** Calling the top 10% of the list reaches 33% of subscribers (lift 3.3); the top 20% reaches 53% (lift 2.6); the top 30% reaches 65% (lift 2.2).
- **Profit.** At \$5 per call and \$50 per deposit, profit peaks at **\$2,675** by calling the top 31% of the list (385 clients, cut-off probability 11.8%, close to the 10% break-even). Calling everyone earns \$615.
- **AUC 0.735**, Gini 0.470. Credit-scoring teams often report the <Term def="2 × AUC − 1: a rescaling of AUC so that 0 is random ranking and 1 is perfect. Common in credit scoring.">Gini coefficient</Term> instead of AUC; it carries the same information.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="One test set is one draw">
Our test set holds only 136 subscribers. The classification lab reruns the whole experiment on 50 random stratified splits: the middle 90% of test AUCs runs from **0.732 to 0.793** (median 0.759). Our deterministic split happens to sit near the bottom of that range. Report a range, not a single number: the same lesson as Chapter 3's time-series cross-validation.
</Callout>

<Callout type="math" title="ROC, AUC, gains and Gini in formulas">
Sort clients by $\hat p$ from highest to lowest, and let $\text{TP}(k)$ be the number of subscribers among the top $k$. With $n_1$ subscribers and $n_0$ non-subscribers:

$$
\text{TPR}(k) = \frac{\text{TP}(k)}{n_1}, \qquad \text{FPR}(k) = \frac{k - \text{TP}(k)}{n_0}, \qquad \text{Gain}(k) = \text{TPR}(k), \qquad \text{Lift}(k) = \frac{\text{TPR}(k)}{k/n}.
$$

The ROC curve plots TPR against FPR; the gain curve plots TPR against $k/n$. The AUC equals the Mann–Whitney probability that a random positive outranks a random negative:<sup>[7](#refs)</sup>

$$
\text{AUC} = P(\hat p_{\text{yes}} \gt \hat p_{\text{no}}) = \frac{\sum_{i:\,y_i=1} r_i - n_1(n_1+1)/2}{n_1\, n_0},
$$

where $r_i$ is client $i$'s rank among all scores (lowest = 1). The Gini coefficient is $2\,\text{AUC} - 1$.
</Callout>

The dataset's own documentation says it plainly: `duration` "highly affects the output target", is not known before a call is made, and should be included only for benchmark purposes and discarded for a realistic predictive model.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> The median subscriber's call lasted 458 seconds against 165 for everyone else. Switch the campaign planner to **+ call duration** and every metric improves; that improvement is fake. This is the cross-sectional version of Chapter 3's look-ahead leakage.<sup>[10](#refs)</sup>

**Before you trust any predictor, ask one question: would I know this value at the moment I make the forecast?** If not, it is leakage, however good the AUC looks.

Leakage can also be quieter. Each row records a client's *last* call, and `campaign` counts the calls to that client, the last one included. A client who says yes is not called again, while one who keeps saying no may be called many times, so a high count partly records earlier refusals. When you decide whether to dial, you do know how many calls you have made, so the column passes the test. But its odds ratio of 0.91 reflects how the bank stopped calling as well as how persistence works, so do not read it as "each extra call lowers the chance".

## Part 2. How many bikes tomorrow?

Now the second manager. Capital Bikeshare's daily file has **731 days** (January 1, 2011 to December 31, 2012); the hourly file has one row for each hour with data, 17,379 in all. `cnt` counts every rental, casual and registered.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> Counts have three features that a bell curve lacks: they are whole numbers, they cannot be negative, and their spread grows with their level. A quiet hour at 4 a.m. varies by a few bikes; a rush hour varies by hundreds.

### Counts are different

The <Term def="A distribution for counts of events in a fixed interval: P(Y = k) = e^(−λ) λ^k / k!. Its mean and variance both equal λ.">Poisson distribution</Term> is the starting point for counts. It has a single parameter, λ, which is both its **mean** and its **variance**. That second fact is a strong assumption, and you can check it against real data.

<div class="breakout">
<CountsLab client:load />
</div>

At 4 a.m. on working days the Poisson curve fits well: the average is 5.4 bikes an hour and the variance is only 1.8 times the mean. At 8 a.m. the variance is 73 times the mean, and at 5 p.m. almost 100 times. No single λ can describe rush hours: some are rainy, some are sunny, some fall in a busy tourist week, and the system grew between 2011 and 2012. When the variance exceeds the mean like this, the counts are <Term def="Variance larger than a Poisson model allows (variance > mean). Very common in business counts; it makes Poisson standard errors and intervals too narrow.">overdispersed</Term>.

<Callout type="math" title="The Poisson distribution">
A count $Y$ follows a Poisson distribution with rate $\lambda \gt 0$ when

$$
P(Y = k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{k}}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots
$$

and then $E[Y] = \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \lambda$. It arises as the number of independent, rare events in a fixed window, such as the number of bikes taken from a quiet station in one hour.
</Callout>

### Poisson regression: a log link and multiplicative effects

Poisson regression lets λ depend on predictors. It models the **log** of the expected count as a straight line, so the expected count $\mu = e^{\text{line}}$ is always positive, and each coefficient *multiplies* expected rentals by $e^{b}$, the <Term def="exp(b) in a count model: the factor by which the expected count is multiplied when the predictor rises by one unit. Also called a rate ratio.">incidence-rate ratio</Term> (IRR). We fit January 2011 to August 2012 and keep September to December 2012 for the forecast test.

Read the IRRs like percentages:

| Predictor | IRR (Poisson) | Meaning, other things equal |
|---|---|---|
| Year 2012 (vs 2011) | 1.556 | 2012 days ran about 56% above 2011 days: the system was growing |
| Rain or snow (vs clear) | 0.513 | Expected rentals roughly halve |
| Mist (vs clear) | 0.908 | About 9% fewer rentals |
| Fall (vs winter) | 1.391 | About 39% more rentals than a winter day at the same temperature and weather |
| Holiday | 0.918 | About 8% fewer |
| Working day | 1.013 | Barely different: commuters on weekdays, leisure riders at weekends |

<Callout type="math" title="Poisson regression and its log-likelihood">
The model is

$$
Y_i \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu_i), \qquad \log \mu_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ki},
$$

so $\mu_i = e^{x_i'\beta}$ and raising $x_j$ by one unit multiplies $\mu_i$ by $\text{IRR}_j = e^{\beta_j}$. The coefficients maximize

$$
\ell(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big[ y_i \log \mu_i - \mu_i - \log(y_i!) \Big],
$$

again by IRLS in `glm()`. When counts are observed over different exposures $t_i$ (hours open, stores, population), model the rate instead: $\log \mu_i = \log t_i + x_i'\beta$, written `offset(log(t))` in an R formula.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> The last step of the lab shows why that matters here: on October 29, 2012 only one hour of data was recorded.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

### Overdispersion: check it, then fix it

The mean of daily rentals in the training period is 4,267; the variance is 3.40 million, about 800 times larger. Predictors explain much of that, but not nearly all. The **dispersion statistic** asks how much is left:

<Predict client:load
  question="After fitting the Poisson regression, the dispersion statistic is about **100**. Roughly how much too small are the Poisson standard errors?"
  options={['About 100 times', 'About 10 times', 'About 2 times', 'They are fine; dispersion only affects the coefficients']}
  answer={1}
  explain="Standard errors scale with the **square root** of the dispersion: √100 = **10**. The lab finds a dispersion of 99.7, and the quasi-Poisson standard errors are 9.99 times the Poisson ones. The coefficients themselves are unchanged; only their precision (and any interval built on it) was overstated."
/>

Two standard fixes, both in the lab:

- **Quasi-Poisson** keeps the Poisson coefficients and multiplies every standard error by $\sqrt{\phi}$, about 10 here. It changes the inference, not the forecasts.
- **Negative binomial** (`MASS::glm.nb`) adds a parameter θ so that the variance grows faster than the mean: $\operatorname{Var} = \mu + \mu^2/\theta$. Here θ = 27.2. Its IRRs stay close to Poisson's (2012: 1.62; rain or snow: 0.52), and its AIC falls from 69,121 to 9,793: an enormous improvement in fit.

At 4 a.m. on working days, by contrast, the same check gives a dispersion of about 1.7. Small counts with little hidden variation are where the plain Poisson model is at home.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup>

<Callout type="math" title="Dispersion statistic, quasi-Poisson and the negative binomial">
With fitted means $\hat\mu_i$, $n$ observations and $k + 1$ coefficients, the Pearson dispersion statistic is

$$
\hat\phi = \frac{1}{n - k - 1}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{(y_i - \hat\mu_i)^2}{\hat\mu_i}.
$$

Under a correct Poisson model $\hat\phi \approx 1$. **Quasi-Poisson** assumes $\operatorname{Var}(Y_i) = \phi\,\mu_i$ and inflates standard errors by $\sqrt{\hat\phi}$. The **negative binomial** (NB2) model assumes

$$
\operatorname{Var}(Y_i) = \mu_i + \frac{\mu_i^2}{\theta},
$$

which arises if each day's Poisson rate is itself multiplied by a gamma-distributed "busyness" shock. As $\theta \to \infty$ it collapses to Poisson. Which variance shape fits better is an empirical question.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup><sup>[11](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="These intervals are still too kind">
The test gave each model the <em>actual</em> weather of each test day, as if the weather forecast were perfect: an ex-post forecast, in Chapter 4's language. Real weather forecasts miss, and the intervals also ignore uncertainty in the estimated coefficients. A real deployment would be a little less accurate and should have somewhat wider intervals. [Chapter 11](/chapters/uncertainty/) returns to coverage audits and to methods that calibrate intervals from past errors.
</Callout>

<KeyIdea>Before you trust a count model's intervals, compare the variance with the mean. If the dispersion statistic is far above 1, Poisson intervals will be far too narrow; use a negative binomial (or quasi-Poisson) model and audit the coverage out of sample.</KeyIdea>

## Chapter checklist

- A straight line through 0/1 data (the **LPM**) can forecast probabilities below 0 or above 1, has heteroskedastic errors by construction and forces constant effects. It still ranks reasonably well.
- The **logit** puts the line on the **log-odds** scale: `glm(y ~ x, family = binomial)`. Read $e^{b}$ as an **odds ratio**, and report effects in percentage points as **average marginal effects**, $b\,p(1-p)$ averaged over clients. The **probit** gives nearly the same probabilities.
- A threshold turns probabilities into actions. Report **precision, recall, specificity and F1**, not just accuracy, which rewards ignoring a rare outcome (the **accuracy paradox**). Set the threshold from costs: call when $p \gt c/V$.
- **ROC/AUC** measures ranking; **gain and lift** charts translate ranking into "call x% of the list, reach y% of buyers". Score on held-out data and report a range across splits.
- Ask of every predictor: **would I know it at forecast time?** Call duration fails, and its AUC of 0.915 is leakage.
- For counts, use **Poisson regression** with a log link and read $e^{b}$ as an **incidence-rate ratio**. Check the **dispersion statistic**; if it is far above 1, use **quasi-Poisson** for inference or a **negative binomial** for forecasts, and **audit interval coverage** out of sample.
