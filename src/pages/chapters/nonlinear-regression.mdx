<p class="lede">Two managers, two yes-or-no forecasts. A bank's marketing lead has a list of clients and a budget for phone calls: <strong>who is likely to say yes to a term deposit, and how far down the list should the team call?</strong> A manufacturer's head of planning is setting next year's budget: <strong>will the economy be in recession a year from now?</strong> Neither answer is a number on a smooth scale. Each is yes or no, and the useful forecast is a probability. [Chapter 4](/chapters/regression/)'s straight line handles that badly. This chapter bends it.</p>

You will work with two kinds of real data. Part 1 uses a Portuguese bank's telephone marketing campaigns, run between May 2008 and November 2010 and published by Moro, Cortez and Rita:<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> thousands of clients, one row each, no time order. Part 2 uses 66 years of monthly U.S. data from FRED: Treasury yields and the official recession dates. One tool, logistic regression, answers both questions. Yes/no forecasts are everywhere in business: will this customer buy, churn, default, click? Will the economy turn down?

<KeyIdea>Keep the regression's straight line, but put it inside a curve. The line predicts the <strong>log-odds</strong>, and the logistic S-curve turns them into a probability that always stays between 0 and 1. Then judge the probabilities two ways: do they <strong>rank</strong> cases well (AUC), and do they <strong>mean what they say</strong> (calibration), on data the model has never seen?</KeyIdea>

## Part 1. Will this client say yes?

The bank file holds **4,119 clients**, a 10% random sample that the dataset's authors drew from the full file of 41,188 contacts.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> For each client you know facts available *before* the call (age, job, the phone type, how many calls this campaign has made to the client, counting this one, the outcome of any previous campaign, and the economic climate that day, such as the 3-month <Term def="The Euro Interbank Offered Rate: the average interest rate at which large European banks lend to one another. The 3-month Euribor is a benchmark for short-term euro interest rates.">Euribor</Term> interest rate) and one outcome, `y`: did the client subscribe to a term deposit? Only **451 of 4,119 (10.9%)** did.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="No dates, so no time split">
Chapter 3 insisted that the test set must come <em>after</em> the training set. This file has no dates: UCI drew the sample at random from a file ordered by date, so the row order carries no information about time.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> The honest substitute is a <strong>stratified</strong> split: set aside 30% of the subscribers and 30% of the non-subscribers as a test set, fit on the rest, and treat the test clients as "the next campaign's call list". That answers <em>"how well will the model rank new clients from the same campaigns?"</em> It is kinder than a real next-campaign forecast, in which the economy (and the bank's script) may have changed. Keep that caveat in mind whenever you read a test score below.
</Callout>

### Why a straight line breaks

The obvious first move is Chapter 4's: code yes as 1 and no as 0 and run ordinary least squares. The fitted value is then read as the probability of a yes. This is the <Term def="An ordinary least-squares regression whose dependent variable is 0 or 1. Its fitted values are read as probabilities.">linear probability model</Term> (LPM). It is simple, and its slopes are easy to read: "one more point of Euribor lowers the chance of a yes by 5 percentage points." So what goes wrong?

The explorer shows the three problems that every econometrics text lists for the LPM:<sup>[4](#refs)</sup>

1. **Forecasts outside [0, 1].** Fitted to all 4,119 clients with the five pre-call facts, the LPM gives 47 of them a negative probability; add the month and weekday of the call and the count jumps to 507. The logit, whatever you give it, never leaves the interval.
2. **Heteroskedasticity by construction.** A 0/1 outcome with probability $p$ has variance $p(1-p)$. Clients with different $p$ have different error variances, so the usual OLS standard errors are wrong (robust ones are needed).
3. **Constant marginal effects.** A straight line says a given change in a predictor moves the probability by the same amount for every client. The gold dots say otherwise: the share of yeses creeps up among low-score clients and climbs steeply among high-score ones, just as the S-curve does.

The lab ends with a surprise worth remembering. On the 1,237 test clients, the three models *rank* clients almost identically: the share of (subscriber, non-subscriber) pairs put in the right order is 0.733 for the LPM, 0.735 for the logit and 0.735 for the probit. The LPM's flaw is not whom it puts first. It is that its outputs are not usable as probabilities, and probabilities are what a business plan multiplies by money.

<Callout type="math" title="The linear probability model and its variance">
For a 0/1 outcome, $E[y \mid x] = P(y = 1 \mid x)$, so OLS of $y$ on $x$ models the probability directly:

$$
P(y_i = 1 \mid x_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ki}.
$$

Because $y_i$ is Bernoulli, $\operatorname{Var}(y_i \mid x_i) = p_i(1 - p_i)$, which changes from client to client: the errors are heteroskedastic by construction. Nothing in the formula stops $\hat p_i$ from leaving $[0, 1]$, and $\partial p / \partial x_j = \beta_j$ is the same at every point.
</Callout>

### Odds, log-odds and the logistic curve

The fix is to let the straight line predict something that *can* run from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$, then translate it back into a probability. That something is the <Term def="log(p / (1 − p)), also called the logit. Probability 0.5 is log-odds 0; probabilities near 0 or 1 have large negative or positive log-odds.">log-odds</Term>. Three scales say the same thing:

- **Probability** $p$: between 0 and 1. A 20% chance.
- **<Term def="p / (1 − p): how many times more likely the event is to happen than not. Probability 0.2 is odds of 0.25, or 1 to 4.">Odds</Term>** $p/(1-p)$: between 0 and $\infty$. A 20% chance is odds of 0.25, "1 to 4". Gamblers and epidemiologists talk this way.
- **Log-odds** $\log\frac{p}{1-p}$: any number. A 20% chance is $\log 0.25 = -1.39$.

The **logistic function** $p = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$ runs the translation backward: it takes any log-odds $z$ and returns a probability between 0 and 1. That is the S-curve.

The logistic regression (or **logit**) model puts the straight line on the log-odds scale. Each coefficient therefore *adds* a fixed amount to the log-odds, which *multiplies* the odds by a fixed factor $e^{b}$, the <Term def="exp(b): the factor by which the odds p/(1 − p) are multiplied when the predictor rises by one unit, holding the others fixed.">odds ratio</Term>. What it does to the probability depends on where you start.

<Callout type="math" title="The logit model and its likelihood">
The logit model says

$$
P(y_i = 1 \mid x_i) = p_i = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ki})}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\log\frac{p_i}{1 - p_i} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1i} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ki}.
$$

Raising $x_j$ by one unit adds $\beta_j$ to the log-odds, so it multiplies the odds by the **odds ratio**

$$
\text{OR}_j = \frac{\text{odds}(x_j + 1)}{\text{odds}(x_j)} = e^{\beta_j}.
$$

The coefficients are chosen by **maximum likelihood**: make the observed yeses and noes as probable as possible.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> The log-likelihood is

$$
\ell(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \Big[ y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - p_i) \Big].
$$

It has no closed-form maximum. R's `glm()` uses iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS), a version of Newton's method; the explorers on this page run the same algorithm in your browser and match `glm()` to at least six decimals. The **probit** model replaces the logistic curve with the normal cumulative distribution, $p_i = \Phi(x_i'\beta)$; its coefficients are smaller, typically by a factor of about 1.6 to 1.8, and its probabilities are nearly identical.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

The Euribor odds ratio does not mean the bank can raise subscriptions by lowering interest rates. <Callout type="empirical" title="Say an odds ratio in words, with its unit">
A memo line has four parts: **the change, its unit, what happens to the odds, and "holding the other facts fixed"**. Four habits make it right:

- **Turn the ratio into a percent change in the odds**:<sup>[21](#refs)</sup> $100 \times (\text{OR} - 1)$. Euribor: $100 \times (0.66 - 1) = -34\%$, so "each percentage point higher Euribor goes with 34% lower odds of a yes". Not "34 percentage points lower probability": the odds, not the probability, move by a fixed percentage.
- **Rescale to a step a manager cares about.** Raise the odds ratio to the power of the step. A quarter point of Euribor (25 basis points, a typical central-bank move): $0.66^{0.25} = 0.90$, about 10% lower odds. A decade of age: $1.020^{10} = 1.21$. This is Chapter 4's [rescaling](/chapters/regression/#rescaling-units-what-changes-and-what-doesnt) idea, on the odds scale.
- **For small coefficients, $b$ itself is close to the percent change.** The calls coefficient is $-0.091$; the exact change is $-8.7\%$ per call. The shortcut fails for big ones: the past-success coefficient is 1.86, but the odds rise by 541%.
- **Stay where the data are.** The calls happened near 1% and near 5% Euribor, with only 2 of 4,119 in between. Any statement about "a client at 3%" reads the curve across an empty gap.
</Callout>

### Marginal effects are not constant

Managers ask in percentage points, not odds: *"how much does one more point of Euribor change the chance of a yes?"* For a logit the answer depends on where you stand on the S-curve. Drag the tangent.

<div class="breakout">
<MarginalEffects client:load />
</div>

For a never-contacted 40-year-old on a mobile phone, one more point of Euribor lowers the chance of a yes by about 8 percentage points when rates are near 0.7% (where her probability is 26%), but by only about 2 points near 4.9% (where it is 5.6%). Same coefficient, $b = -0.421$; very different effects.

Two summaries are common, and they can disagree. The <Term def="The average, over all clients in the sample, of each client's own marginal effect b·p(1 − p). The usual way to report a logit effect in percentage points.">average marginal effect</Term> (AME) computes each client's slope and averages them. The <Term def="The marginal effect computed once, for a hypothetical client whose every predictor is at its sample mean.">marginal effect at the mean</Term> (MEM) computes the slope for one hypothetical "average client". For Euribor the AME is **−3.41** percentage points per point and the MEM is **−2.92**; individual clients' effects range from about −10.5 to −0.1. The mean rate, 3.61%, sits in the empty gap between the two clusters of calls (only 2 of 4,119 calls were made at rates between 2% and 4%), and the "average client" is 3.6% "past success". The MEM describes a client who does not exist, which is why most applied work reports the AME.

For a yes/no predictor, compute a *discrete change* instead: switching every training client from "never contacted" to "past success" raises the predicted probability by **25.7 percentage points** on average.

<Callout type="math" title="The marginal effect p(1 − p)β, and the divide-by-4 rule">
Differentiate the logistic curve with respect to $x_j$:

$$
\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial x_{ji}} = \beta_j\, p_i (1 - p_i).
$$

The factor $p(1-p)$ is largest at $p = 0.5$, where it equals $1/4$. So $\beta_j / 4$ is the **largest** possible marginal effect, a quick upper bound known as the divide-by-4 rule.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> The two summaries are

$$
\text{AME}_j = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \beta_j\, \hat p_i (1 - \hat p_i),
\qquad
\text{MEM}_j = \beta_j\, \bar p (1 - \bar p), \quad \bar p = \Lambda(\bar x'\hat\beta),
$$

where $\Lambda$ is the logistic function. For a 0/1 predictor, use the discrete change $\frac{1}{n}\sum_i \big[\hat p_i(x_j = 1) - \hat p_i(x_j = 0)\big]$.
</Callout>

### What the model says about one client

Odds ratios and marginal effects describe the model. A manager asks about a person: *"Should we call this client?"* To answer, build the client's probability one fact at a time. Start from a reference client the model gives 5.6% (40 years old, mobile phone, first call, never contacted, Euribor 4.9%: the client from the odds Predict above). Each fact where your client differs adds its coefficient times the difference to the log-odds. Add them up, then translate the total back into a probability.

Try **Best prospect**: a 77-year-old reached on a mobile phone while Euribor was 0.9%, whose previous campaign had succeeded. Age adds 0.72 to the log-odds, the low rate 1.68 and the past success 1.86, and 5.6% becomes **80.9%**. The bars for the low rate and the past success are about the same length, so they multiply the odds by about the same factor (5.4 and 6.4). On the probability scale they would look very different depending on which came first. That is why the model adds on the log-odds scale and you translate only at the end.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Translate a probability before you hand it over">
People read "out of 100" faster and more correctly than percentages.<sup>[20](#refs)</sup> So write a client-level answer in three lines:

1. **The probability, as a count:** for the reference client, "5.6%: about 6 in 100 clients like her say yes."
2. **Against the base rate:** 10.9% of all clients subscribed, so she is about half as likely as a typical client. A probability means little until you know what "normal" is.
3. **In money:** at \$5 a call and \$50 a deposit, a call is worth $0.056 \times 50 - 5 = -\$2.18$ on average. Skip her, unless something the model does not see changes the picture.

And never treat one outcome as a verdict. If she does say yes, the 5.6% was not "wrong": among 100 clients like her, about 6 should.
</Callout>

### From probability to decision: the confusion matrix

A probability is not yet a call list. To act, you pick a <Term def="The probability above which a client is classified as a 'yes' (here: gets a phone call).">threshold</Term> and call everyone above it. Each threshold produces a <Term def="A 2×2 table that counts true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives for one threshold.">confusion matrix</Term>:

| | Actually subscribed | Actually said no |
|---|---|---|
| **Predicted yes (called)** | True positive (TP): a reached buyer | False positive (FP): a wasted call |
| **Predicted no (not called)** | False negative (FN): a missed buyer | True negative (TN): a call rightly skipped |

Five metrics summarize it:

| Metric | Formula | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Accuracy** | (TP + TN) / all | What share of clients did we classify correctly? |
| **Precision** | TP / (TP + FP) | Of the clients we called, what share subscribed? |
| **Recall** (sensitivity) | TP / (TP + FN) | Of all the subscribers, what share did we reach? |
| **Specificity** | TN / (TN + FP) | Of all the non-subscribers, what share did we leave alone? |
| **F1** | $2 \cdot \frac{\text{precision} \cdot \text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}}$ | One number that is high only if both precision and recall are |

### Plan the campaign: ROC, AUC, gains and lift

A threshold is one point. To see every possible threshold at once, rank the clients from most to least promising and walk down the list. Two curves come out of that walk:

- The <Term def="Receiver operating characteristic curve: true-positive rate (recall) against false-positive rate (1 − specificity) for every threshold.">ROC curve</Term> plots the share of buyers reached against the share of non-buyers called. The area under it, the <Term def="Area under the ROC curve: the probability that a randomly chosen subscriber gets a higher score than a randomly chosen non-subscriber. 0.5 = coin flip, 1 = perfect ranking.">AUC</Term>, is the probability that a random subscriber outranks a random non-subscriber.
- The <Term def="Cumulative share of all positives (buyers) found when you act on the top x% of the ranked list. Also called a cumulative gains or lift chart.">gain curve</Term> plots the share of buyers reached against the share of the *whole list* called. That is the chart a marketing manager reads: "call 30% of the list, reach 65% of the buyers". <Term def="Gain divided by the share called: how many times better than random calling. Lift 2 at 30% means the top 30% holds twice its share of buyers.">Lift</Term> divides that gain by the share called.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="One test set is one draw">

Our deterministic split happens to sit near the bottom of that range. Report a range, not a single number: the same lesson as Chapter 3's time-series cross-validation.
</Callout>

<Callout type="math" title="ROC, AUC, gains and Gini in formulas">
Sort clients by $\hat p$ from highest to lowest, and let $\text{TP}(k)$ be the number of subscribers among the top $k$. With $n_1$ subscribers and $n_0$ non-subscribers:

$$
\text{TPR}(k) = \frac{\text{TP}(k)}{n_1}, \qquad \text{FPR}(k) = \frac{k - \text{TP}(k)}{n_0}, \qquad \text{Gain}(k) = \text{TPR}(k), \qquad \text{Lift}(k) = \frac{\text{TPR}(k)}{k/n}.
$$

The ROC curve plots TPR against FPR; the gain curve plots TPR against $k/n$. The AUC equals the Mann–Whitney probability that a random positive outranks a random negative:<sup>[5](#refs)</sup>

$$
\text{AUC} = P(\hat p_{\text{yes}} \gt \hat p_{\text{no}}) = \frac{\sum_{i:\,y_i=1} r_i - n_1(n_1+1)/2}{n_1\, n_0},
$$

where $r_i$ is client $i$'s rank among all scores (lowest = 1). The Gini coefficient is $2\,\text{AUC} - 1$.
</Callout>

## Part 2. Will there be a recession next year?

Now scale the question up from one client to the whole economy. You run planning at a manufacturer, and next year's budget depends on one question: **will there be a recession?** Nobody can answer yes or no with confidence. But you can attach a *probability* to it, check how good past probabilities were, and decide in advance how high that probability must be before you cut hiring.

In May 2023 the gap between the 10-year Treasury yield and the 3-month bill rate fell to **−1.57 percentage points**, its lowest monthly reading since 1981. That gap, the *yield-curve spread*, had turned negative ahead of every U.S. recession since 1969 except one (before 1990 it only flattened to about +0.1). Many forecasters expected a downturn. Yet through the latest data in this chapter (October 2026), the NBER has not declared one. In this part you build the famous model behind those warnings, look under its hood, learn to score probability forecasts properly, and find out exactly how much that two-year false alarm cost it.

The tool is Part 1's logit, but four things change:

- **The rows are months in time order.** The test set must come after the training set ([Chapter 3](/chapters/benchmarks/)), and neighboring months are not independent draws.
- **The event is rare and a year away.** Only about 12% of months since 1959 were followed by a recession 12 months later.
- **The answer arrives late.** The official referee dates recessions many months after the fact.
- **The probability itself is the message.** Part 1 used probabilities mainly to *rank* clients. A board reads "30% chance of recession" as a number, so it had better mean what it says.

That last point has a name. A model that says "30% chance" is not wrong when the event fails to happen; it would be wrong if events it called 30% happened 90% of the time. A forecast whose probabilities match the frequencies is <Term def="A probability forecast is calibrated when events it gives probability p happen about a fraction p of the time.">calibrated</Term>, and calibration will matter a lot by the end of the chapter.

Our event is a U.S. recession. The official referee is the <Term def="The National Bureau of Economic Research's Business Cycle Dating Committee. It dates U.S. business-cycle peaks and troughs after the fact.">NBER</Term> dating committee, which defines a recession as a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup> FRED turns its dates into a monthly indicator, `USREC`: 1 in every recession month, 0 otherwise.

Our predictor is the <Term def="The 10-year Treasury yield minus the 3-month Treasury bill rate, in percentage points. Positive = normal upward-sloping yield curve; negative = inverted curve.">term spread</Term>: the 10-year Treasury yield (`GS10`) minus the 3-month bill rate (`TB3MS`), both monthly averages in percent. When short rates rise above long rates, the yield curve is <Term def="A yield curve on which short-term interest rates are higher than long-term ones, so the term spread is negative.">inverted</Term>.

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why would bond yields predict recessions?">
Two stories fit together. First, a long-term yield roughly reflects the short rates that investors expect over the coming years. If markets expect the Fed to cut rates, usually because they expect the economy to weaken, long yields fall below short ones. Second, an inverted curve often means monetary policy is tight right now: the Fed has pushed short rates up to fight inflation, and tight money slows the economy with a lag of a year or so.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup> Neither story is a law of nature, which is why we test the relationship rather than assume it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="The spread is in percentage points, and one point is a big move">
Both yields are interest rates in percent per year, so their difference is measured in **percentage points** (pp). Bond traders count in **basis points**: 1 point = 100 basis points (bp). Three habits:

- **Never quote a percent change of a spread.** It crosses zero, so "the spread fell 150%" means nothing. Say "the spread fell from +1.2 to −0.3 points" or "by 150 basis points".
- **Know the typical size.** Since 1959 the monthly spread has ranged from −2.65 to +4.42 points, with a standard deviation of 1.24. The average month-to-month move is only 0.21 points, but over a year the spread changes by more than a full point about 37% of the time. So "one more point" in the coefficient table is a big but ordinary one-year swing; for a single month, think in quarter points.
- **Check the measure.** The New York Fed uses a bond-equivalent bill rate, FRED's `TB3MS` is on a discount basis, and many news stories quote the 10-year minus 2-year spread instead. They differ by tenths of a point, which matters near zero. Write down which spread you use.
</Callout>

### Build the target: a recession 12 months from now

The question is about the future, so the target has to look forward. We follow the New York Fed: for each month *t*, the target is **whether month *t* + 12 is a recession month**.<sup>[10](#refs)</sup> In code, that means shifting `USREC` back by 12 months, so that each row pairs *this* month's spread with the economy's state *a year later*. The last 12 rows have no answer yet, and those are exactly the months we want to forecast.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The referee announces the score late">
The NBER dates turning points only once it is confident, often many months later. It announced the December 2007 peak on December 1, 2008, and the July 1990 peak in April 1991. The fastest call in recent history, the February 2020 peak, came on June 8, 2020.<sup>[11](#refs)</sup> Two consequences for you:
<ul>
<li>The most recent zeros in `USREC` are <strong>provisional</strong>. If the NBER later declares a recession that started in 2026, the last few targets in this part flip from 0 to 1.</li>
<li>A real-time forecaster never knows the latest outcomes. When you backtest, use only outcomes that would have been known, or accept that your test is a little too kind.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

Why exactly a year? Three reasons. A budget, a hiring plan or an inventory order is set about a year ahead, so that is the horizon a planner needs. The spread's warning tends to arrive roughly a year early, as the radar below will show. And it is the New York Fed's convention, so your numbers can be compared with its published ones. (The exercise at the end tries the looser target "any recession within 12 months", and it scores worse.)

### The same S-curve, fitted to 66 years of months

As in Part 1, the [logit](#odds-log-odds-and-the-logistic-curve) passes a straight-line index, here $a + b \cdot \text{spread}$, through an S-shaped function that keeps every probability between 0 and 1, and it is fitted by maximum likelihood: the best curve gives high probabilities to months that were followed by recession and low probabilities to months that were not.

Three things to notice:

- **The curve slopes down.** A steeper yield curve means a lower chance of recession a year later. Make the curve flat (steepness 0) and it gives every month the same 50% chance, which the log-likelihood punishes heavily: even a flat line at the 12% base rate scores better.
- **The fitted curve crosses 50% at a spread of about −1.1 points.** Even a mildly inverted curve gives "only" 20 to 40%. Recessions are rare (about 12% of months since 1959 were followed by one 12 months later), so the model needs strong evidence before it calls one likely.
- **Most dots overlap.** Plenty of months with a negative spread were *not* followed by recession 12 months later. The spread is informative, not decisive.

<Callout type="math" title="The recession model in Part 1's notation">
The model is Part 1's logit with one predictor and a target dated a year ahead:

$$
P(y_{t+12} = 1 \mid s_t) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(a + b\,s_t)}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\log\frac{p}{1-p} = a + b\,s_t ,
$$

so $b$ is the change in the **log-odds** for a one-point change in today's spread $s_t$. The probit uses $p = \Phi(a + b\,s_t)$ instead. Both are fitted by maximum likelihood with `glm()`'s IRLS algorithm, which the explorer above also runs; the [next section](#under-the-hood-how-glm-finds-the-curve-and-how-sure-it-can-be) opens it up. One difference from Part 1 matters: the rows are months in time order, so neighboring months share most of their information, and the usual `glm()` standard errors are too small. That section measures by how much.
</Callout>

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why logit and probit agree: a hidden 'recession pressure'">
Picture an unobserved index of recession pressure, $y^\ast_t = a + b\,s_t + \varepsilon_t$, and suppose a recession follows whenever $y^\ast_t \gt 0$. Then $P(y = 1) = P(\varepsilon_t \gt -(a + b\,s_t))$. If the shock $\varepsilon$ has a logistic distribution you get the logit; if it is standard normal you get the probit. ### Under the hood: how `glm()` finds the curve, and how sure it can be

<Callout type="tip" title="Optional depth">
In class we take only the three bold answers below (about 5 minutes). The lab and the math box are for students who want to see the machinery; nothing later in the course depends on them.
</Callout>

This section is about trust: how the computer picks the curve, whether the spread really helps, and how precise the slope is. The three answers are in bold below; the formulas stay in the "Show the math" box.

Part 1 said the logit is fitted by *maximum likelihood*. Every candidate curve gives each month a probability. A month that was followed by a recession scores the log of the probability the curve gave it; a calm month scores the log of one minus that probability. Logs of numbers below 1 are negative, so every score is negative, and the best curve is the one whose total is closest to zero:

| Candidate curve, 802 months with a known outcome | Log-likelihood |
|---|---|
| A coin flip: 50% for every month | −555.9 |
| The base rate: 11.8% for every month | −291.8 |
| The fitted logit: log-odds = −1.134 − 1.014 × spread | **−238.6** |

The coin flip scores worst because nine months in ten were calm and it gives each of them only a 50% chance. The base rate fixes that; the spread improves on it again. The lab below climbs to the top by hand, tests whether the climb was worth it, and asks how precisely the data pin down the slope.

What the lab shows:

- **Fitting is a climb.** Starting from a coin flip, IRLS reaches `glm()`'s coefficients to four decimals in five steps. Each step is a weighted least-squares regression in which months with probabilities near 50% get the most weight, because their outcomes are the most uncertain.
- **The spread clearly helps.** The <Term def="Twice the gain in log-likelihood from adding predictors. If the added predictors are useless, it follows a chi-squared distribution with as many degrees of freedom as predictors added.">likelihood-ratio statistic</Term> is twice the gain in log-likelihood from adding the spread: from −291.8 to −238.6, so about 106. For one added predictor, anything above 3.84 counts as significant at the 5% level, so 106 is far beyond chance. One caution: like `glm()`'s standard errors, the test treats the months as independent, so it overstates the certainty. The honest check below still finds the spread clearly significant.
- **Fit is modest, and that is normal.** <Term def="1 − (log-likelihood of the model ÷ log-likelihood of the base-rate-only model). A common goodness-of-fit summary for logit and probit models; it is not comparable with the R² of a linear regression.">McFadden's pseudo-R²</Term> is 0.18. It is not Chapter 4's R²: for yes/no outcomes, values of 0.2 to 0.4 already describe a good fit.<sup>[19](#refs)</sup> One predictor cannot see a pandemic coming.

**The slope is less precise than `glm()` says.** Why are the plain standard errors too small? Look at how the target is built. The target for January 2007 asks about January 2008; the target for February 2007 asks about February 2008. A recession that lasts 18 months turns 18 consecutive targets into 1s, while the spread barely moves from one month to the next. So the leftover errors $y_t - \hat p_t$ come in long runs: their correlation between neighboring months is **0.86**. Eight hundred such months carry far less independent information than 800 coin tosses. `glm()` assumes independent rows; the <Term def="Heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent standard errors (Newey–West): they allow errors to be correlated over time, so z- and t-statistics are not overstated.">Newey–West (HAC)</Term> estimator, which you met in Chapter 4, keeps the same coefficients and widens the standard errors to match.<sup>[18](#refs)</sup> With 24 lags the slope's standard error rises from 0.114 to 0.247; with 12 or 36 lags it is 0.237 or 0.256, so the answer does not hinge on the exact choice. [Chapter 8](/chapters/dynamic-regression/) will meet the same problem in regressions of job growth on jobless claims.

<Callout type="math" title="IRLS, the likelihood-ratio test and the Newey–West sandwich">
With the design matrix $X$ (a column of ones and the spread), the logit log-likelihood $\ell(\beta) = \sum_t \big[y_t \log p_t + (1 - y_t)\log(1 - p_t)\big]$ has gradient $X'(y - p)$ and second derivative $-X'WX$, where $W = \operatorname{diag}\big(p_t(1 - p_t)\big)$. Newton's method therefore updates

$$
\beta^{(m+1)} = \beta^{(m)} + (X'WX)^{-1} X'(y - p),
$$

with $p$ and $W$ evaluated at $\beta^{(m)}$. Each step is a weighted least-squares regression, hence the name iteratively reweighted least squares. At the maximum, $(X'WX)^{-1}$ estimates the variance of $\hat\beta$: those are `glm()`'s standard errors.

To test whether $q$ added predictors help, compare the log-likelihoods of the bigger model ($\ell_1$) and the smaller one ($\ell_0$): $\text{LR} = 2(\ell_1 - \ell_0)$, approximately $\chi^2_q$ if they do not (with independent rows). Here $\text{LR} = 2 \times (-238.65 + 291.79) = 106.3$ with $q = 1$: a p-value of about $6 \times 10^{-25}$, if the months were independent. McFadden's pseudo-$R^2$ is $1 - \ell_1/\ell_0$.

When rows are correlated over time, the Newey–West estimator replaces $(X'WX)^{-1}$ by a "sandwich":

$$
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta) = B^{-1}\, \hat S\, B^{-1}, \qquad B = X'WX, \qquad \hat S = \sum_{l=-L}^{L} \Big(1 - \frac{|l|}{L+1}\Big) \sum_{t} g_t\, g_{t-l}',
$$

where $g_t = x_t (y_t - \hat p_t)$ is month $t$'s contribution to the gradient. With $L = 0$ it reduces to the robust (heteroskedasticity-only) version. For an $h$-step-ahead target, choose $L$ of at least $h - 1$ = 11; the lab uses 24 to allow for slow-moving errors. In R, `sandwich::NeweyWest()` computes it for a fitted model; in Python, `fit(cov_type="HAC", cov_kwds={"maxlags": 24})`.
</Callout>

### The yield-curve recession model

This model has a pedigree. Arturo Estrella and Gikas Hardouvelis showed in 1991 that the slope of the yield curve predicts U.S. output growth and NBER recessions.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup> Estrella and Frederic Mishkin then compared many financial indicators and found that beyond one quarter ahead, the yield-curve slope was the best single predictor of recessions, and out of sample it usually did better alone than combined with other variables.<sup>[12](#refs)</sup>

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York publishes a monthly recession probability based on this work.<sup>[10](#refs)</sup> Its recipe:

- **Predictor:** the 10-year Treasury yield minus the 3-month Treasury bill rate, monthly averages. The New York Fed converts the bill rate to a *bond-equivalent* basis; FRED's `TB3MS` is on a discount basis, so our spread differs slightly.<sup>[13](#refs)</sup>
- **Target:** whether the economy is in recession **12 months later**, the same target you just built.
- **Model:** a probit estimated on January 1959 to December 2009, with intercept −0.5333 and slope −0.6330.

Its November 5, 2026 update put the October 2026 spread at 0.98 points and the probability of recession in October 2027 at **12.4%**.<sup>[10](#refs)</sup>

Now look at the whole history. Each forecast is drawn at the month it is *about*, so a good model's peaks should land on the grey recession bars.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Has the curve stopped working?">
Nobody knows yet, and you should be wary of confident answers in either direction. In the middle of the 2023–24 episode, economists were already warning that the nominal spread "could be less informative than it has been in the past", for example because long-run and short-run inflation expectations had diverged unusually.<sup>[14](#refs)</sup> Others have argued that a near-term forward spread, built from expected short rates over the next 18 months, is a cleaner signal than the 10-year minus 3-month spread.<sup>[15](#refs)</sup> And the NBER lag means the final verdict on 2024–26 is not in. What you *can* say precisely is how much the episode hurt the model's track record. That is the next two sections.
</Callout>

### From probability to warning: pick a threshold

A probability is not yet an action. Your CFO wants to know: **do we freeze hiring or not?** As with Part 1's [call list](#from-probability-to-decision-the-confusion-matrix), you choose a threshold and read the confusion matrix. Forecasters of economic events use their own words for its cells: a **hit** is a warning followed by recession, a **false alarm** a warning followed by none, a **miss** a recession nobody warned of. The <Term def="Hits ÷ all months followed by recession. Part 1 called it recall, or the true positive rate.">hit rate</Term> is the share of pre-recession months you flagged; the <Term def="False alarms ÷ all months not followed by recession. Also called the false positive rate.">false alarm rate</Term> is the share of calm months you flagged by mistake.

The natural first guess, a 50% threshold, is a poor choice for rare events. In sample, only 21 of 802 months ever reached 50%, so the model flagged just **7 of 95** pre-recession months (a 7% hit rate). Lowering the threshold to 20% catches 56% of them, at the price of flagging 14% of calm months.

The ROC curve on the left, met in [Part 1](#plan-the-campaign-roc-auc-gains-and-lift), shows every threshold at once. Sliding the threshold only moves you *along* the curve; a better model shifts the whole curve toward the top-left corner. The cost chart on the right is where the business decision lives: if a missed recession costs your firm five times as much as a false alarm, the cheapest in-sample threshold is about **14%**. At that setting the model catches 74 of 95 pre-recession months and raises false alarms in a quarter of calm ones.

<Callout type="math" title="Why the best threshold is 1 / (1 + k)">
Suppose a false alarm costs 1 and a missed recession costs $k$. If you warn, your expected cost is $(1-p) \cdot 1$. If you stay quiet, it is $p \cdot k$. Warning is cheaper when $(1-p) \lt p\,k$, that is, when

$$
p \gt \frac{1}{1+k}.
$$

This is the same break-even logic as Part 1's $p^\ast = c/V$: act when the expected cost of acting falls below the expected cost of not acting. There a call cost $c$ whatever happened; here a warning costs something only when no recession follows, which happens with probability $1-p$, so the break-even is $p^\ast = 1/(1+k)$ rather than $1/k$. With $k = 5$, warn above $1/6 \approx 17\%$. This rule assumes the probabilities are well calibrated. The data-driven optimum in the threshold game (about 14% in sample, 16% out of sample) is close but not identical, because the model's probabilities are not perfectly calibrated.
</Callout>

<Callout type="business" title="Who should choose the threshold?">
Not the analyst alone. The threshold encodes a value judgment about the cost of errors. A bank deciding whether to raise loan-loss reserves, a retailer deciding on holiday inventory and a central bank deciding on policy face very different costs. Your job is to deliver a well-calibrated probability **and** to show decision makers the trade-off, ideally as a chart like the threshold game above. Report the probability itself, not just "recession: yes".
</Callout>

### Scoring the probabilities themselves

The confusion matrix depends on one threshold. Two scores judge the whole set of probabilities instead.

The <Term def="Area under the ROC curve. It equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive case gets a higher forecast than a randomly chosen negative case. 0.5 = coin flip, 1 = perfect ranking.">AUC</Term> (area under the ROC curve) measures **ranking**: the chance that a randomly chosen pre-recession month gets a higher probability than a randomly chosen calm month. In sample, the yield-curve logit scores **0.820**. A coin flip scores 0.5.

The <Term def="Mean squared difference between the forecast probability and the 0/1 outcome. Lower is better; 0 is perfect.">Brier score</Term> measures **accuracy and calibration together**: the average of $(p_t - y_t)^2$. It was proposed by the meteorologist Glenn Brier in 1950 for weather forecasts.<sup>[16](#refs)</sup> On its own the number is hard to read, so compare it with <Term def="A constant forecast equal to the historical frequency of the event (here: the share of months followed by recession).">climatology</Term>, a forecast that always says the base rate. In sample, the logit's Brier score is **0.091** against **0.104** for climatology. The <Term def="1 − (Brier of the model ÷ Brier of the benchmark). Positive = better than the benchmark.">Brier skill score</Term> is therefore 1 − 0.091 / 0.104 ≈ **13%**: the model removes about an eighth of the benchmark's squared error.

<Callout type="empirical" title="How to read a calibration chart">
Read it group by group, like a table:

- **Across** is what the model said (the group's average forecast); **up** is what happened (the share of those months followed by recession). The diagonal is "said = happened".
- **Below the diagonal = overconfident** (it happened less often than forecast); **above = underconfident**.
- **Weigh each dot by its size.** A group of 300 months that misses by 2 points is real information; a group of 10 that misses by 20 may be luck. Recessions are rare, so the high-probability groups are always the small ones.
- **Look hardest near your decision threshold.** If you act at 15–20%, calibration in that range matters more than at 70%.
</Callout>

#### Why it lost: reliability against resolution

*Optional depth: the one-sentence takeaway is in the last paragraph of this subsection.* The lab's last step takes the Brier score apart. Group the forecasts into ten bins (0–10%, 10–20%, …) and three pieces appear:<sup>[17](#refs)</sup>

- <Term def="The average squared gap between each bin's average forecast and the share of its months that actually saw the event. The calibration-error part of the Brier score: lower is better.">Reliability</Term>: how far each bin's forecasts sit from what happened in that bin. This is the calibration error; lower is better.
- <Term def="How much the event rates of the forecast bins differ from the overall event rate. The part of the Brier score that rewards telling risky cases from safe ones: higher is better.">Resolution</Term>: how different the bins' actual outcomes are from the overall rate. This is the ability to tell risky months from calm ones; higher is better.
- **Uncertainty**: $\bar y(1 - \bar y)$, fixed by how often recessions happened in the window. No model can change it, and it is exactly the Brier score of a constant forecast at that rate (slightly below the climatology column above, which uses the training base rate).

| Brier score, split (logit frozen in 1999) | Reliability | Resolution | Uncertainty | Brier |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| In sample, 1959–1999 | 0.004 | 0.030 | 0.118 | 0.091 |
| Out of sample, 2000–2019 | 0.003 | 0.021 | 0.103 | 0.084 |
| Out of sample, 2000–Oct 2025 | **0.031** | 0.011 | 0.082 | 0.102 |

Since Brier ≈ reliability − resolution + uncertainty, a model beats a constant forecast of the window's base rate only when its resolution exceeds its reliability. Through 2019 the frozen model kept its calibration and lost about a third of its resolution: still a clear win. Adding the forecasts made since 2020 multiplied the calibration error by about ten and halved the resolution, because probabilities near 80% in 2023–24 were followed by no recession. That is the precise sense in which the yield curve "broke": it still ranked months better than chance, but its probabilities stopped meaning what they said.

<Callout type="math" title="Murphy's decomposition of the Brier score">
Sort the $n$ forecasts into bins $k = 1, \dots, K$. Let $n_k$ be a bin's size, $\bar f_k$ its average forecast, $\bar o_k$ its actual event rate, and $\bar y$ the overall rate. Then

$$
\text{BS} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(p_t - y_t)^2 \;\approx\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k} n_k(\bar f_k - \bar o_k)^2}_{\text{reliability}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k} n_k(\bar o_k - \bar y)^2}_{\text{resolution}} \;+\; \underbrace{\bar y(1 - \bar y)}_{\text{uncertainty}} .
$$

The identity is exact when every forecast in a bin is the same; with ten bins the gap here is below 0.002. The Brier skill score against the window's base rate is approximately $(\text{resolution} - \text{reliability}) / \text{uncertainty}$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="math" title="Direct forecasts: one model per horizon">
The 3-month and 12-month models are separate fits, each with its own shifted target: $P(y_{t+h} = 1 \mid x_t) = \Lambda(a_h + b_h' x_t)$ for $h = 3$ and $h = 12$, where $\Lambda$ is the logistic curve. A **direct** model jumps straight to the horizon you care about, which is the natural choice when the predictors themselves (spreads, claims, sentiment) would otherwise need forecasting too. The price is one model per horizon, each with overlapping targets and therefore the Newey–West issue above.
</Callout>

## Chapter checklist

- A straight line through 0/1 data (the **LPM**) can forecast probabilities below 0 or above 1, has heteroskedastic errors by construction and forces constant effects. It still ranks reasonably well.
- The **logit** puts the line on the **log-odds** scale: `glm(y ~ x, family = binomial)`. The **probit** gives nearly the same probabilities; its coefficients are smaller by a factor of about 1.6 to 1.8.
- **Read a coefficient in words, with its unit.** $e^{b}$ is an **odds ratio**: "each point of Euribor goes with 34% lower odds" ($100 \times (\text{OR} - 1)$), never "34 points lower probability". Rescale by powers: a quarter point is $0.66^{0.25} = 0.90$. Stay inside the range of the data.
- **Report effects in percentage points as marginal effects**, $b\,p(1-p)$: averaged over clients (AME) or at chosen values. They depend on where you start; $b/4$ is the most they can be.
- **Translate for one case:** probability → "about 6 in 100 like her" → compared with the base rate → in money ($p \times V - c$). One outcome cannot prove a probability right or wrong.
- Both models are fitted by **maximum likelihood** (IRLS). Test added predictors with a **likelihood-ratio test**; McFadden's pseudo-R² of 0.2 to 0.4 is already a good fit.
- A threshold turns probabilities into actions. Report **precision, recall, specificity and F1**, not just accuracy, which rewards ignoring a rare outcome. Set the threshold from costs: call when $p \gt c/V$; warn of a recession when $p \gt 1/(1+k)$ if a miss costs $k$ times a false alarm.
- **ROC/AUC** measures ranking; **gain and lift** charts turn ranking into "call x% of the list, reach y% of buyers"; the **Brier score**, its **reliability/resolution** split and a **calibration** chart judge whether the probabilities mean what they say. A dot below the diagonal means overconfident.
- Ask of every predictor: **would I know it at forecast time?** Call duration fails, and its AUC of 0.915 is leakage.
- For time series, **build the target with a lead, not a lag** (`USREC` 12 months ahead; check one known date by hand; the latest values are provisional). Quote the **spread in percentage points** (1 point = 100 basis points), never as a percent change. Use **Newey–West** standard errors for overlapping targets, and test **out of sample**: the yield-curve model beat a constant through 2019 but lost to it once the 2022–24 false alarm entered the window.

One 30-minute activity per session. Teams of three. Data file for the first: [bank_marketing.csv](/data/bank_marketing.csv).

**Session 1 activity: campaign war room (30 minutes).**

1. **Predict (5 min).** Give each team a cost per call and a value per deposit: A = \$5 and \$50, B = \$5 and \$100, C = \$8 and \$40, D = \$5 and \$30. Before touching code, each team writes down what share of the list it expects to call and whether calling everyone would make money.
2. **Plan (15 min).** Teams run the classification lab, then change the `profit` line (or use the campaign planner for team A) to find the share of the list that maximizes profit on the test clients.
3. **Brief (10 min).** Each team presents one slide: the break-even probability $c/V$, the share called, expected subscribers, profit, and one sentence on why calling everyone is or is not a good idea.

*Expected results (five-fact logit, 1,237 test clients):*

| Team | Cost, value | Break-even $p$ | Best share called | Subscribers reached | Profit | Call everyone |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | \$5, \$50 | 0.10 | 31% (385 calls) | 92 | \$2,675 | \$615 |
| B | \$5, \$100 | 0.05 | 82% (1,020 calls) | 128 | \$7,700 | \$7,415 |
| C | \$8, \$40 | 0.20 | 18% (224 calls) | 70 | \$1,008 | −\$4,456 |
| D | \$5, \$30 | 0.17 | 21% (258 calls) | 76 | \$990 | −\$2,105 |

In every case the profit-maximizing cut-off probability lands near $c/V$ (11.8%, 3.4%, 18.5% and 17.6%). Higher value per deposit pushes the team to call much more of the list; for teams C and D, calling everyone loses money.

**Session 2 activity: recession call desk (30 minutes).** The same teams now play an economics desk advising a company's board.

1. **Predict (5 min).** Before running anything, each team writes down the probability it would have given in May 2023 for a recession by May 2024, and the threshold at which it would advise the board to freeze hiring.
2. **Model (10 min).** Run the timeline lab and the out-of-sample lab. Record the 1999-frozen model's probability for May 2024 and the full-sample model's.
3. **Decide (10 min).** In the threshold game, set the cost ratio the team believes fits its company (a retailer, a bank, a hospital) and read off the cheapest threshold. Would it have frozen hiring in 2023?
4. **Brief (5 min).** One slide: today's probability, the threshold, the decision, and one sentence on how much to trust the model.

*Expected results:* the frozen 1999 model gives about **80%** for May 2024; the full-sample logit about **61%**. With a cost ratio of 5, the cheapest threshold is about 14% in sample and 16% out of sample, so every team using the model would have warned in 2023, a false alarm as of the latest data. Through 2019 the model beats climatology on the Brier score (0.084 against 0.103); through October 2025 it loses (0.102 against 0.084), because its reliability penalty grew about tenfold. The latest reading is 9.7% for October 2027 (12.4% in the New York Fed's own published version). Use the gap between teams' gut probabilities and the model to discuss overconfidence.

**Variations.** (a) Add `month` to the bank logit (it is known before the call: the campaign schedule) and compare test AUC. (b) Replace the bank logit with a probit and confirm that the gain table barely changes. (c) In the under-the-hood lab, set `L <- 0` and then `L <- 48` and watch the Newey–West standard error move.
