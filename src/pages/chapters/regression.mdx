<p class="lede">In January 2026 a gallon of regular gasoline cost <strong>\$2.81</strong> on average. By May it cost <strong>\$4.48</strong>. Suppose you plan production for a carmaker. Your boss asks two questions: <em>how much do gas prices, jobs and interest rates actually move our sales?</em> And: <em>what happens if gas hits \$5?</em> Benchmarks from Chapter 3 cannot answer either question, because they only look at sales. To answer them you need a model that links sales to its <strong>drivers</strong>. That model is regression.</p>

In this chapter you will fit a driver model of U.S. car sales, read every number in its output, and turn it into scenarios with honest uncertainty.

<KeyIdea>A regression tells you how demand has <em>moved with</em> its drivers. It becomes a forecast only when you supply the drivers' future values, and those are uncertain too.</KeyIdea>

Session 1 is about **reading** a regression output in units a manager understands; session 2 is about **using** it: calendar dummies, honest forecast tests and scenarios. If you keep one page, keep the [cheat sheet](#how-to-read-a-coefficient).

## A line of best fit, by hand

Start with one driver. Below, each dot is one month: the 30-year mortgage rate on the horizontal axis and U.S. housing starts (new homes begun, in thousands at a seasonally adjusted annual rate) on the vertical axis. Your job is to draw the line that fits best.

What does "best" mean? For each month, the vertical gap between the dot and your line is a <Term def="The difference between an actual value and the value the line predicts: actual minus fitted. Positive when the dot is above the line.">residual</Term>. Square each residual and add them up. **Ordinary least squares (OLS)** picks the line with the smallest possible sum. The chart draws every residual as a real square.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Check the units before you read a single coefficient">
Write down the units of every variable first.

- **Sales (TOTALSA)** are in **millions at a seasonally adjusted annual rate** (SAAR): 16.0 means "if every month were like this one, the year would sell 16.0 million". So −0.48 is 480,000 fewer vehicles *a year*, about 40,000 in a single month. Misreading SAAR as a monthly count makes every effect 12 times too big.
- **Gas** is in nominal **dollars per gallon**; the **unemployment** and **mortgage** rates are in **percent**, so one unit is one percentage point.
- **Rescaling is cosmetic.** Sales in thousands multiplies the coefficient by 1,000; gas in cents divides it by 100; t-statistics and R² do not move ([details](#rescaling-units-what-changes-and-what-doesnt) in Beyond the basics).
- **Sanity-check the size.** 480,000 cars is 3% of a 16-million market for a \$1 swing in gas: plausible. A 30% swing would signal a units mistake.
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Percent or percentage points? Say which">
The unemployment coefficient, −0.90, is per **percentage point**. Unemployment going from 4.1% to 5.1% is **+1 point** (about 900,000 fewer cars a year), but it is *also* a 24% rise in the rate, a completely different number. Write "a one-point rise in the unemployment rate", never "a 1% rise", when you mean points. ([Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#differences-change-in-the-original-units) has the general rule.)
</Callout>

### Holding the other drivers fixed

"Holding jobs and rates fixed" is the most important and most misunderstood phrase in regression. A multiple-regression slope compares months that **match on the other drivers** and differ only in this one. Change the list of other drivers, and you change the comparison, so the slope changes too.

Add the drivers one at a time and the mortgage-rate slope keeps moving (all numbers in millions of cars per percentage point):

| Drivers in the model | Mortgage-rate slope | Read it as |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage rate alone | +0.067 | Higher-rate months had slightly *more* sales |
| + unemployment | −0.203 | Same unemployment, 1 point higher rates: 203,000 fewer cars |
| + unemployment + gas | −0.398 | … and the same gas price: 398,000 fewer |
| + chip dummy (full model) | −0.480 | … and the same chip status: 480,000 fewer |

It moves because the mortgage rate is **correlated** with the other drivers (−0.31 with unemployment, −0.51 with gas): a left-out driver's effect loads onto the drivers that are in. This is <Term def="The bias in a coefficient that arises when a left-out variable both affects y and is correlated with an included driver. The included driver gets credit (or blame) for the omitted one.">omitted-variable bias</Term>, the same trap as the housing-starts Predict above. Two practical rules:

1. Always say *what* you held fixed: "holding unemployment and gas prices fixed", not just "holding other things fixed".
2. A coefficient that swings as you add sensible drivers is fragile. Say so.

### Confidence intervals in words

`summary()` reports a <Term def="An estimate of how much a coefficient would vary across repeated samples. Small relative to the coefficient means it is precisely estimated.">standard error</Term> for each slope, and the <Term def="The coefficient divided by its standard error. Roughly, |t| above 2 means the coefficient is statistically different from zero at the 5% level.">t-statistic</Term> = estimate ÷ standard error. A 95% **confidence interval**, roughly the estimate ± 2 standard errors, is the range of effects the data are consistent with. For gas it runs from −0.81 to −0.16: each \$1 a gallon goes with between 160,000 and 810,000 fewer cars a year. Quote the range when the decision depends on the size.

"Significant" means "distinguishable from zero", not "large". "Not significant" is not "no effect": it means *we cannot tell*. One warning: textbook standard errors assume each month's error is unrelated to the next, and the lab's residual chart shows long runs of misses, so textbook intervals are too narrow. The gas interval above uses **Newey–West** standard errors, the usual fix ([Standard errors you can defend](#standard-errors-you-can-defend), in Beyond the basics).

### R² in words, and what it is not

R² = 0.58 means *58% of the variation in monthly sales around their average lines up with the four drivers.* Two cautions:

- The **residual standard error**, 1.28 million cars, is the typical in-sample miss **in the units of sales**. "Typically off by about 1.3 million cars a year" means more to a manager than "R² = 0.58".
- **R² is not forecast accuracy.** It is computed on the fitting data, and adding a variable never lowers it, even pure noise. Later this same model loses to the naive benchmark out of sample.

## Logs: reading coefficients as percentages

Managers think in percentages, and logs translate coefficients into them. The natural log (`log()` in R, `np.log()` in Python) turns *proportional* changes into *equal* steps: 10 → 11 and 20 → 22 are both +10%, and both add about 0.095 to the log. [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#transformations-every-analyst-uses) covers the transformation itself; here we read what it does to a coefficient.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Why analysts so often model log sales">
(1) **Effects scale with the market.** A \$1 rise in gas is unlikely to cost the same 480,000 cars in a 9-million market as in a 17-million one; a percent effect scales automatically. (2) **Seasonal swings grow with the level.** December retail sales ran \$47 billion above November in 2012 and \$74 billion above in 2025, but in percent the spike barely moved (+13% and +12%), so log seasonal dummies stay stable ([Chapter 2](/chapters/decomposition/#additive-or-multiplicative)). (3) **Coefficients become unit-free** percentages you can compare across products. Logs need strictly positive data.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

There are four combinations of logs and levels. Each gives the slope $b$ a different reading:<sup>[8, 10](#refs)</sup>

| Model | Form | Read the slope $b$ as | Our estimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| level–level | $y = a + b\,x$ | $y$ changes by $b$ units when $x$ rises by 1 unit | gas: −0.48, so −480,000 cars per \$1 |
| log–level | $\log y = a + b\,x$ | **semi-elasticity**: $y$ changes by about $100b$% when $x$ rises by 1 unit | unemployment: −0.063, so about 6% fewer sales per point |
| level–log | $y = a + b \log x$ | $y$ changes by $b/100$ units when $x$ rises by 1% | gas: −1.05, so −10,500 cars per 1% rise in gas |
| log–log | $\log y = a + b \log x$ | **elasticity**: $y$ changes by $b$% when $x$ rises by 1% | gas: −0.067, so 10% pricier gas goes with 0.67% fewer sales |

The explorable fits all four on the same data. Switch forms to see the coefficient's meaning change, then drag the gas price (it starts at today's \$4.31) and compare four answers to the *same* question.

**Large coefficients need the exact percent.** "Multiply by 100" is a shortcut: a log coefficient $b$ multiplies $y$ by $e^b$, so the exact change is $100(e^b - 1)$%.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup> Below about |b| = 0.1 the two agree; above it they do not. A December dummy of 0.221 means **+24.7%**, not +22.1% ([more](#exact-versus-approximate-percent) in Beyond the basics).

## How to read a coefficient

A one-page reference for the whole course,<sup>[8](#refs)</sup> with examples from this chapter's models.

| Situation | Read the coefficient $b$ as | Example from this chapter | Don't say |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Level–level**: $y = a + bx$ | A 1-unit rise in $x$ goes with $b$ units of $y$, holding the other drivers fixed | Gas −0.48: each \$1 a gallon goes with 480,000 fewer cars a year (SAAR) | "Gas prices cause…" |
| **Log–level**: $\log y = a + bx$ | A 1-unit rise in $x$ goes with about $100b$% change in $y$; exactly $100(e^b - 1)$% | Unemployment −0.063: each extra point, about 6.1% fewer cars | "−0.063 million cars" |
| **Level–log**: $y = a + b\log x$ | A 1% rise in $x$ goes with $b/100$ units of $y$ | Gas −1.05: each 1% rise in gas, about 10,500 fewer cars | "−1.05 per 1%" |
| **Log–log**: $\log y = a + b\log x$ | **Elasticity**: a 1% rise in $x$ goes with $b$% change in $y$ | Gas −0.067: 10% pricier gas, 0.67% fewer cars | "−0.067 cars per dollar" |
| **Dummy**, level $y$ | When the dummy is 1, $y$ is $b$ units higher than in the **base** group | Chip −2.43: 2.4 million fewer cars a year than the drivers predict | "The shortage cut sales by exactly 2.43 million" |
| **Dummy**, log $y$ | $100(e^b - 1)$% above the base category | December +0.221: 24.7% above **January** | "22% above November" |
| **Two non-base categories** | Subtract their coefficients first, then convert | December vs November: $e^{0.221 - 0.113} - 1$ = 11.4% | Comparing either one to the other's base |
| **Trend** in a log model | About $100b$% growth per period | Retail trend 0.0039: about 0.4% a month, 5% a year | "0.0039 dollars a month" |
| **Rates in percent** | Per **percentage point** | Unemployment 4.1% → 5.1% is +1 point (a 24% rise) | "A 1% rise in unemployment" |
| **Confidence interval** | The range of effects the data are consistent with | Gas, Newey–West: −0.81 to −0.16 | "Not significant, so no effect" |
| **R²** | Share of *in-sample* variation explained | 0.58 | "Forecasts 58% accurately" |

Two rules sit under every row: **name what you held fixed**, and **say association, not cause**.

## Dummy variables: teaching a regression the calendar

Many business series are **not seasonally adjusted** (NSA): every December, retail sales jump. A regression learns the calendar with **seasonal dummies**, one 0/1 variable per month except a base month, each shifting the intercept for its month. Add a **trend** (1, 2, 3, …) and a one-month **pulse dummy** for April 2020, when stores closed, and you have a surprisingly strong model.

Add the terms one at a time (log retail sales, January 2012 on). Trend alone gives R² = 0.872; monthly dummies raise it to 0.952 and cut the typical miss from about 7.6% to 4.8%. <Callout type="pitfall" title="The dummy variable trap">
With an intercept, use <strong>11</strong> monthly dummies, not 12: the 12 always add up to 1, exactly the intercept column, so they cannot be separated. R's `tslm(y ~ season)` and Python's `C(month)` drop the first month for you. For <em>k</em> groups, use <em>k</em> − 1 dummies.
</Callout>

### Reading a dummy: always relative to the base

A dummy's coefficient is never "the effect of December". It is **the gap between December and the base month** (January), holding the other terms fixed.

- **December vs November:** both are measured from January, so subtract first: $e^{0.221 - 0.113} - 1$ = **+11.4%**. Comparing either one directly with the other's month is the most common dummy mistake.
- **Event dummies** use "all other months" as the base. A **pulse** (one month, like April 2020: $e^{-0.269} - 1$ = −23.6%) absorbs a one-off shock; a **level shift** (a whole stretch, like the chip dummy) moves the intercept for the episode.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Pick the base category on purpose">
Changing the base month changes every seasonal coefficient but **not** the fitted values, forecasts or R². So choose the base your audience thinks in (an ordinary month such as September, say): `relevel()` in R, `C(month, Treatment(9))` in Python. Then name the base in every sentence: "December runs 25% above January", never "December is +0.22".
</Callout>

## The forecasting trap: you need tomorrow's drivers

The car-sales model links sales to this month's gas price, unemployment and mortgage rate. To forecast next year's sales you need next year's drivers. Nobody has those.

- An **ex-post** forecast plugs in the drivers' actual values after they are known. It tests the *relationship*, but no one could have made it at the time.
- An **ex-ante** forecast uses only what was known at the forecast date: forecasts of the drivers (or scenarios). This is the only kind you can hand your boss.
- Some predictors are **known in advance**: trend, calendar dummies, holidays, planned price changes, or a driver that leads sales (Chapter 8).

## What if gas hits \$5? Scenario forecasting

This is where regression earns its keep. You do not predict gas prices; you ask **what if**. Set a path for each driver and the regression turns it into a sales forecast. **Scenario forecasting** turns a debate about the future into explicit, comparable assumptions.

<ScenarioDashboard client:load />
</div>

1. **The gap between scenarios is just slope × change.** Gas rising from \$4.31 (October 2026) to \$5.00 lowers the forecast by 0.48 × 0.69 ≈ **0.33 million** cars a year once the rise is complete. The bars in the panel show each driver's contribution.
2. **Even a known scenario is uncertain.** Even if the drivers follow your path exactly, the 95% band after 12 months is about **5.1 million** cars wide: the regression's own error.
3. **Unknown drivers double the band.** When the drivers can also surprise you (the light band replays real historical moves), the 12-month band is about **10.2 million** wide, mostly on the downside: history includes recession-sized jumps in unemployment. The interval from `predict()` leaves this out.

## Prediction is not causation

A forecast needs a **stable association**: if gas and sales have moved together reliably, knowing gas helps forecast sales, whatever the reason. A decision about a lever ("should we cut our price?") needs a **causal effect**: what happens *because* you pull it. Historical regression delivers the first; the second only under strong assumptions. Each slope is a *partial association*, and it can mislead in three ways, all from this chapter:

- **A left-out driver.** Leave unemployment out and the mortgage rate appears to *raise* car sales. And 2008 had expensive gas **and** a financial crisis; some of the crisis may load onto the gas coefficient.
- **Reverse causation.** Housing starts and mortgage rates moved *together* in 2007–2012 because the housing bust pushed rates down, not because low rates killed construction.
- **One episode, many months.** The chip dummy rests on a single two-year event; its estimate mixes the shortage with everything else that happened in 2021–22.

For forecasting that is fine *as long as the relationships keep holding*. In scenarios, read each coefficient as "what usually came with" a change in a driver, and say so.

## Chapter checklist

- OLS draws the line that minimizes the **sum of squared residuals**. Read each slope as a change in $y$ per unit of $x$, **holding the other drivers fixed**, and name which drivers those are: an association, not proof of cause.
- **Check units first** (SAAR, dollars, percent). Rates move in **percentage points**. Rescaling $y$ or $x$ rescales $b$ and nothing else.
- A coefficient changes when you add a **correlated driver** (the mortgage rate goes from +0.07 alone to −0.48 in the full model): that is omitted-variable bias at work.
- Read a **confidence interval** as the range of effects the data allow. "Not significant" is not "no effect". R² describes the past; it is not forecast accuracy.
- **Logs** give percentages: log–level, about $100b$% per unit; level–log, $b/100$ units per 1%; log–log, an **elasticity**. For |b| above about 0.1, use the exact $100(e^b - 1)$%.
- **Dummies** are shifts from a **base category**: compare two other categories by subtracting their coefficients. Use k − 1 of them; `tslm()` builds `trend` and `season`.
- A driver model needs **future drivers**. Judge it by **ex-ante** accuracy against a benchmark; use it for **scenarios** with intervals, and remember those intervals leave out driver uncertainty.

Every outcome in this chapter was a quantity on a smooth scale: millions of cars, billions of dollars. Many business forecasts are not. *Will this customer buy?* and *will there be a recession next year?* are yes-or-no questions, and the useful answer is a probability between 0 and 1. [Chapter 5](/chapters/nonlinear-regression/) keeps the regression line you built here but puts it inside an S-shaped curve: logistic regression, applied first to bank clients and then to the whole economy.

#### The least-squares formulas

<Callout type="math" title="Slope, intercept and R² in formulas">
For one driver, the fitted line is $\hat y_t = b_0 + b_1 x_t$. Minimizing $\sum_t (y_t - b_0 - b_1 x_t)^2$ gives

$$
b_1 = \frac{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)(y_t - \bar y)}{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)^2}, \qquad b_0 = \bar y - b_1 \bar x .
$$

So the line always passes through the point of means $(\bar x, \bar y)$: rotating the flat "mean" line about that point is exactly what you did in the explorable.

R² compares your squares with the squares around the mean: $R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum e_t^2}{\sum (y_t - \bar y)^2}$. In the hero chart at the top of the page, it is one minus the ratio of the two bars.
</Callout>

#### Rescaling units: what changes and what doesn't

Analysts rescale variables all the time: sales in thousands instead of millions, prices in cents instead of dollars, a rate as 0.041 instead of 4.1. Rescaling changes the coefficient by a known factor and changes nothing else.

| You rescale | The coefficient | Its t-statistic, p-value and R² |
|---|---|---|
| y × 1,000 (millions → thousands) | × 1,000 | unchanged |
| x × 100 (dollars → cents) | ÷ 100 | unchanged |
| x ÷ 100 (4.1% → 0.041) | × 100 | unchanged |
| y in logs, then y × 1,000 | unchanged (only the intercept moves) | unchanged |

The last row is why elasticities are popular: they are **unit-free**. Measure sales in cars or in millions, in dollars or in euros, and a log–log coefficient stays the same.

#### Standard errors you can defend

The textbook standard errors from `summary()` assume each month's error is unrelated to the next. The residual chart in the lab shows they are not: when the model under-predicts one month, it keeps under-predicting for months. With autocorrelated errors the usual standard errors are too small, and you will "find" significance that isn't there.

The fix used across economics is **Newey–West** (heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent, HAC) standard errors:<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> `coeftest(m1, vcov. = NeweyWest(m1))`. In our model they are two to five times larger than the textbook ones. Gas, unemployment and mortgage rates remain clearly significant. The chip dummy's p-value rises to about 0.08: 24 consecutive months are really one episode, not 24 independent pieces of evidence. (That uses R's automatic lag choice. The Python tab uses a fixed 12-month window and finds the dummy significant, which tells you the evidence for it is fragile.)

All four 95% confidence intervals with Newey–West standard errors, read in words:

| Driver | Estimate | 95% interval (Newey–West) | In words |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gas price | −0.48 | −0.81 to −0.16 | Each \$1 a gallon goes with between about 160,000 and 810,000 fewer cars a year |
| Unemployment | −0.90 | −1.11 to −0.70 | Each extra point goes with 700,000 to 1.1 million fewer cars a year |
| Mortgage rate | −0.48 | −0.68 to −0.28 | Each extra point goes with 280,000 to 680,000 fewer cars a year |

The chip row shows why "not significant" is not "no effect": the interval contains zero, and it also contains very large losses.

#### R² across log and level models

<Callout type="empirical" title="Never compare R² across a level model and a log model">
The level model has R² = 0.583 and the log–level model 0.577. That does **not** make the level model better: one explains variation in sales, the other variation in *log* sales, and those are different totals. Compare models with different left-hand sides by their errors in the original units (millions of cars), out of sample.
</Callout>

#### Exact versus approximate percent

"Multiply a log coefficient by 100 to get a percent" is a shortcut. In a log model, a coefficient $b$ multiplies sales by $e^b$, so the exact change is $100 \times (e^b - 1)$ percent.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup> For small $b$ the two agree. For large ones they do not: the April 2020 pulse dummy in the retail model is −0.269, which the shortcut reads as −26.9% but is really **−23.6%**; December's +0.221 reads as +22.1% but is really **+24.7%**.

<div class="breakout">
<LogPercent client:load />
</div>

<Callout type="math" title="Where e^b − 1 comes from">
In $\log y = a + b\,x$, raising $x$ by $\Delta x$ raises $\log y$ by $b\,\Delta x$, so $y$ is multiplied by $e^{b\Delta x}$: the exact percent change is $100(e^{b\Delta x} - 1)$. Because $e^{z} \approx 1 + z$ for small $z$, this is about $100\,b\,\Delta x$. In a log–log model, raising $x$ by a factor $r$ (say $r = 1.25$ for +25%) multiplies $y$ by $r^{b}$, so the exact change is $100(r^{b} - 1)$, about $b \times$ (percent change in $x$) when the change is small. For a level–log model, a 1% rise in $x$ adds $b \log(1.01) \approx b/100$ to $y$.

These percent readings describe the typical (median) level of $y$. Turning a whole forecast of log sales back into dollars has one more wrinkle, the difference between the median and the mean, which [Chapter 10](/chapters/uncertainty/) covers.
</Callout>

#### Choosing a model without fooling yourself

Adding a predictor never lowers R², even when the predictor is pure noise, because OLS can always use a new column to shave a little off the squares. A model that fits noise is **overfit**: it describes the past in detail and forecasts the future badly. Watch it happen.

</div>

As the trend's degree rises from 1 to 8, in-sample R² climbs from 0.887 to 0.946. The 2019 test error tells a different story: 11.6 thousand starts with a straight line, a minimum of 9.2 with a parabola, then 31 at degree 5 and 180 at degree 8. High-degree polynomials bend wildly at the edge of the data, which is exactly where forecasts live.

To choose among candidate models, use a criterion that **penalizes complexity**:

- **Adjusted R²** charges a small price for each coefficient. It is the weakest penalty.
- **AIC** and **AICc** (AIC corrected for small samples) estimate how well the model would predict new data from the same process. Lower is better.
- **BIC** charges more per coefficient than AIC and prefers smaller models. Lower is better.
- **Cross-validation (CV)**: `CV()` in **forecast** reports leave-one-out CV, the average squared error when each month is predicted by a model fitted without it.
- **A time-ordered test set** (Chapter 3) is the most honest, because it respects the arrow of time.

All four in-sample criteria pick the five-driver model with the collinear 10-year yield. The time-ordered test picks the two-driver model. And none of them beats the naive benchmark's test RMSE of 0.58 from the ex-ante test. Report this honestly: the in-sample criteria tell you which model describes 1993–2026 best; the test tells you which one forecast 2024–26 best; they are different questions.

<Callout type="math" title="The criteria in formulas">
With $T$ observations, $k$ predictors (not counting the intercept) and $\text{SSE} = \sum e_t^2$:

$$
\bar R^2 = 1 - (1 - R^2)\frac{T - 1}{T - k - 1}, \qquad
\text{AIC} = T \log\left(\frac{\text{SSE}}{T}\right) + 2(k + 2),
$$

$$
\text{AIC}_c = \text{AIC} + \frac{2(k+2)(k+3)}{T - k - 3}, \qquad
\text{BIC} = T \log\left(\frac{\text{SSE}}{T}\right) + (k + 2)\log T .
$$

Leave-one-out CV for OLS needs no refitting: $\text{CV} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \left(\frac{e_t}{1 - h_t}\right)^2$, where $h_t$ is the leverage of observation $t$ (the diagonal of the hat matrix). These are the formulas `CV()` uses (Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, ch. 7).<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> The **variance inflation factor** of driver $j$ is $\text{VIF}_j = 1/(1 - R_j^2)$, where $R_j^2$ comes from regressing driver $j$ on all the other drivers.
</Callout>
