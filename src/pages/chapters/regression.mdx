<p class="lede">In January 2026 a gallon of regular gasoline cost <strong>\$2.81</strong> on average. By May it cost <strong>\$4.48</strong>. Suppose you plan production for a carmaker. Your boss asks two questions: <em>how much do gas prices, jobs and interest rates actually move our sales?</em> And: <em>what happens if gas hits \$5?</em> Benchmarks from Chapter 3 cannot answer either question, because they only look at sales. To answer them you need a model that links sales to its <strong>drivers</strong>. That model is regression.</p>

The gas prices above are monthly averages of the weekly U.S. regular-gasoline price (FRED series GASREGW). In this chapter you will fit a driver model of U.S. car sales, read every number in its output, run scenarios with honest uncertainty, and learn the three traps that make regression forecasts look better on paper than they are in practice.

<KeyIdea>A regression tells you how demand has <em>moved with</em> its drivers. It becomes a forecast only when you supply the drivers' future values, and those are uncertain too.</KeyIdea>

This chapter is taught in two sessions. The first is about **reading**: what each number in a regression output means, in units a manager understands, and how not to misread it. The second is about **using** the model: calendar dummies, honest forecast tests, scenarios and model choice. If you only keep one page from this chapter, keep the [cheat sheet](#how-to-read-a-coefficient).

## A line of best fit, by hand

Start with one driver. Below, each dot is one month: the 30-year mortgage rate on the horizontal axis and U.S. housing starts (new homes begun, in thousands at a seasonally adjusted annual rate) on the vertical axis. Your job is to draw the line that fits best.

What does "best" mean? For each month, the vertical gap between the dot and your line is a <Term def="The difference between an actual value and the value the line predicts: actual minus fitted. Positive when the dot is above the line.">residual</Term>. Square each residual and add them up. **Ordinary least squares (OLS)** picks the line with the smallest possible sum. In the chart, every residual is drawn as a real square, so you can *see* the quantity being minimized.

</div>

The line starts flat at the average, which is the Chapter 3 "mean" benchmark. Tilting it shrinks the squares; the snap button finds the minimum. For 1993–2007 the least-squares slope is about **−165**: in months when the mortgage rate was one percentage point higher, housing starts ran about 165,000 units (annual rate) lower. The line explains 37% of the variation (R² = 0.37).

<Callout type="math" title="The least-squares formulas">
For one driver, the fitted line is $\hat y_t = b_0 + b_1 x_t$. Minimizing $\sum_t (y_t - b_0 - b_1 x_t)^2$ gives

$$
b_1 = \frac{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)(y_t - \bar y)}{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)^2}, \qquad b_0 = \bar y - b_1 \bar x .
$$

So the line always passes through the point of means $(\bar x, \bar y)$: rotating the flat "mean" line about that point is exactly what you did above. With $k$ drivers, stack them in a matrix $X$ (with a column of 1s) and the solution is $b = (X'X)^{-1}X'y$. R's `lm()` computes this with a QR decomposition, which is more accurate than inverting $X'X$.

R² compares your squares with the squares around the mean: $R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum e_t^2}{\sum (y_t - \bar y)^2}$. In the hero chart at the top of the page, it is one minus the ratio of the two bars.
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Check the units before you read a single coefficient">
Before you interpret anything, write down the units of every variable. Here:

- **Sales (TOTALSA)** are in **millions of units at a seasonally adjusted annual rate** (SAAR). A value of 16.0 means "if every month were like this one, the year would sell 16.0 million vehicles". So −0.48 means 480,000 fewer vehicles *per year at an annual rate*, about 40,000 fewer in a single month. Misreading SAAR as a monthly count makes every effect look 12 times too big.
- **Gas** is in **dollars per gallon**, nominal (not adjusted for inflation). The **unemployment** and **mortgage** rates are in **percent**, so "one unit" is one percentage point.
- **Sanity-check the size.** 480,000 cars is 3% of a 16-million market for a \$1 swing in gas. That is plausible. A coefficient implying a 30% swing would mean a units mistake or a broken model.
</Callout>

### Rescaling units: what changes and what doesn't

Analysts rescale variables all the time: sales in thousands instead of millions, prices in cents instead of dollars, a rate as 0.041 instead of 4.1. Rescaling changes the coefficient by a known factor and changes nothing else.

| You rescale | The coefficient | Its t-statistic, p-value and R² |
|---|---|---|
| y × 1,000 (millions → thousands) | × 1,000 | unchanged |
| x × 100 (dollars → cents) | ÷ 100 | unchanged |
| x ÷ 100 (4.1% → 0.041) | × 100 | unchanged |
| y in logs, then y × 1,000 | unchanged (only the intercept moves) | unchanged |

The last row is why elasticities are popular: they are **unit-free**. Measure sales in cars or in millions, in dollars or in euros, and a log–log coefficient stays the same.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Percent or percentage points? Say which">
The unemployment coefficient, −0.90, is per **percentage point**. If unemployment goes from 4.1% to 5.1%, that is **+1 percentage point**, and the model expects about 900,000 fewer cars a year. It is *also* a 24% increase in the unemployment rate (1 ÷ 4.1), which is a completely different number. In a memo, write "a one-point rise in the unemployment rate", never "a 1% rise", when you mean points. The same goes for mortgage rates: 7.3% to 8.3% is one point. ([Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#differences-change-in-the-original-units) has the general rule.)
</Callout>

### Holding the other drivers fixed

The table above says "holding jobs and rates fixed". That phrase is the most important and most misunderstood idea in regression. A multiple-regression slope compares months that **match on the other drivers** and differ only in this one. Change the list of other drivers, and you change the comparison, so the slope changes too.

Add the drivers one at a time and the mortgage-rate slope keeps moving (all numbers in millions of cars per percentage point, from the lab below):

| Drivers in the model | Mortgage-rate slope | Read it as |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage rate alone | +0.067 | Months with higher rates had slightly *more* sales |
| + unemployment | −0.203 | Among months with the same unemployment, 1 point higher rates: 203,000 fewer cars |
| + unemployment + gas | −0.398 | … and the same gas price: 398,000 fewer |
| + chip dummy (the full model) | −0.480 | … and outside or inside the chip shortage alike: 480,000 fewer |

Why does it move? The mortgage rate is **correlated** with the other drivers (−0.31 with unemployment, −0.51 with gas). When a correlated driver is left out, its effect loads onto the drivers that are in. This is <Term def="The bias in a coefficient that arises when a left-out variable both affects y and is correlated with an included driver. The included driver gets credit (or blame) for the omitted one.">omitted-variable bias</Term>, the same trap as the housing-starts Predict above. Two practical rules:

1. Always say *what* you held fixed: "holding unemployment and gas prices fixed", not just "holding other things fixed".
2. A coefficient that swings a lot as you add sensible drivers is fragile. Report that, rather than the one specification that tells the best story.

### Standard errors you can defend

`summary()` reports a <Term def="An estimate of how much a coefficient would vary across repeated samples. Small relative to the coefficient means it is precisely estimated.">standard error</Term> for each slope, and the <Term def="The coefficient divided by its standard error. Roughly, |t| above 2 means the coefficient is statistically different from zero at the 5% level.">t-statistic</Term> = estimate ÷ standard error. Those formulas assume each month's error is unrelated to the next. The residual chart in the lab shows they are not: when the model under-predicts one month, it keeps under-predicting for months. With autocorrelated errors the usual standard errors are too small, and you will "find" significance that isn't there.

The fix used across economics is **Newey–West** (heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent, HAC) standard errors:<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> `coeftest(m1, vcov. = NeweyWest(m1))`. In our model they are two to five times larger than the textbook ones. Gas, unemployment and mortgage rates remain clearly significant. The chip dummy's p-value rises to about 0.08: 24 consecutive months are really one episode, not 24 independent pieces of evidence. (That uses R's automatic lag choice. The Python tab uses a fixed 12-month window and finds the dummy significant, which tells you the evidence for it is fragile.)

**Read a confidence interval in words.** A 95% confidence interval is roughly the estimate ± 2 standard errors: the range of effects the data are consistent with. With Newey–West standard errors:

| Driver | Estimate | 95% interval (Newey–West) | In words |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gas price | −0.48 | −0.81 to −0.16 | Each \$1 a gallon goes with between about 160,000 and 810,000 fewer cars a year |
| Unemployment | −0.90 | −1.11 to −0.70 | Each extra point goes with 700,000 to 1.1 million fewer cars a year |
| Mortgage rate | −0.48 | −0.68 to −0.28 | Each extra point goes with 280,000 to 680,000 fewer cars a year |
| Chip shortage | −2.43 | −5.12 to +0.26 | Anything from a 5-million drop to a small rise: zero is inside the interval |

Three habits: (1) quote the **range**, not just the point estimate, when the decision depends on the size; (2) "significant" means "distinguishable from zero", not "large" or "important"; (3) "not significant" is not "no effect": the chip interval also includes very large losses. It means *we cannot tell*.

### R² in words, and what it is not

The model's R² is 0.58. In words: *58% of the month-to-month variation in sales around their 1993–2026 average lines up with the four drivers.* Two numbers next to it are often more useful:

- The **residual standard error**, 1.28 million cars, is the model's typical in-sample miss **in the units of sales**. A manager understands "typically off by about 1.3 million cars a year (annual rate)" far better than "R² = 0.58".
- **R² is not forecast accuracy.** It is computed on the data used to fit the model. Later in this chapter the same model loses to the naive benchmark out of sample.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Never compare R² across a level model and a log model">
The level model has R² = 0.583 and the log–level model 0.577. That does **not** make the level model better: one explains variation in sales, the other variation in *log* sales, and those are different totals. Compare models with different left-hand sides by their errors in the original units (millions of cars), out of sample.
</Callout>

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why a great fit is not a great forecast">
R² measures how well the line fits the data it was <em>estimated on</em>. A forecast is judged on data the model has never seen. You will see below that adding variables never lowers R², often while making forecasts worse. Treat R² as a description of the past, never as a forecast score.
</Callout>

## Logs: reading coefficients as percentages

Managers think in percentages. Logs translate coefficients into them. Taking the log of a variable (natural log, `log()` in R, `np.log()` in Python) turns *proportional* changes into *equal* steps: a rise from 10 to 11 and from 20 to 22 are both +10%, and both add about 0.095 to the log. [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#transformations-every-analyst-uses) covers the transformation itself; here we read what it does to a coefficient.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Why analysts so often model log sales">
Three practical reasons, all visible in this chapter's data. (1) **Effects scale with the market.** A \$1 rise in gas is unlikely to cost the same 480,000 cars in a 9-million market as in a 17-million market; a percent effect scales with the market automatically. (2) **Seasonal swings grow with the level.** Retail sales in December 2012 ran \$47 billion above November; in December 2025 they ran \$74 billion above. In dollars the spike grew by more than half; in percent it barely moved (+13% and +12%). So seasonal dummies in a log model stay stable (see [additive vs multiplicative](/chapters/decomposition/#additive-or-multiplicative) in Chapter 2). (3) **Coefficients become unit-free** percentages and elasticities you can compare across products and countries. You need strictly positive data to take logs.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

There are four combinations of logs and levels. Each gives the slope $b$ a different reading:<sup>[8, 10](#refs)</sup>

| Model | Form | Read the slope $b$ as | Our estimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| level–level | $y = a + b\,x$ | $y$ changes by $b$ units when $x$ rises by 1 unit | gas: −0.48, so −480,000 cars per \$1 |
| log–level | $\log y = a + b\,x$ | **semi-elasticity**: $y$ changes by about $100b$% when $x$ rises by 1 unit | unemployment: −0.063, so about 6% fewer sales per point |
| level–log | $y = a + b \log x$ | $y$ changes by $b/100$ units when $x$ rises by 1% | gas: −1.05, so −10,500 cars per 1% rise in gas |
| log–log | $\log y = a + b \log x$ | **elasticity**: $y$ changes by $b$% when $x$ rises by 1% | gas: −0.067, so 10% pricier gas goes with 0.67% fewer sales |

The explorable fits all four on the same data. Switch the forms and watch the coefficient's units and meaning change. Then drag the gas price and compare the four answers to the *same* question (the explorable starts from today's \$4.31 a gallon).

### Exact versus approximate percent

"Multiply a log coefficient by 100 to get a percent" is a shortcut. In a log model, a coefficient $b$ multiplies sales by $e^b$, so the exact change is $100 \times (e^b - 1)$ percent.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup> For small $b$ the two agree. For large ones they do not: the April 2020 pulse dummy in the retail model is −0.269, which the shortcut reads as −26.9% but is really **−23.6%**; December's +0.221 reads as +22.1% but is really **+24.7%**.

<div class="breakout">
<LogPercent client:load />
</div>

<Callout type="math" title="Where e^b − 1 comes from">
In $\log y = a + b\,x$, raising $x$ by $\Delta x$ raises $\log y$ by $b\,\Delta x$, so $y$ is multiplied by $e^{b\Delta x}$: the exact percent change is $100(e^{b\Delta x} - 1)$. Because $e^{z} \approx 1 + z$ for small $z$, this is about $100\,b\,\Delta x$. In a log–log model, raising $x$ by a factor $r$ (say $r = 1.25$ for +25%) multiplies $y$ by $r^{b}$, so the exact change is $100(r^{b} - 1)$, about $b \times$ (percent change in $x$) when the change is small. For a level–log model, a 1% rise in $x$ adds $b \log(1.01) \approx b/100$ to $y$.

These percent readings describe the typical (median) level of $y$. Turning a whole forecast of log sales back into dollars has one more wrinkle, the difference between the median and the mean, which [Chapter 10](/chapters/uncertainty/) covers.
</Callout>

## Prediction is not causation

A forecasting model needs a **stable association**: if gas prices and sales have moved together in a reliable way, knowing gas helps forecast sales, whatever the reason. A decision about a lever you control ("should we cut our price?", "should the Fed raise rates?") needs a **causal effect**: what happens to sales *because* you change the lever. Regression on historical data delivers the first. It delivers the second only under strong assumptions.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="&quot;Holding fixed&quot; is not &quot;causes&quot;">
Each slope is a <em>partial association</em>: the gap in sales between two months that differ in one driver but match on the others. That is useful for forecasting and scenarios. It is not proof that gas prices <em>cause</em> the gap: 2008 had expensive gas <strong>and</strong> a financial crisis, and some of the crisis may be loading onto the gas coefficient. The gas price here is also nominal (not adjusted for inflation), so \$4 meant more in 2008 than in 2026.
</Callout>

Three ways an association misleads, all from this chapter:

- **A left-out driver.** Leave unemployment out and the mortgage rate appears to *raise* car sales (the explorable above).
- **Reverse causation.** Housing starts and mortgage rates moved *together* in 2007–2012 because the housing bust pushed rates down, not because low rates killed construction.
- **One episode, many months.** The chip dummy rests on a single two-year event; its estimate mixes the shortage with everything else that happened in 2021–22.

For forecasting, that is fine *as long as the relationships keep holding*. For scenarios, treat each coefficient as "what usually came with" a change in a driver, and say so.

| Situation | Read the coefficient $b$ as | Example from this chapter | Don't say |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Level–level**: $y = a + bx$ | A 1-unit rise in $x$ goes with $b$ units of $y$, holding the other drivers fixed | Gas −0.48: each \$1 a gallon goes with 480,000 fewer cars a year (SAAR) | "Gas prices cause…" |
| **Log–level**: $\log y = a + bx$ | A 1-unit rise in $x$ goes with about $100b$% change in $y$; exactly $100(e^b - 1)$% | Unemployment −0.063: each extra point, about 6.1% fewer cars | "−0.063 million cars" |
| **Level–log**: $y = a + b\log x$ | A 1% rise in $x$ goes with $b/100$ units of $y$ | Gas −1.05: each 1% rise in gas, about 10,500 fewer cars | "−1.05 per 1%" |
| **Log–log**: $\log y = a + b\log x$ | **Elasticity**: a 1% rise in $x$ goes with $b$% change in $y$ | Gas −0.067: 10% pricier gas, 0.67% fewer cars | "−0.067 cars per dollar" |
| **Dummy**, level $y$ | When the dummy is 1, $y$ is $b$ units higher than in the **base** group | Chip −2.43: 2.4 million fewer cars a year than the drivers predict | "The shortage cut sales by exactly 2.43 million" |
| **Dummy**, log $y$ | $100(e^b - 1)$% above the base category | December +0.221: 24.7% above **January** | "22% above November" |
| **Two non-base categories** | Subtract their coefficients first, then convert | December vs November: $e^{0.221 - 0.113} - 1$ = 11.4% | Comparing either one to the other's base |
| **Trend** in a log model | About $100b$% growth per period | Retail trend 0.0039: about 0.4% a month, 5% a year | "0.0039 dollars a month" |
| **Rescaled units** | $y \times c$: $b \times c$. $x \times c$: $b \div c$. t, p, R² unchanged | Sales in thousands, gas in cents: −4.8 | "The effect got 10 times bigger" |
| **Rates in percent** | Per **percentage point** | Unemployment 4.1% → 5.1% is +1 point (a 24% rise) | "A 1% rise in unemployment" |
| **Confidence interval** | The range of effects the data are consistent with | Gas, Newey–West: −0.81 to −0.16 | "Not significant, so no effect" |
| **R²** | Share of *in-sample* variation explained | 0.58 | "Forecasts 58% accurately" |

Two rules sit under every row: **name what you held fixed**, and **say association, not cause**, unless you have a design that justifies more.

## Dummy variables: teaching a regression the calendar

Many business series are reported **not seasonally adjusted** (NSA): every December, retail sales jump. A regression can learn the calendar with **seasonal dummies**: one 0/1 variable per month except a baseline month. Each dummy's coefficient shifts the intercept for its month. Add a **trend** (1, 2, 3, … counting months) and a one-month **pulse dummy** for April 2020, when stores closed, and you have a surprisingly strong forecasting model.

Try the terms one at a time on retail sales (January 2012 to the latest month, fitted to log sales). Trend alone explains 87% of the variation in log sales (R² = 0.872). <Callout type="pitfall" title="The dummy variable trap">
With an intercept in the model, use <strong>11</strong> monthly dummies, not 12. The 12 dummies always add up to 1, which is exactly the intercept column, so the computer cannot separate them (perfect multicollinearity). R's `tslm(y ~ season)` and Python's `C(month)` drop the first month for you. The same rule applies to any category: for <em>k</em> groups, use <em>k</em> − 1 dummies.
</Callout>

### Reading a dummy: always relative to the base

A dummy's coefficient is never "the effect of December". It is **the gap between December and the base category**, holding the other terms fixed. In the retail model (log sales, January as base):

- **December vs January:** $b_{Dec}$ = 0.221, so $100(e^{0.221} - 1)$ = **+24.7%**.
- **December vs November:** both are measured from January, so subtract first: $e^{0.221 - 0.113} - 1$ = **+11.4%**. Comparing either coefficient directly with the other's month is the most common dummy mistake.
- **An event dummy** works the same way, with "all other months" as the base. A **pulse** (1 for one month, like April 2020: $e^{-0.269} - 1$ = −23.6%) absorbs a one-off shock. A **level shift** (1 for a whole stretch, like the 24-month chip dummy) moves the intercept for the whole episode.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Pick the base category on purpose">
Changing the base month changes every seasonal coefficient, but **not** the fitted values, the forecasts or R²: the model just reports the same pattern from a different reference point. So choose the base your audience thinks in. A retailer may want "% above an ordinary month" (pick a typical month such as September as base); a planner may want "% above January". In R, `relevel()` on a factor picks the base; in Python, `C(month, Treatment(9))` makes September the base. Then say the base in every sentence: "December runs 25% above January", never "December is +0.22".
</Callout>

## The forecasting trap: you need tomorrow's drivers

Here is the catch that separates regression *analysis* from regression *forecasting*. The car-sales model says sales depend on this month's gas price, unemployment and mortgage rate. To forecast next year's sales you need next year's gas prices, unemployment and mortgage rates. Nobody has those.

- An **ex-post** forecast plugs in the drivers' actual values after they are known. It tests whether the *relationship* held up, but no one could have made it at the time.
- An **ex-ante** forecast uses only what was known at the forecast date: forecasts of the drivers (or scenarios). This is the only kind you can hand your boss.
- Some predictors are **known in advance** and avoid the problem: trend, calendar dummies, holidays, planned price changes, or a driver that leads sales by several months (Chapter 8).

## What if gas hits \$5? Scenario forecasting

This is where regression earns its keep. You do not need to predict gas prices; you ask **what if**. Set a path for each driver and the regression turns it into a sales forecast. This is **scenario forecasting**. It turns a debate about the future into a set of explicit, comparable assumptions.

<ScenarioDashboard client:load />
</div>

Three things to notice:

1. **The gap between scenarios is just slope × change.** Gas rising from \$4.31 (October 2026) to \$5.00 lowers the forecast by 0.48 × 0.69 ≈ **0.33 million** cars a year once the rise is complete. The bars in the panel show each driver's contribution.
2. **Even a known scenario is uncertain.** If gas, unemployment and rates follow your path exactly, the 95% band after 12 months is still about **5.1 million** cars wide. That is the regression's own error.
3. **Unknown drivers double the band.** Once the drivers can also surprise you (the light band replays real historical month-to-month moves in all three at once), the 12-month band is about **10.2 million** cars wide, and almost all of the extra width is on the downside: history includes recession-sized jumps in unemployment, such as April 2020. The textbook interval from `predict()` leaves this out, so it understates real uncertainty.

## Choosing a model without fooling yourself

Adding a predictor never lowers R², even when the predictor is pure noise, because OLS can always use a new column to shave a little off the squares. A model that fits noise is **overfit**: it describes the past in detail and forecasts the future badly. Watch it happen.

<div class="breakout">
<OverfitMachine client:load />
</div>

As the trend's degree rises from 1 to 8, in-sample R² climbs from 0.887 to 0.946. The 2019 test error tells a different story: 11.6 thousand starts with a straight line, a minimum of 9.2 with a parabola, then 31 at degree 5 and 180 at degree 8. High-degree polynomials bend wildly at the edge of the data, which is exactly where forecasts live.

To choose among candidate models, use a criterion that **penalizes complexity**:

- **Adjusted R²** charges a small price for each coefficient. It is the weakest penalty.
- **AIC** and **AICc** (AIC corrected for small samples) estimate how well the model would predict new data from the same process. Lower is better.
- **BIC** charges more per coefficient than AIC and prefers smaller models. Lower is better.
- **Cross-validation (CV)**: `CV()` in **forecast** reports leave-one-out CV, the average squared error when each month is predicted by a model fitted without it.
- **A time-ordered test set** (Chapter 3) is the most honest, because it respects the arrow of time.

All four in-sample criteria pick the five-driver model with the collinear 10-year yield. The time-ordered test picks the two-driver model. And none of them beats the naive benchmark's 0.58 from the previous lab. Report this honestly: the in-sample criteria tell you which model describes 1993–2026 best; the test tells you which one forecast 2024–26 best; they are different questions.

<Callout type="math" title="The criteria in formulas">
With $T$ observations, $k$ predictors (not counting the intercept) and $\text{SSE} = \sum e_t^2$:

$$
\bar R^2 = 1 - (1 - R^2)\frac{T - 1}{T - k - 1}, \qquad
\text{AIC} = T \log\left(\frac{\text{SSE}}{T}\right) + 2(k + 2),
$$

$$
\text{AIC}_c = \text{AIC} + \frac{2(k+2)(k+3)}{T - k - 3}, \qquad
\text{BIC} = T \log\left(\frac{\text{SSE}}{T}\right) + (k + 2)\log T .
$$

Leave-one-out CV for OLS needs no refitting: $\text{CV} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \left(\frac{e_t}{1 - h_t}\right)^2$, where $h_t$ is the leverage of observation $t$ (the diagonal of the hat matrix). These are the formulas `CV()` uses (Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, ch. 7).<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> The **variance inflation factor** of driver $j$ is $\text{VIF}_j = 1/(1 - R_j^2)$, where $R_j^2$ comes from regressing driver $j$ on all the other drivers.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Three ways regression forecasts fool you">
<ul>
<li><strong>Ex-post flattery.</strong> Testing with the drivers' true future values hides the hardest part of the job.</li>
<li><strong>In-sample flattery.</strong> R², t-statistics and even AIC are computed on the data used for fitting. Only out-of-sample tests measure forecasting skill.</li>
<li><strong>Autocorrelated residuals.</strong> In time series they are the norm. They make standard errors and prediction intervals too narrow. Use Newey–West for inference now, and dynamic regression (Chapter 8) for forecasting.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

## Chapter checklist

- OLS draws the line that minimizes the **sum of squared residuals**. Read each slope as a change in $y$ per unit of $x$, **holding the other drivers fixed**, and name which drivers those are: an association, not proof of cause.
- **Check units first** (SAAR, millions, dollars, percent). Rescaling $y$ or $x$ rescales $b$ but leaves t, p and R² unchanged; rates in percent move in **percentage points**.
- A coefficient changes when you add a **correlated driver** (the mortgage rate goes from +0.07 alone to −0.48 in the full model): that is omitted-variable bias at work.
- **Logs** give percentages: log–level, about $100b$% per unit; level–log, $b/100$ units per 1%; log–log, an **elasticity**. For |b| above about 0.1, use the exact $100(e^b - 1)$%. Never compare R² across log and level models.
- **Dummies** are shifts from a **base category**: compare two other categories by subtracting their coefficients. Use k − 1 of them; `tslm()` builds `trend` and `season`; **Fourier** terms are a compact alternative.
- Read a **confidence interval** as the range of effects the data allow; use **Newey–West** standard errors when residuals are autocorrelated. "Not significant" is not "no effect".
- A driver model needs **future drivers**. Judge it by **ex-ante** accuracy against a benchmark; use it for **scenarios** with intervals, and remember those intervals leave out driver uncertainty.
- Choose models with **AIC, BIC, CV or a time-ordered test**, not R². **Multicollinearity** (VIF above about 10) muddles coefficients but barely changes predictions.

Every outcome in this chapter was a quantity on a smooth scale: millions of cars, billions of dollars. Many business forecasts are not. *Will this customer buy?* and *will there be a recession next year?* are yes-or-no questions, and the useful answer is a probability between 0 and 1. [Chapter 5](/chapters/nonlinear-regression/) keeps the regression line you built here but puts it inside an S-shaped curve: logistic regression, applied first to bank clients and then to the whole economy.

**Activity 1 (Session 1): coefficient translator (10 minutes).** Pairs get four "coefficient cards" and write one memo sentence for each, with units, the drivers held fixed, and the exact percent where needed. Then they swap and critique.

1. Level model: gas −0.48 (sales in millions, SAAR; gas in \$ per gallon).
2. Log–level model: unemployment −0.0627.
3. Log–log model: gas −0.0674. Question: what does a 20% rise in gas do?
4. Retail log model: December +0.221, November +0.113 (January is the base). Question: December vs November?

*Expected results:* (1) "Each \$1 a gallon goes with about 480,000 fewer vehicles a year (annual rate), holding unemployment and mortgage rates fixed." (2) "Each extra percentage point of unemployment goes with about 6.1% fewer sales" ($e^{-0.0627} - 1$ = −6.08%). (3) "A 20% rise in gas goes with about 1.2% fewer sales" (rule of thumb −1.35%; exact $1.2^{-0.0674} - 1$ = −1.22%, about 196,000 cars on today's 16.0 million). (4) $e^{0.221 - 0.113} - 1$ = +11.4%; December vs January is +24.7%. Watch for "a 1% rise in unemployment", "22%" for December, and "causes".

**Activity 2 (Session 2): scenario war-room (25 minutes).** Teams of three play the planning team of a carmaker.

1. **Predict (3 min).** Each team writes down how many fewer cars a year it expects if gas goes to \$5 and how sure it is.
2. **Model (10 min).** Run the drivers lab and the scenario lab. Each team designs one *named, internally consistent* scenario of its own in the scenario dashboard and in code (change the `squeeze` data frame).
3. **Brief (8 min).** Each team presents a 60-second brief: baseline, their scenario, the gap from baseline by driver, and the band.
4. **Debrief (4 min).** Reveal the ex-ante vs ex-post result and ask whether the scenarios should change any decisions.

*Expected results:* the fitted model is sales = 24.98 − 0.48 gas − 0.90 unemployment − 0.48 mortgage rate − 2.43 chip (R² 0.58). With every driver at its October 2026 value, the baseline is 15.68 million; gas at \$5 gives 15.35 million (−0.33 once the rise is complete); the lab's "squeeze" scenario averages 14.45 million over the year and ends at 13.76 million. The 95% interval is about ±2.6 million. Compare the teams' guesses with −0.33 million (or about −0.16 million under the log–log model, as FormSwitcher shows); if they guessed larger, discuss why, and stress that the estimate is an association, not a controlled experiment. In the ex-ante test, naive (MAE 0.42) beats ex-ante (0.48) and ex-post (0.54).

**Variations.** (a) Restrict the drivers lab to 1993–2019 and compare coefficients (structural stability). (b) Re-run with `log(veh)` and report elasticities. (c) Instructors who use `jtools` can show `summ(m1, robust = "HAC")` as an alternative summary; `car::vif()` reproduces the VIFs in the selection lab (21.9 and 23.8).
