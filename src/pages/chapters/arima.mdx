<p class="lede">In September 2026, core PCE prices, the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge, were up 3.1% from a year earlier. The Fed's target is 2%. Your manager asks a simple question: <strong>where will inflation be a year from now?</strong> Before you can answer, you have to decide something deeper. When inflation is pushed away from its usual level, does it come back on its own, or does it stay wherever the last shock left it?</p>

That question has a name: **stationarity**. It decides whether your forecast drifts back toward a long-run average or stays flat at today's value. It decides whether your forecast intervals level off or widen forever. This chapter turns it into something you can see, test and model, and ends with a 12-month inflation outlook you could hand to a manager.

The first session is about one practical decision that comes before any model: **should you difference the series, and why?** The second session builds ARIMA models, reads their output in plain words, and turns their forecasts back into the units your manager cares about.

## Shocks that fade, shocks that stick

Start with the simplest model of persistence, the <Term def="An autoregressive model of order 1: today's value equals a fraction φ of yesterday's value plus a new random shock.">AR(1)</Term>:

$$
y_t = \phi\, y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t
$$

Here $y_t$ is how far the series sits from its long-run mean, and $\varepsilon_t$ is a fresh, unpredictable shock each month, called <Term def="A sequence of independent random shocks with mean zero and constant variance. Nothing about its past helps predict its future.">white noise</Term>. The single number φ (phi) controls everything. Imagine inflation has just been pushed 4 points above its normal level. Drag the dial and watch 40 possible futures unfold.

Three regimes appear as you turn the dial:

- **φ below 1 (stationary).** The paths are pulled back toward the mean. Half of any shock is gone after log(0.5)/log(φ) months, its <Term def="The number of periods until half of a shock's effect has died out. For an AR(1), log(0.5)/log(φ).">half-life</Term>. The forecast band widens for a while and then **levels off** at the long-run range.
- **φ = 1 (a random walk, or <Term def="A series with a unit root: shocks are permanent, so it has no fixed mean to return to. A random walk is the simplest example.">unit root</Term>).** Nothing pulls the paths back. Each shock is added to all the ones before, and the band grows **without limit**. The best forecast is simply today's value.
- **φ above 1 (explosive).** Shocks are amplified. That is how bubbles look, and few economic series behave like this for long.

<KeyIdea>A stationary series forgets its shocks; a random walk remembers every one. That single difference decides whether a forecast returns to an average or stays put, and whether its intervals level off or keep widening.</KeyIdea>

### Reading φ in plain words: persistence and half-life

An estimated AR coefficient is easiest to explain as a share. **φ is the fraction of today's gap from the long-run mean that is still there next month.** With φ = 0.8, a shock that pushes a series 10 units above its mean leaves 8 units next month, 6.4 the month after, and $10 \times 0.8^h$ after $h$ months. The gap shrinks by the same *percentage* every month, so it never quite reaches zero. That is why analysts summarize persistence with the **half-life**: the number of periods until half of the shock is gone.

| φ (monthly) | Left after 1 month | Left after 12 months | Half-life |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.50 | 50% | 0.02% | 1 month |
| 0.80 | 80% | 7% | 3.1 months |
| 0.90 | 90% | 28% | 6.6 months |
| 0.95 | 95% | 54% | 13.5 months |
| 0.99 | 99% | 89% | 69 months |
| 1.00 | 100% | 100% | never |

Notice how the table bends. Moving φ from 0.5 to 0.9 adds five and a half months to the half-life; moving it from 0.95 to 0.99 adds more than four years. Near 1, small differences in φ are huge differences in behavior, and that is exactly where many economic series live.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Report persistence as a half-life, with its units">
"φ = 0.962" means little to a manager. "Half of an inflation surprise is still there after about a year and a half" means a lot. Two habits make the number honest:

- **State the frequency.** The same persistence is φ = 0.962 in monthly data, about 0.89 in quarterly data ($0.962^3$) and about 0.63 in annual data ($0.962^{12}$). The half-life in *months* is the same in each case. Compare φ's only across series of the same frequency.
- **Use the right model's φ.** In this chapter's fit lab, a plain AR(1) on monthly core inflation gives φ = 0.43 (a half-life under one month), but the ARMA(1,1) gives φ = 0.96 (18 months). The MA term soaks up month-to-month noise, so the AR coefficient measures how long the *underlying* rate stays away from its mean. Ask which persistence your question is about.
</Callout>

Formally, a series is <Term def="A series whose mean, variance and autocorrelations do not change over time. It fluctuates around a fixed level and keeps returning to it.">stationary</Term> if its mean and variance stay constant over time, and the correlation between $y_t$ and $y_{t-k}$ depends only on the gap $k$, not on the date. White noise and an AR(1) with $|\phi| < 1$ are stationary. A random walk is not: its variance grows with time.

<Callout type="math" title="Why the AR(1) band levels off and the random walk's does not">
Substitute the AR(1) into itself $h$ times:

$$
y_{T+h} = \phi^h y_T + \varepsilon_{T+h} + \phi\,\varepsilon_{T+h-1} + \dots + \phi^{h-1}\varepsilon_{T+1}.
$$

The forecast made at time $T$ is the expected value, $\hat y_{T+h} = \phi^h y_T$, which shrinks toward the mean (zero) when $|\phi|<1$. The forecast error is the sum of the future shocks, so its variance is

$$
\operatorname{Var}(y_{T+h} - \hat y_{T+h}) = \sigma^2 \left(1 + \phi^2 + \phi^4 + \dots + \phi^{2(h-1)}\right) \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{\sigma^2}{1-\phi^2} \quad (h \to \infty).
$$

The geometric sum converges when $|\phi| < 1$: that limit is the long-run range on the dial. When $\phi = 1$ every term equals 1, the variance is $h\sigma^2$, and the 95% band is $\pm 1.96\,\sigma\sqrt{h}$: it never stops growing. A **random walk with drift** adds a constant, $y_t = c + y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$, so the forecast is a straight line with slope $c$, wrapped in the same widening band.
</Callout>

## Two kinds of trend

Most economic series in levels go up over time: prices, GDP, payrolls. A trend can arise in two very different ways.

| | Deterministic trend | Stochastic trend |
|---|---|---|
| Model | $y_t = a + bt + u_t$, with $u_t$ stationary | $y_t = b + y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ (random walk with drift) |
| After a shock | The series returns to the trend line | The trend line itself moves, permanently |
| Forecast intervals | Bounded width around the line | Widen like $\sqrt h$ |
| Fix | Regress on time and model the residuals (**detrending**) | Model the changes (**differencing**) |

The two can look nearly identical in a chart. They imply very different forecasts, though: after a recession, does output return to its old trend path, or has the path been permanently lowered? Nelson and Plosser (1982) famously found that most long U.S. macroeconomic series behave more like the stochastic kind.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup> That is why differencing, not detrending, is the default in this chapter.

## What a trend does to a regression and a forecast

Why not just model the level and be done with it? Because a trend, especially a stochastic one, quietly breaks two things you care about.

**It invents relationships.** Two series that both trend upward will line up in a scatter plot even when nothing connects them. Food prices and online sales both rose steadily from 2000 to 2019, for completely different reasons.

This is <Term def="A regression between unrelated nonstationary series that looks statistically significant only because both trend.">spurious regression</Term>. It is the main reason econometricians test for unit roots before regressing one time series on another. [Chapter 8](/chapters/dynamic-regression/#spurious-regression-when-two-random-walks-look-related) will show by simulation how often it happens with pure random walks (about three times in four).

**It decides where the forecast goes.** A model fitted to a trending level must say what happens to the trend. A model with a unit root says shocks are permanent: its forecast stays wherever the last shock left it (or keeps rising at the average drift) and **never reverts**. A stationary model says the series comes back to its mean or trend line. You saw both on the persistence dial; in session 2 you will see them disagree about real inflation by 0.3 points a year out.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Difference before you correlate trending series">
Before you report a correlation or a regression between two time series, look at both in levels. If both trend, rerun the analysis on **growth rates** (100 × the change in logs) or **changes**. A relationship that survives differencing is about *movements*: "months when online sales grow faster are months when food prices…". One that vanishes was about two trends.

In the lab below, the levels regression of food prices on e-commerce sales reports R² = 0.96 and t = 76. In growth rates, R² = 0.000 and t = −0.08. The one exception is when two trending series share the *same* stochastic trend, so a combination of them is stationary (<Term def="Two nonstationary series are cointegrated when some combination of them is stationary: they wander, but together, like a dog on a leash.">cointegration</Term>); Chapter 8 will discuss it. For reading the slope of a growth-rate regression, see [Chapter 4](/chapters/regression/#logs-reading-coefficients-as-percentages): with both sides in log differences, it reads as an elasticity of growth rates.
</Callout>

## Spot the random walk

Your eyes are the first test of stationarity. Try them on four mystery series before any statistics.

<div class="breakout">
<SpotRandomWalk client:load />
</div>

The game uses two formal tests, and the most important fact about them is that **their null hypotheses are opposite**:

| Test | R function | Null hypothesis | A small p-value (< 0.05) means… |
|---|---|---|---|
| Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF)<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> | `adf.test()` | The series has a unit root | Evidence of stationarity |
| KPSS<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> | `kpss.test()` | The series is stationary | Evidence of a unit root |

Because the nulls are opposite, use the tests together. When ADF rejects and KPSS does not, both point to stationary. When KPSS rejects and ADF does not, both point to a unit root. When they disagree, the data cannot settle the question, and you need judgment. The function `ndiffs()` from the forecast package automates the common recipe: it applies KPSS, differences, and repeats until the test stops rejecting, then tells you how many differences it took.

### Reading the two tests in words

A p-value is the answer to one narrow question: *if the null were true, how surprising is this sample?* So every test result turns into a sentence with three parts: **the null, the decision, and what it means for differencing.**

- ADF, p = 0.82: "If the series had a unit root, data like these would be quite ordinary. We **cannot reject** a unit root." That is weak evidence: it does *not* prove there is one.
- KPSS, p ≤ 0.01: "If the series were stationary, data like these would be very unusual. We **reject** stationarity." That is evidence *for* a unit root.
- Together: both point the same way, so **difference once and test the result again.**

<Callout type="pitfall" title="ADF has low power near φ = 1">
Failing to reject a unit root is not proof of one. In 1,000 simulations of an AR(1) with φ = 0.9, which is stationary, the ADF test at the 5% level correctly rejected the unit root only **19%** of the time with 120 months of data, **51%** with 240 months and **84%** with 360 months. Ten years of monthly data is often not enough to tell "very persistent" from "permanent". (The simulation code is in the Instructor kit below.)
</Callout>

## The cure: differencing

If a series has a unit root, model its changes instead. The first <Term def="The change from one period to the next, y(t) − y(t−1). In R: diff(y).">difference</Term> $\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}$ turns a random walk into white noise and a log price level into an inflation rate. Seasonal data may also need a <Term def="The change from the same season a year earlier, y(t) − y(t−12) for monthly data. In R: diff(y, lag = 12).">seasonal difference</Term>, $y_t - y_{t-12}$, which removes a seasonal pattern that repeats but drifts. Try both on real data, and watch the autocorrelation function (ACF) on the right. It shows the correlation between the series and its own past at each lag.

### What differencing removes, and what it costs

Differencing removes **everything that is permanent**: the level, any stochastic trend, and (for a series with drift) the straight-line growth becomes a constant average change. What is left is the month-to-month movement, which is what a stationary model can describe. That is the benefit. The costs are real too:

- **You lose the level.** A model of changes knows nothing about whether the level is high or low. If the series really does return to a mean (an unemployment rate, an interest-rate spread), differencing throws that information away, and the forecast will **never revert**. This is the cost that matters most.
- **You lose observations.** One per regular difference, twelve per seasonal difference of monthly data.
- **You add noise.** A change is the difference of two noisy numbers. Difference a series that did not need it, and its spread *rises* instead of falling. That is the over-differencing signal below.
- **The forecast must be turned back.** Your manager wants the price level or the inflation rate, not "the change in the change in log prices". Session 2 shows how to undo differences and logs.

Robert Nau's notes at Duke give a useful way to read *d* as a forecasting assumption:<sup>[12](#refs)</sup> **d = 0** says the series returns to a mean; **d = 1** says it has no fixed mean, but its average change is constant (the forecast is flat, or a straight line if you add a drift); **d = 2** says even the growth rate wanders (the forecast is a straight line from wherever the recent trend points). Choosing *d* is choosing which of those stories you believe.

### Log first, then difference: growth rates

Most business and economic series grow by percentages: prices, sales, GDP, payrolls. For those, take the **log first** and then difference. The result is (almost exactly) the growth rate, because $\ln(1+g) \approx g$ for small $g$. [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#logs-why-and-when) introduced the idea; here is why it matters for ARIMA.

| What you compute | What it is | Example: core PCE, September 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| `100 * diff(log(p))` | monthly growth rate, % | 0.316 (exact % change: 0.3165) |
| `1200 * diff(log(p))` | same, annualized: "% per year at this month's pace" | 3.79 |
| `100 * diff(log(p), lag = 12)` | 12-month (year-over-year) log growth, % | 3.07 (exact: 3.12) |
| `diff(diff(log(p)))` | change in the monthly growth rate | what ARIMA(0,2,1) models |

<Callout type="empirical" title="Why we model inflation as a log difference">
Three reasons, all visible in the lab below.

1. **It is the inflation rate.** One difference of log prices *is* monthly inflation, so "does the price level need one difference or two?" is the same question as "is inflation stationary?". An ARIMA(0,2,1) on log prices is an ARIMA(0,1,1) on inflation.
2. **Log changes add up.** Twelve monthly log changes sum exactly to the 12-month log change: 3.073 for core PCE through September 2026. To get the familiar percent, take $100\,(e^{3.073/100} - 1) = 3.12\%$, which matches the published year-over-year rate. Percent changes do not add up like this.
3. **A seasonal difference of logs is year-over-year growth.** For seasonal data that are *not* seasonally adjusted, `diff(log(y), lag = 12)` removes the seasonal pattern and leaves the 12-month growth rate. That is why ARIMA models of NSA retail sales so often have D = 1. Seasonally adjusted data usually need no seasonal difference.

Annualize a monthly rate (× 12 for log changes) only to compare it with yearly figures such as the 2% target, and remember that annualizing multiplies the noise by 12 too.
</Callout>

### How to tell you went one difference too far

Over-differencing is easy to do and easy to spot. Two numbers settle it, both printed by the last step of the lab for the unemployment rate (1990–2019):

| | Standard deviation | Lag-1 autocorrelation |
|---|---|---|
| d = 0 (level) | 1.60 | 0.99 |
| d = 1 (monthly change) | **0.15** | 0.12 |
| d = 2 (change in the change) | 0.20 | **−0.57** |

The first difference cuts the spread by a factor of ten and leaves a small positive autocorrelation: it removed the trend. The second difference makes the spread *larger* and produces a lag-1 autocorrelation below −0.5. Both are the fingerprints of over-differencing: you are now modeling noise you created. A practical rule (Nau): **choose the d with the smallest standard deviation, and back off if the lag-1 autocorrelation drops to −0.5 or below.**<sup>[12](#refs)</sup> Mild under-differencing can be patched with an AR term, mild over-differencing with an MA term, but the cleaner fix is the right d.

## Fingerprints: the ACF and PACF

Differencing gives you a stationary series. Next you need to describe its short-run dynamics, and two plots do most of the work. The **ACF** at lag $k$ is the correlation between $y_t$ and $y_{t-k}$. The <Term def="Partial autocorrelation: the correlation between y(t) and y(t−k) after removing the effect of the lags in between. It equals the last coefficient in a regression of y(t) on its first k lags.">PACF</Term> at lag $k$ is the same correlation *after controlling for* the lags in between, so it answers "does lag $k$ add anything new?". Each family of models leaves a distinctive fingerprint in the pair.

Two building blocks are new here. An **AR(p)** model regresses today on the last $p$ values. An <Term def="Moving-average model of order q: today's value is a weighted sum of the current and last q random shocks. Its memory lasts exactly q periods.">MA(q)</Term> model builds today from the last $q$ *shocks*, so its memory lasts exactly $q$ periods. An **ARMA(p, q)** mixes both.

</div>

| Model | ACF | PACF |
|---|---|---|
| AR(p) | Tails off (smoothly or in damped waves) | **Cuts off after lag p** |
| MA(q) | **Cuts off after lag q** | Tails off |
| ARMA(p, q) | Tails off after lag q | Tails off after lag p |

This is the heart of the <Term def="The model-building approach of Box and Jenkins (1970): make the series stationary, identify candidate ARMA orders from the ACF and PACF, estimate, check the residuals, then forecast.">Box–Jenkins method</Term>: make the series stationary, match its ACF and PACF to a fingerprint, estimate a few candidates, check the residuals, then forecast.<sup>[1, 11](#refs)</sup> In practice the sample fingerprints are noisy (the open circles in the gallery), so treat them as a way to shortlist two or three candidates, not to pick one.

<Callout type="math" title="Where the fingerprints come from">
For an **AR(1)**, $\rho_k = \phi^k$: geometric decay. Its PACF is $\phi$ at lag 1 and exactly 0 after, because once you know $y_{t-1}$, older values add nothing.

For an **MA(1)**, $y_t = \varepsilon_t + \theta\varepsilon_{t-1}$, so $y_t$ and $y_{t-1}$ share one shock and $y_t$ and $y_{t-2}$ share none: $\rho_1 = \theta/(1+\theta^2)$ and $\rho_k = 0$ for $k \ge 2$.

In general, any stationary ARMA can be written as $y_t = \sum_{j\ge0} \psi_j \varepsilon_{t-j}$, and then $\rho_k = \sum_j \psi_j\psi_{j+k} / \sum_j \psi_j^2$. That is exactly how the gallery computes its theoretical lollipops; its PACF comes from the Durbin–Levinson recursion, the same algorithm R's `ARMAacf(..., pacf = TRUE)` uses. An AR(2) is stationary only inside the triangle $\phi_1 + \phi_2 < 1$, $\phi_2 - \phi_1 < 1$, $|\phi_2| < 1$.
</Callout>

## ARIMA(p, d, q), decoded

Put the pieces together and you have the <Term def="AutoRegressive Integrated Moving Average model. 'Integrated' means it is fitted to a series differenced d times.">ARIMA</Term> family. Each letter is one decision you have already practiced:

| Letter | Meaning | How you choose it |
|---|---|---|
| **p** | number of AR terms (lags of the series) | PACF cut-off, then AICc |
| **d** | number of differences | unit-root tests, `ndiffs()` |
| **q** | number of MA terms (lags of the shocks) | ACF cut-off, then AICc |

So ARIMA(0,1,1) means "difference once, then fit an MA(1)". Seasonal data add a second set in capitals: **ARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)[m]**, where P, D and Q are the same ideas at the seasonal lag $m$ (12 for monthly data). Read a label from the middle out: **first the differences (d and D), because they say what is being modeled; then the AR and MA terms, which describe the short-run dynamics of that differenced series.**

| Label | What is modeled | In words | What the forecast does |
|---|---|---|---|
| ARIMA(1,0,0) with mean | the level | an AR(1): the gap from the mean shrinks by a fixed share each period | returns to the mean |
| ARIMA(0,1,0) | the change | a random walk | flat at the last value |
| ARIMA(0,1,0) with drift | the change | a random walk with an average change per period | a straight line |
| ARIMA(0,1,1) | the change | simple exponential smoothing (Chapter 6) | flat at a smoothed recent level |
| ARIMA(0,2,1) on log prices | the change in inflation | inflation follows an IMA(1,1) | inflation flat; prices a straight line in logs |
| ARIMA(0,1,1)(0,1,1)[12] on logs | the change in the year-over-year growth rate | the classic "airline" model for seasonal sales | the seasonal pattern and recent trend, carried forward |

In Chapter 0, `auto.arima()` chose ARIMA(2,0,2)(0,1,1)[12] with drift for log retail sales since 2012. In words: one seasonal difference (D = 1), so it models year-over-year growth; no regular difference (d = 0), so that growth rate returns to an average; two AR and two MA terms for the short-run wiggles; one *seasonal* MA term that lets the model learn from last year's same-month surprise; and a drift, which pins down the average growth rate that year-over-year growth returns to.

<Callout type="empirical" title="What 'drift', 'mean' and 'intercept' mean depends on d">
The constant in an ARIMA printout changes meaning with the number of differences. With **d = 0** R calls it `mean` (or `intercept`): the long-run level the series returns to, in the series' own units. With **d = 1** it is called `drift`: the average change per period, so on log core PCE prices the ARIMA(1,1,1) drift of 0.0018 (R rounds it to 0.002) means prices rise about 0.18% per month, or 2.2% per year, on average. With **d = 2** R leaves it out, because a constant there would make the forecast curve like a parabola, which is rarely sensible. Always translate the constant into units before you report it.
</Callout>

`auto.arima()` makes these choices for you, using the Hyndman–Khandakar algorithm.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> It chooses d with repeated KPSS tests, chooses D with a measure of seasonal strength, then searches over p, q, P and Q by AICc, stepping from a simple starting model to its neighbours. It is fast and usually sensible, but it cannot know things you know: that a pandemic distorted two years of data, or that the Fed is committed to a target.

<Callout type="math" title="Backshift notation in one line">
Write $B y_t = y_{t-1}$. A difference is $(1-B)y_t$ and a seasonal difference is $(1-B^{12})y_t$. Then ARIMA(p,d,q) is

$$
(1 - \phi_1 B - \dots - \phi_p B^p)\,(1-B)^d\, y_t = c + (1 + \theta_1 B + \dots + \theta_q B^q)\,\varepsilon_t .
$$

A unit root is literally a root of the AR polynomial at $B = 1$: factor out $(1-B)$ and you have one more difference. The seasonal model multiplies in $(1-\Phi_1B^{12}-\dots)(1-B^{12})^D$ on the left and $(1+\Theta_1B^{12}+\dots)$ on the right.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Never compare AICc across different d">
`auto.arima()` uses AICc to choose p and q **after** fixing d. Differencing changes the data the likelihood is computed on, so an ARIMA(0,1,1) and an ARMA(1,1) for the same series are not on the same AICc scale. To compare models with different d, compare their **out-of-sample forecasts**, which is exactly what the outlook section below does.
</Callout>

<Callout type="business" title="Analyst judgment versus auto.arima">
The two models fit history about equally well, but they disagree about the future. The IMA model says inflation stays wherever it is now, forever. The ARMA model says it drifts back to its 26-year average. The second view is also a statement about policy credibility: it assumes the central bank keeps pulling inflation back toward a stable anchor. Stock and Watson (2007) found that U.S. inflation is well described by an IMA(1,1), equivalently a random-walk trend plus short-lived noise, whose parameters shift over time.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> Whichever you pick, say which one, and why.
</Callout>

## Reading a fitted ARIMA in plain words

An ARIMA printout has four things worth reading aloud. Here they are for the two inflation models from the lab, translated into sentences a manager can use. (Units: annualized monthly core PCE inflation, % per year.)

| Printout | Value | Say it like this |
|---|---|---|
| `ar1` in ARMA(1,1) | 0.962 | "Each month, 96% of the gap between the underlying inflation rate and its average is still there. Half of a surprise has faded after about 18 months." |
| `mean` in ARMA(1,1) | 2.16 | "The model's long-run inflation rate is 2.2% per year, its 2000–2026 average." |
| `ma1` in ARIMA(0,1,1) | −0.818 | "When inflation surprises by 1 point, the model raises its forecast by only 0.18 point. It treats 82% of each surprise as noise that will reverse and 18% as a lasting change." |
| `sigma^2` | 1.64 | "A typical one-month surprise is $\sqrt{1.64} \approx 1.3$ points of annualized inflation, about 0.11 point at the monthly rate." |

**An AR term is about the series' own past.** It says how much of where the series *was* carries into where it *will be*, so read it as persistence and turn it into a half-life. Watch the sign: a negative AR(1) coefficient means the series tends to flip back and forth around its mean (common in inventory changes, rare in prices).

**An MA term is about past surprises.** It says how much of last period's *forecast error* carries into this period. A negative MA coefficient on a differenced series, as here, means the model expects part of each surprise to be undone: a spike in one month's inflation is partly a measurement blip. A positive one means surprises tend to be followed by more of the same, for one period only. Unlike an AR term, an MA(q) term's influence ends completely after q periods.

**Standard errors** are read the usual way: θ = −0.818 with a standard error of 0.033 is far from zero. They matter less here than in a regression, because the question in ARIMA is rarely "is this coefficient zero?" but "does the model forecast well and leave no pattern behind?".

<Callout type="empirical" title="Read the residual check like a mechanic, not a judge">
`checkresiduals()` shows three panels and a test. Read them in this order.

1. **Time plot of residuals.** Look for structure, not size: stretches of all-positive errors, a change in spread (here the residual standard deviation is 0.8 point in 2010–19 and 1.8 points in 2020–22), or single huge months. The two largest residuals in the inflation model are a pair in September and October 2001, a sharp one-month drop in measured core inflation that reversed the next month.
2. **Residual ACF.** With 36 bars and a 95% band, one or two outside the band are expected by chance (36 × 5% ≈ 2). Worry about spikes at meaningful lags: 1, 2, or 12 in monthly data. Here 1 of 36 falls outside.
3. **Histogram.** A roughly bell-shaped center is enough. Fat tails do not bias the point forecast, but they make the normal-theory intervals too narrow in turbulent times.
4. **Ljung–Box test.** Null: "no autocorrelation left". p = 0.27 means no evidence of leftover pattern. A small p-value says "something is left"; look at the residual ACF to see *where*, add an AR or MA term at that lag, or check for a seasonal lag you missed. With very long samples the test flags tiny correlations, so let the forecast accuracy decide whether a fix is worth it.

Passing the check does not make a model *right*. Both inflation models pass it, and they disagree about the future by 0.3 points.
</Callout>

## From differences back to levels

Your model may live in log differences, but your manager asks about **prices** and **12-month inflation**. <Callout type="empirical" title="Undo transformations in reverse order, and check the result">
If you modeled the monthly log change and have a forecast `fc` of the next few log changes:

1. **Undo the difference:** start from the log of the last observed value and add up the forecast changes. In R, `log(last) + cumsum(fc)`.
2. **Undo the log:** take `exp()` of the result, which is the same as `last * exp(cumsum(fc))`. If the changes were in percent, divide by 100 first.
3. **Check one number by hand.** The 12-month rate is `exp()` of the sum of the 12 forecast log changes, minus 1. If your index forecast and this number disagree, a step is wrong.

`forecast()` does steps 1 and 2 for you if you fit the model to the *level* with `d = 1` (and `lambda = 0` for the log), which is why the outlook lab models log prices directly. Two things it cannot fix: exp() of a log forecast is a **median**, not a mean (the gap is small for inflation, larger for volatile series; see [Chapter 10](/chapters/uncertainty/)), and the intervals must be transformed from the log scale bound by bound, never by adding and subtracting a fixed amount in levels.
</Callout>

## Chapter checklist

- **Stationary** series return to a fixed level and have bounded forecast intervals; **unit-root** series do not, and their intervals widen like √h.
- Read an AR coefficient as **persistence**: φ is the share of a gap that survives one period, and the **half-life** is log(0.5)/log(φ). Always state the frequency.
- **Trends invent relationships** (food prices and online sales: R² = 0.96 in levels, 0.00 in growth rates) and decide whether a forecast ever reverts. Difference before you correlate trending series.
- Test with **ADF and KPSS together** and say the result in words: ADF's null is a unit root, KPSS's null is stationarity; "cannot reject" is not "proved". When they conflict, use judgment and out-of-sample forecasts.
- **Difference** to remove a stochastic trend, at the cost of the level and its mean reversion; **log first** so a difference is a growth rate that adds up; **seasonally difference** NSA data (a seasonal log difference is year-over-year growth).
- Spot **over-differencing** by a larger standard deviation and a lag-1 ACF of −0.5 or below.
- Read the **ACF and PACF** to shortlist orders, read a **(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)[m]** label from the differences out, and never compare AICc across different d.
- Say an **MA term** in words (how much of last period's surprise carries over), translate `drift`/`mean` and σ into units, and read **residual checks** panel by panel.
- Turn forecasts back into levels in **reverse order**: running sum to undo the difference, exp() to undo the log, and check the 12-month rate by hand.
- Judge ARIMA against **ETS and a benchmark** on the same rolling-origin errors, and state your unit-root judgment in the memo.

One activity per session. Teams of three.

**Session 1: the "difference or not?" desk (15 minutes).** Each team gets one series from the verdict map: housing starts, consumer sentiment, payroll jobs, the yield spread or the unemployment rate.

1. **Look (3 min).** Plot the level and the first difference in the verdict map. Before reading the tests, each team writes its guess: d = 0 or d = 1?
2. **Test (5 min).** Read the four p-values (level and change), the lag-1 autocorrelation of the change and `ndiffs()`.
3. **Defend (7 min).** Each team writes the one-sentence decision (test, null, result, action) plus one sentence of economics, and reads it out.

*Expected results* (R, `tseries` defaults):

Use the yield spread and payroll jobs to make the point that the tests do not always decide, and the decision has to be defended with economics and out-of-sample forecasts.

**Session 2: the persistence debate (28 minutes).** Split the class into "mean reversion" and "unit root" teams.

1. **Predict (3 min).** Bring back the gut forecasts from session 1.
2. **Build (15 min).** The mean-reversion team runs the outlook lab with `Arima(lp, order = c(1, 1, 1), include.drift = TRUE)` as its headline model; the unit-root team keeps `auto.arima()`. Each team produces a fan chart and a two-sentence memo that states its d in words and gives the half-life (mean reversion) or the share of each surprise treated as permanent (unit root).
3. **Confront (10 min).** Put both memos next to the FOMC's September 2026 median projections (core PCE 3.4% for 2026 and 2.5% for 2027, Q4 over Q4), and use the back-to-levels explorable to show what each view implies for the price level.

*Expected results* (data through September 2026): ARIMA(0,2,1) forecasts **3.1%** for September 2027 with an 80% interval of **2.0% to 4.1%**; ARIMA(1,1,1) with drift forecasts **2.8%** (φ = 0.96, half-life about 18 months; the unit-root model's θ = −0.82 treats 18% of each surprise as permanent). In the back-to-levels explorable, the price index in September 2027 is 135.0 with no mean reversion and 134.7 with an 18-month half-life and a 2% anchor. On the rolling-origin scorecard the 12-month MAE is 0.54 (ARIMA(0,2,1)), 0.56 (ETS), 0.58 (mean-reverting ARIMA) and 0.61 (benchmark). Errors from 2020 onward are more than four times those before 2020. Use the gap between the two teams to discuss why "which d?" is an economic question as much as a statistical one.

**ADF power simulation (for the pitfall box).** In R, with `library(tseries)`: `set.seed(123); for (n in c(120, 240, 360)) print(mean(replicate(1000, suppressWarnings(adf.test(arima.sim(list(ar = 0.9), n))$p.value) < 0.05)))`. *Expected:* 0.189, 0.510 and 0.835. It takes about 5 seconds natively (much longer in the browser), so run it locally.
