## What exactly is a forecast?

A <Term def="A statement about the value of a variable at a future date, made using only the information available at the time the forecast is made, ideally with a measure of uncertainty.">forecast</Term> is more specific than a guess about the future. Every forecast has five parts:

| Part | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Target** | The exact variable, with units | U.S. CPI inflation, % change from a year earlier |
| **Origin** | The date you stand on | Today, with data through September 2026 |
| **Horizon** $h$ | How far ahead | 6 months ahead, so March 2027 |
| **Information set** | Only data released by the origin | CPI, wages, oil prices *as published by today* |
| **Uncertainty** | A point forecast and an interval | 3.1%, with an 80% interval of 2.4% to 3.8% (illustrative) |

Forecasters write this as $\hat y_{T+h \mid T}$: the forecast of $y$ at time $T+h$, made with information up to time $T$.

<KeyIdea>A forecast is a statement about a specific variable at a specific future date, made with only the information available at the origin, and it should come with a range, not only a single number.</KeyIdea>

Two distinctions trip up beginners.

- **A forecast is not a target or a plan.** "We will sell 10,000 units" might be a sales goal. The forecast is what you expect if nothing changes; the plan is what you do about it.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup>
- **Forecasting is not explaining.** To explain *why* unemployment rose, you need causes. To forecast it, any reliable signal will do. Jobless claims do not cause job losses, but they move first, so they might help forecast them (Chapter 8 tests whether they do).

## Three shapes of data

- A <Term def="Data on many units (people, firms, states, products) observed at one point in time.">cross-section</Term> compares many units at **one** time: sales of every store type in 2025.
- A <Term def="Observations of one variable on one unit, recorded at regular intervals over time, in time order.">time series</Term> follows **one** unit over many periods: monthly online sales from 2015 to today. Order matters: you cannot shuffle the rows.
- A <Term def="Data on many units, each observed over many periods. Also called longitudinal data.">panel</Term> holds both: every store type, every year.

Most of this course works with time series. Chapters 12 and 13 return to panels.

## Changing frequency: sum, average, or last value?

Models need every series at the same frequency. The right way to combine observations depends on what the series measures:

| Kind of series | What it measures | Combine by | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| <Term def="A quantity measured over a period of time, such as dollars spent per month. Flows over consecutive periods add up.">Flow</Term> | An amount **during** the period | **Sum** (months of a quarter); **average** weekly flows to compare months | Retail sales, jobless claims |
| <Term def="A quantity measured at a point in time, such as the number of people employed. Adding stocks double-counts.">Stock</Term> | A level **at a point** in time | **Average** or **end of period** | Payroll jobs, inventories |
| Rate or price | A ratio or price at a point in time | **Average** or **end of period** | Interest rates, unemployment rate, CPI |

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Annual rates are already annualized">
Some flows are published at a **seasonally adjusted annual rate** (SAAR): housing starts, vehicle sales, GDP. A monthly SAAR value answers "how many per year, at this month's pace?" For a quarterly value, *average* the three months. Summing them would triple the number.
</Callout>

The explorable starts from a daily rate, the gap between the 10-year and 3-month Treasury yields (`T10Y3M`, in percentage points). Below zero, the yield curve is *inverted*, a classic recession warning that Chapter 5 turns into a recession-probability model.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> Try every rule at every frequency. Then switch to weekly jobless claims, a flow: a month holds four or five weeks, so the monthly sum jumps whenever a month gains a week. To compare months, average the weeks.

## Logs: why and when

The natural log, $\ln y$, does three useful things to a series that grows by percentages:

1. **Steady percentage growth becomes a straight line**, so a bend in the log line is a change in the *growth rate*.
2. **Swings that grow with the level become the same size.** That is why Chapter 2 uses logs for seasonal patterns that grow with sales.
3. **Changes in logs are (almost) percent changes.** This is the one you will use every day.

**How to read it.** A change of **0.01 in a log is about a 1% change** in the level. Multiplied by 100, log changes are "log points", which read like percents for small moves:

| Percent change | Log change × 100 | Gap |
|---|---|---|
| +1% | +0.995 | tiny |
| +5% | +4.88 | 0.1 point |
| +10% | +9.53 | 0.5 point |
| +50% | +40.5 | 9.5 points |
| −50% | −69.3 | 19.3 points |

The shortcut is excellent for monthly and quarterly growth and fails for big moves.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup> Log changes also **add up** (twelve monthly log changes sum exactly to the yearly one) and are **symmetric** (down 0.2 then up 0.2 brings you back). Percent changes have neither property.

**How to convert back.** $y = e^{\ln y}$, and a log change $d$ is a percent change of $100\,(e^{d} - 1)$: `exp()` in R, `np.exp()` in Python.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Read the slope of a log chart as a growth rate">
On a chart of $\ln y$, the slope is the growth rate. U.S. retail sales (excluding food services) rose from \$4.64 trillion in 2015 to \$7.32 trillion in 2025. The log rose by 0.456 over ten years: about **4.6 log points a year**, close to the exact compound rate of 4.7%. Ask "where does the slope change?" rather than "how high is the line?"

<Callout type="pitfall" title="When not to take logs">
- **Zero or negative values** have no log. Profits, net exports and spreads can be negative; even the daily WTI oil price was −\$36.98 on April 20, 2020 (`DCOILWTICO`).
- **Series that are already percents**, such as the unemployment rate: read changes in **percentage points** with a difference ([see below](#differences-change-in-the-original-units)).
</Callout>

<Callout type="math" title="Why log differences are (almost) percent changes">
For a small growth rate $g$, $\ln(1+g) \approx g$. Since $y_t / y_{t-1} = 1 + g_t$,

$$
100\,\Delta \ln y_t = 100 \ln\frac{y_t}{y_{t-1}} = 100\ln(1+g_t) \approx 100\, g_t .
$$

The next term is $-g^2/2$, so the gap grows with the square of the move. Log changes add up because $\ln\frac{y_t}{y_{t-12}} = \sum_{k=0}^{11} \ln\frac{y_{t-k}}{y_{t-k-1}}$, and are symmetric because $\ln\frac{a}{b} = -\ln\frac{b}{a}$.
</Callout>

## Growth rates: MoM, annualized and YoY

A growth rate divides the change by the starting level, so it has no units. Monthly data have three common growth rates:

| Growth rate | Answers | Typical use | Pitfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Month over month** (MoM) | What happened last month? | Latest news, SA data | Noisy; meaningless for NSA data |
| **Annualized** | What if last month's pace lasted a year? | Comparing with yearly targets (the Fed's 2%) | Compounding magnifies noise |
| **Year over year** (YoY) | How much higher than a year ago? | Inflation headlines, NSA data | Slow: it lags turning points |

Annualizing compounds: a monthly rate $m$ becomes $(1+m)^{12} - 1$, so CPI's 0.35% rise in October 2026 is **4.3%** at an annual rate.

**YoY is the last 12 months added up.** In log points, the YoY change is *exactly* the sum of the last 12 monthly changes. That explains its two quirks. It works on seasonal data, because every 12-month window holds exactly one of each month. And it moves for two reasons: when this month's change comes **in**, and when the same month of last year drops **out**. A big month a year ago can move today's YoY rate even if nothing happens now: a <Term def="A change in a year-over-year growth rate caused by an unusual month a year earlier dropping out of the 12-month comparison, rather than by anything happening now.">base effect</Term>.

## Differences: change in the original units

A <Term def="The change from one period to the next in the series' own units: y(t) minus y(t-1). A seasonal difference subtracts the value from a year earlier instead.">difference</Term>, $y_t - y_{t-1}$, answers "how many more?" rather than "what percent more?". The jobs report is quoted this way: payrolls rose by **165,000** in October 2026 (as a growth rate, 0.10%, a number nobody remembers). The year-over-year version, $y_t - y_{t-12}$, is the *seasonal difference*.

Use differences when the unit itself is meaningful and for series that are **already rates**. The unemployment rate fell from 4.2% to 4.1% in October 2026: a change of −0.1 **percentage point**, not "fell 2.4%".

**How to convert back.** Add the changes to a known starting level (`cumsum()` in R and Python). When and why to difference a trending series before modeling is a [Chapter 7](/chapters/arima/) topic.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Percent or percentage points?">
When the thing that changes is itself a percent, say **percentage points** (pp). Inflation fell from 9.0% in June 2022 to 3.7% in September 2026: a drop of **5.3 percentage points**. For interest rates, markets use **basis points**: 1 basis point = 0.01 percentage point, so a quarter-point Fed cut is 25 basis points.
</Callout>

## Seasonally adjusted or not

A <Term def="Seasonally adjusted: the agency has estimated and removed the regular calendar pattern (holidays, weather, trading days), so consecutive months can be compared directly.">seasonally adjusted</Term> (SA) series has the regular calendar pattern removed, so you can compare one month with the next. A <Term def="Not seasonally adjusted: the raw data, including the regular calendar pattern.">not seasonally adjusted</Term> (NSA) series is the raw count, December spike and all. Chapter 2 shows how the adjustment is done.

| You have | You can | Do not |
|---|---|---|
| **SA** data | Compare consecutive months (MoM), annualize | Forget that SA data are revised every year |
| **NSA** data | Compare with the same month a year earlier (YoY) | Compare consecutive months or annualize them |

The lede's headline is the textbook case. In December 2025, NSA retail sales rose **11.8%** from November; the SA series (`RSAFS`, which also includes restaurants) rose just **0.1%**. The SA number asks whether this December beat a *normal* December.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Never mix an SA number with an NSA number">
Before you compare or divide two series, check that both are SA or both NSA. FRED IDs often give it away: `RSXFSN` ends in **N** for NSA, `MRTSSM4541USS` in **S** for SA. When only NSA data exist, use YoY comparisons.
</Callout>

## Real vs. nominal: more stuff, or higher prices?

Retail sales are measured in current dollars: <Term def="Measured in the prices of the time. Nominal growth mixes changes in quantity with changes in price.">nominal</Term> values. When prices rise 30%, nominal sales rise even if people buy exactly the same things. Dividing by a price index gives a <Term def="Adjusted for inflation by dividing by a price index; measured in the prices of a fixed base period.">real</Term> (or *deflated*) series. Guess first.

<div class="breakout">
<RealNominal client:load />
</div>

From the 2019 average to September 2026, nominal retail and food services sales grew 47.4% and consumer prices 31.5%, so real sales grew only **12.0%**, not the 15.8 points that subtraction suggests. Growth rates combine by division: $1.474 / 1.315 \approx 1.12$. Subtracting is a fine shortcut only for one year of low inflation.

**How to read and convert.** Real = nominal ÷ price index × 100 gives values in the dollars of the index's base period ("1982–84 dollars" for CPI). To restate in **today's dollars**, multiply each month by (CPI today ÷ CPI that month). To go back to nominal, multiply by the index and divide by 100.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Say which dollars, and match the deflator to the spending">
A real number needs its base: "in October-2026 dollars" or "in chained 2017 dollars" (BEA's real GDP, `GDPC1`). Use CPI for consumer spending, a producer price index for business inputs, the GDP deflator for the whole economy. Deflate *before* comparing across years.
</Callout>

## Transformation decision guide

Find your question in the left column.

| Your question | Transformation | Read the result as | Back to original units |
|---|---|---|---|
| How big is it? | **Level** | "\$745 billion a month (SA)" | — |
| How many more than last period? | **Difference** | "payrolls +165,000"; "−0.1 percentage point" | `cumsum` from a starting level |
| Is growth steady? | **Log** | Slope 0.01 per period ≈ 1% growth | `exp` |
| What happened last month? (SA only) | **MoM %** | "+0.35% from September" | $y_t = y_{t-1}(1 + m)$ |
| What if this pace lasted a year? (SA only) | **Annualized** $(1+m)^{12} - 1$ | "4.3% at an annual rate" | $m = (1+a)^{1/12} - 1$ |
| How much higher than a year ago? | **YoY %** | "+3.7% from a year earlier" | $y_t = y_{t-12}(1 + g)$ |
| More stuff, or higher prices? | **Real** = nominal ÷ price index × 100 | "in 1982–84 dollars" | nominal = real × index ÷ 100 |
| Is the average person better off? | **Per capita** = total ÷ population | "\$26,000 per person per year" | × population |
| Compare series in different units | **Index** $100\,y_t / y_{\text{base}}$ | "105 = 5% above 2019" | × $y_{\text{base}} / 100$ |
| Is this month normal for the season? | **SA** series, or YoY | "SA, at an annual rate" | Use the NSA series |

**Order of operations:** fix units and frequency → deflate → divide by population → handle seasonality → take logs → difference or compute growth → convert back and label.

## Chapter checklist

- A forecast has a **target**, an **origin**, a **horizon**, an **information set** and an **uncertainty range**. It is not a goal and not an explanation.
- Store series with their dates (`ts` or a `DatetimeIndex`); keep missing months as `NA`.
- When changing frequency, **sum flows**, **average or take the last value of stocks and rates**, and never sum a SAAR series.
- **Pick the question, then the transformation**, and label every transformed number.
- **Logs**: a 0.01 change ≈ 1%; the shortcut fails for big moves; convert back with `exp()`. No logs of zero, negatives or rates.
- **Growth**: MoM and annualized only for **SA** data; **YoY** works for NSA data but lags turning points and has **base effects**.
- **Differences** for "how many more" and for rates (in **percentage points**). **Real** = nominal ÷ price index; growth rates combine by **division**.

#### Data get revised: vintages and ALFRED

The numbers you download today are not necessarily the numbers that were published at the time. Statistical agencies release a first estimate fast, then revise it as more complete source data arrive.

  explain="The second estimate, one month later, was already −6.2%. />

For GDP, the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) publishes an *advance* estimate roughly four weeks after the quarter ends, then a second and a third estimate as more data arrive, then annual updates each summer and comprehensive updates about every five years.<sup>[8, 9](#refs)</sup> Seasonally adjusted CPI is revised too: each February, BLS recomputes its seasonal factors and revises the previous five years of seasonally adjusted data.<sup>[10](#refs)</sup>

Each published version of a series is called a <Term def="The version of a data series as it was published on a particular date. Real-time forecast evaluation uses the vintage that was available at each forecast origin.">vintage</Term>. The St. Louis Fed keeps every vintage of many FRED series in <Term def="Archival FRED: the St. Louis Fed's archive of past vintages of FRED series, at alfred.stlouisfed.org.">ALFRED</Term>.<sup>[11](#refs)</sup>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Revisions are a hidden form of look-ahead leakage">
If you test a forecasting model on today's revised data, you give it cleaner numbers than any forecaster had at the time. For a fair "how would this have done in 2009?" test, use the vintage available at each forecast origin. Chapter 3 will show how leakage flatters a model, and Chapter 8 will run a pseudo out-of-sample test and explain what a fully real-time test with ALFRED vintages would add.
</Callout>

#### Annualizing in detail

**Converting between rates.** A monthly rate $m$ annualizes to $(1+m)^{12} - 1$. To go back, take the 12th root: an annual rate $a$ is a monthly rate of $(1+a)^{1/12} - 1$. Quarterly data use the 4th power and the 4th root. Multiplying by 12 (or 4) is close but always a little too low for positive growth, because it ignores compounding.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Headline GDP growth is an annualized quarterly rate">
When BEA reports that real GDP grew 3.4% in the third quarter of 2026 (the bundled `A191RL1Q225SBEA`), that is **not** how much GDP grew in the quarter. GDP grew 0.83% from the second quarter; 3.4% is that quarterly rate compounded over four quarters, $(1.0083)^4 - 1$.<sup>[12](#refs)</sup> Compared with a year earlier, GDP was up only 2.1%. All three numbers are correct. Before you compare a GDP growth rate with anything else (a company's sales growth, another country's GDP, a forecast), check that both are on the same basis: quarterly, annualized or year over year. You can reproduce BEA's 3.4% in the Extra practice below.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="YoY growth is late at turning points">
Because YoY growth averages a whole year of monthly changes, it reacts slowly. When a series turns down, YoY growth keeps looking healthy for months. Analysts who need an early read look at the annualized change over the last three or six months of SA data, a compromise between noise and speed.
</Callout>

#### Per capita: adjusting for population

A country, a state or a customer base that grows will have rising totals even if nobody is better off. Dividing by population gives a <Term def="Per person: a total divided by the population. It removes growth that comes only from having more people.">per-capita</Term> number, which answers "is the *average person* getting more?" Hyndman and Athanasopoulos list population and inflation adjustments among the standard ways to remove a known source of variation before modeling.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup>

<Predict client:load
  question="U.S. real GDP grew **2.6%** from the third quarter of 2024 to the third quarter of 2025. The resident population grew from 340.0 million to 341.8 million over about the same year. How fast did real GDP **per person** grow?"
  options={['3.1%', '2.6%: population does not matter', 'About 2.1%', '0.5%']}
  answer={2}
  explain="Population grew about 0.5%, so real GDP per person grew about 1.026 / 1.005 − 1 ≈ **2.1%**. As with real and nominal, growth rates divide; for small rates, subtracting (2.6 − 0.5) is a fine shortcut."
/>

The Census Bureau estimates the U.S. population once a year (July 1); its January 2026 release put it at 341.8 million, up 0.5%, the slowest growth since the pandemic.<sup>[13](#refs)</sup> The same idea works with any denominator that matches the question: sales per store, claims per 1,000 workers, Iowa's share of U.S. jobs. **To convert back**, multiply the per-capita value by the population.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Per capita is not always the right denominator">
Per-capita GDP measures average output per resident, not the typical person's income or how it is shared. Use per-worker or per-hour figures for productivity, per-household for housing demand, and per-store for comparing chains. The denominator *is* the question.
</Callout>

#### Index numbers and rebasing

An <Term def="A series divided by its value in a base period and multiplied by 100. 112 means 12% above the base period.">index number</Term> divides a series by its value in a base period and multiplies by 100: $\text{index}_t = 100 \times y_t / y_{\text{base}}$. Many official series are published only as indexes: CPI (1982–84 = 100), industrial production (2017 = 100). Index *levels* have no meaning on their own. A CPI of 336.3 does not mean prices are \$336; it means prices are 236% higher than in 1982–84. Only **ratios** of index values carry information.

<Predict client:load kind="check"
  question="CPI rose from 324.2 in September 2025 to 336.3 in September 2026, a rise of **12.1 index points**. What was the inflation rate?"
  options={['12.1%', '3.7%', '1.2%']}

**Rebasing** moves the base period: divide the whole index by its value in the new base period and multiply by 100. Rebasing changes every index level, but never a growth rate between two dates. That makes the choice of base year a presentation decision, and a powerful one, as the next explorable shows with jobs in Iowa and the whole United States.

#### Units and scaling

Every bundled series has units, and getting them wrong by a factor of 1,000 is the most common error in student (and professional) work. Retail sales are in **millions** of dollars, payrolls in **thousands** of jobs, GDP in **billions** of chained 2017 dollars at an annual rate, housing starts in **thousands** of units at an annual rate, and CPI is an index.

- **Rescaling** (dividing by 1,000, reporting in billions) changes no information, only the labels. It does change a regression coefficient by the same factor, so the units of the coefficient must always be stated; [Chapter 4](/chapters/regression/) shows how.
- **Pick units your reader can picture**: "\$745 billion a month" beats "745,021 (millions)".
- **Logs and growth rates are unit-free**, which is one reason forecasters like them: switching from millions to billions shifts $\ln y$ by a constant and leaves every log change untouched.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Sanity-check a magnitude in one line">
Before you report any number, check it against something you know. Seasonally adjusted retail and food services sales were \$745 billion in September 2026 (`RSAFS` = 745,021, in millions). Times 12 months is about \$8.9 trillion a year. Divided by 341.8 million people, that is roughly **\$26,000 per person per year**, or about \$2,200 a month. Plausible. If your calculation had said \$26 or \$26 million, you would know a unit went wrong before your manager does.
</Callout>
