## What exactly is a forecast?

A <Term def="A statement about the value of a variable at a future date, made using only the information available at the time the forecast is made, ideally with a measure of uncertainty.">forecast</Term> is more specific than a guess about the future. Every forecast has five parts:

| Part | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Target** | The exact variable being forecast, with units | U.S. CPI inflation, % change from a year earlier |
| **Origin** | The date you stand on when you make it | Today, with data through September 2026 |
| **Horizon** $h$ | How far ahead | 6 months ahead, so March 2027 |
| **Information set** | Everything you are allowed to use: data released by the origin, nothing later | CPI, wages, oil prices *as published by today* |
| **Uncertainty** | A point forecast (one number) and an interval (a range) | 3.1%, with an 80% interval of 2.4% to 3.8% (illustrative numbers) |

Forecasters write this compactly as $\hat y_{T+h \mid T}$: the forecast of $y$ at time $T+h$, made with information up to time $T$. The hat means "estimate", the bar means "given what we know at".

<KeyIdea>A forecast is a statement about a specific variable at a specific future date, made with only the information available at the origin, and it should come with a range, not only a single number.</KeyIdea>

Two distinctions trip up beginners.

- **A forecast is not a target or a plan.** "We will sell 10,000 units" might be a sales goal. The forecast is what you expect to happen if nothing changes; the plan is what you decide to do about it.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup>
- **Forecasting is not explaining.** To explain *why* unemployment rose, you need causes. To forecast it, any reliable signal will do, even one that causes nothing. Jobless claims do not cause unemployment, but they move first, so they help forecast it (you will test exactly this in Chapter 8).

## Three shapes of data

Economic data come in three shapes. The difference decides which methods you can use.

- A <Term def="Data on many units (people, firms, states, products) observed at one point in time.">cross-section</Term> compares many units at **one** time: sales of every store type in 2025.
- A <Term def="Observations of one variable on one unit, recorded at regular intervals over time, in time order.">time series</Term> follows **one** unit over many periods: monthly online sales from 2015 to today. Order matters: you cannot shuffle the rows.
- A <Term def="Data on many units, each observed over many periods. Also called longitudinal data.">panel</Term> holds both: every store type, every year. A panel is a stack of time series, or equivalently a sequence of cross-sections.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The month that does not exist">
The fifth step of the lab finds a hole: there is **no unemployment rate and no CPI for October 2025**. The federal government shut down from October 1 to November 12, 2025. The household survey behind the unemployment rate was not collected that month and was not collected afterwards, and BLS could not publish most October 2025 price indexes either.<sup>[2, 3](#refs)</sup> The gap is permanent.

Missing values must stay in place as `NA`. A `ts` object assumes evenly spaced observations. If you delete the blank row, every later value slides one month earlier. Nothing errors, but every month after September 2025 is now labelled wrong, and a year-over-year change computed from it silently compares months 13 apart.

The course helper `fred()` takes a middle road. It keeps every month on the calendar, but it fills a gap of one to three months by *linear interpolation* (the average of the neighbors) and prints a **Note** saying so, because most models cannot run on `NA`. That is fine for fitting a model. It is not fine for reporting a number: an October 2026 inflation rate built on an interpolated October 2025 divides by a price level nobody measured. BLS will not publish that 12-month change for the same reason.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> Whenever `fred()` prints a Note, ask whether the number you are about to quote depends on the filled month.
</Callout>

## Changing frequency: sum, average, or last value?

Models need every series at the same frequency. Often that means converting daily or weekly data to monthly, or monthly data to quarterly. The right way to combine the observations depends on what the series measures:

| Kind of series | What it measures | Combine by | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| <Term def="A quantity measured over a period of time, such as dollars spent per month. Flows over consecutive periods add up.">Flow</Term> | An amount **during** the period | **Sum** (or the average, for a per-period rate) | Retail sales, jobless claims, new orders |
| <Term def="A quantity measured at a point in time, such as the number of people employed. Adding stocks double-counts.">Stock</Term> | A level **at a point** in time | **Average** or **end of period** | Payroll jobs, money supply, inventories |
| Rate or price | A ratio or price at a point in time | **Average** or **end of period** | Interest rates, unemployment rate, CPI |

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Annual rates are already annualized">
Some flows are published at a **seasonally adjusted annual rate** (SAAR): housing starts, vehicle sales, GDP. A monthly SAAR value answers "how many per year, if this month's pace lasted twelve months?" To get a quarterly SAAR value, *average* the three months. Summing them would triple the number.
</Callout>

The explorable below starts from a daily rate, the gap between the 10-year and 3-month Treasury yields (`T10Y3M`, in percentage points). When it drops below zero the yield curve is *inverted*, a classic recession warning that has preceded each U.S. recession since the 1950s.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> Try every rule at every frequency. Then switch to weekly jobless claims, a flow, where the sum is the right answer.

## Transformations every analyst uses

Almost nobody forecasts the raw level of a series without first looking at it in other forms. Each transformation answers a different question. Switch between them below and watch the same data tell six different stories.

| View | Formula | Answers | Use it when… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Level** | $y_t$ | How big is it? | You need the actual quantity (dollars, people) |
| **Difference** | $y_t - y_{t-1}$ | How much did it change, in its own units? | Changes are what matter: "payrolls rose by 120,000" |
| **Log** | $\ln y_t$ | Is growth steady? | A series grows by percentages; makes growth linear and seasonal swings proportional |
| **MoM %** | $100\left(\frac{y_t}{y_{t-1}} - 1\right)$ | What happened last month? | The data are seasonally adjusted |
| **YoY %** | $100\left(\frac{y_t}{y_{t-12}} - 1\right)$ | How fast is it growing over a year? | Always safe, even for NSA data; the standard for inflation |
| **Annualized** | $100\left[\left(\frac{y_t}{y_{t-1}}\right)^{12} - 1\right]$ | What if last month's pace lasted a year? | SA data only; comparing with yearly figures such as GDP growth |
| **Index** | $100\,\frac{y_t}{\bar y_{2019}}$ | How does it compare with a base period? | Comparing series in different units on one chart |

<Callout type="math" title="Why log differences are (almost) percent changes">
For a small growth rate $g$, $\ln(1+g) \approx g$. Since $y_t / y_{t-1} = 1 + g_t$,

$$
100\,\Delta \ln y_t = 100 \ln\frac{y_t}{y_{t-1}} = 100\ln(1+g_t) \approx 100\, g_t .
$$

The approximation is excellent for moves of a few percent and poor for big ones. In the lab, January 2026 retail sales fell 19.2%, while the log difference says −21.3. Log differences have one big advantage: they add up. The log change over a year is exactly the sum of the twelve monthly log changes, which is why models are usually written in logs.
</Callout>

## Real vs. nominal: more stuff, or higher prices?

Retail sales are measured in current dollars. These are <Term def="Measured in the prices of the time. Nominal growth mixes changes in quantity with changes in price.">nominal</Term> values. When prices rise 30%, nominal sales rise even if people buy exactly the same things. To measure the quantity of goods, you divide by a price index. The result is a <Term def="Adjusted for inflation by dividing by a price index; measured in the prices of a fixed base period.">real</Term> series, also called a *deflated* series. Guess first.

From the 2019 average to September 2026, nominal retail and food services sales grew 47.4%. Consumer prices grew 31.5% over the same span. Real sales grew only **12.0%**. Notice that subtracting gives about 15.8 points (the lab subtracts the unrounded rates), not 12.0. Growth rates combine by division, not subtraction: $1.474 / 1.315 \approx 1.12$.

With inflation running at 3–4%, a company whose nominal sales grew 3% sold *fewer* units than last year. Annual reports, budgets and wage negotiations all hinge on this. Pick the deflator that matches what is being bought: CPI for consumer spending, a producer price index for business inputs, the GDP deflator for the whole economy.
</Callout>

## Data get revised: vintages and ALFRED

The numbers you download today are not necessarily the numbers that were published at the time. Statistical agencies release a first estimate fast, then revise it as more complete source data arrive.

<Predict client:load
  question="On January 30, 2009, the first official estimate said U.S. real GDP shrank at a **3.8%** annual rate in the fourth quarter of 2008. What does today's data say?"
  options={['−3.8%: GDP is measured once and stays fixed', '−2.0%: it was revised up once the panic passed', '−6.2%', '−8.5%']}
  answer={3}
  explain="The second estimate, one month later, was already −6.2%. After annual and comprehensive revisions, the bundled data (`A191RL1Q225SBEA`) now show **−8.5%**: the worst quarter of the Great Recession was more than twice as bad as first reported. A forecaster in early 2009 was working from much rosier numbers than the ones we see today."
/>

For GDP, the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) publishes an *advance* estimate roughly four weeks after the quarter ends, then a second and a third estimate as more data arrive, then annual updates each summer and comprehensive updates about every five years.<sup>[5, 6](#refs)</sup> Seasonally adjusted CPI is revised too: each February, BLS recomputes its seasonal factors and revises the previous five years of seasonally adjusted data.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup>

Each published version of a series is called a <Term def="The version of a data series as it was published on a particular date. Real-time forecast evaluation uses the vintage that was available at each forecast origin.">vintage</Term>. The St. Louis Fed keeps every vintage of many FRED series in <Term def="Archival FRED: the St. Louis Fed's archive of past vintages of FRED series, at alfred.stlouisfed.org.">ALFRED</Term>.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Revisions are a hidden form of look-ahead leakage">
If you test a forecasting model on today's revised data, you give it cleaner numbers than any forecaster had at the time. For a fair "how would this have done in 2009?" test, use the vintage available at each forecast origin. Chapter 3 shows how leakage flatters a model; Chapter 8 runs a real-time out-of-sample test.
</Callout>

```python
# Python: the same CSV, straight into a Series with a DatetimeIndex
import pandas as pd
url = "https://fred.stlouisfed.org/graph/fredgraph.csv?id=UNRATE"
unrate = pd.read_csv(url, index_col=0, parse_dates=True)["UNRATE"]
```

The file you get is exactly the format you opened in the first lab: a date column and a value column, with blanks where data are missing.<sup>[10](#refs)</sup>

## Chapter checklist

- A forecast has a **target**, an **origin**, a **horizon**, an **information set** and an **uncertainty range**. Forecasting is not explaining, and it is not goal-setting.
- Know your data's shape: **cross-section**, **time series** or **panel**.
- Store series with their dates (`ts` or a `DatetimeIndex`). Keep missing months as `NA`; never delete them.
- When changing frequency, **sum flows**, **average or take the last value of stocks and rates**, and never sum a SAAR series.
- Use **YoY** for NSA data, **MoM** and **annualized** rates only for SA data, **logs** for growth, and an **index** to compare units.
- **Deflate** nominal values by a price index to get real quantities. Growth rates combine by division, not subtraction.
- Data are **revised**. Test forecasts on the vintage that was available at the time (ALFRED).
