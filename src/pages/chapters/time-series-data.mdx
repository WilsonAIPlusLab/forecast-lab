## What exactly is a forecast?

A <Term def="A statement about the value of a variable at a future date, made using only the information available at the time the forecast is made, ideally with a measure of uncertainty.">forecast</Term> is more specific than a guess about the future. Every forecast has five parts:

| Part | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Target** | The exact variable being forecast, with units | U.S. CPI inflation, % change from a year earlier |
| **Origin** | The date you stand on when you make it | Today, with data through September 2026 |
| **Horizon** $h$ | How far ahead | 6 months ahead, so March 2027 |
| **Information set** | Everything you are allowed to use: data released by the origin, nothing later | CPI, wages, oil prices *as published by today* |
| **Uncertainty** | A point forecast (one number) and an interval (a range) | 3.1%, with an 80% interval of 2.4% to 3.8% (illustrative numbers) |

Forecasters write this compactly as $\hat y_{T+h \mid T}$: the forecast of $y$ at time $T+h$, made with information up to time $T$. The hat means "estimate", the bar means "given what we know at".

<KeyIdea>A forecast is a statement about a specific variable at a specific future date, made with only the information available at the origin, and it should come with a range, not only a single number.</KeyIdea>

Two distinctions trip up beginners.

- **A forecast is not a target or a plan.** "We will sell 10,000 units" might be a sales goal. The forecast is what you expect to happen if nothing changes; the plan is what you decide to do about it.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup>
- **Forecasting is not explaining.** To explain *why* unemployment rose, you need causes. To forecast it, any reliable signal will do, even one that causes nothing. Jobless claims do not cause job losses, but they move first, so they might help forecast them (Chapter 8 tests whether they actually do).

## Three shapes of data

Economic data come in three shapes. The difference decides which methods you can use.

- A <Term def="Data on many units (people, firms, states, products) observed at one point in time.">cross-section</Term> compares many units at **one** time: sales of every store type in 2025.
- A <Term def="Observations of one variable on one unit, recorded at regular intervals over time, in time order.">time series</Term> follows **one** unit over many periods: monthly online sales from 2015 to today. Order matters: you cannot shuffle the rows.
- A <Term def="Data on many units, each observed over many periods. Also called longitudinal data.">panel</Term> holds both: every store type, every year. A panel is a stack of time series, or equivalently a sequence of cross-sections.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The month that does not exist">
The fifth step of the lab finds a hole: there is **no unemployment rate and no CPI for October 2025**. The federal government shut down from October 1 to November 12, 2025. The household survey behind the unemployment rate was not collected that month and was not collected afterwards, and BLS could not publish most October 2025 price indexes either.<sup>[2, 3](#refs)</sup> The gap is permanent.

Missing values must stay in place as `NA`. A `ts` object assumes evenly spaced observations. If you delete the blank row, every later value slides one month earlier. Nothing errors, but every month after September 2025 is now labelled wrong, and a year-over-year change computed from it silently compares months 13 apart.

The course helper `fred()` takes a middle road. It keeps every month on the calendar, but it fills a gap of one or two months by *linear interpolation* (a straight line between the neighbors; for one month, their average) and prints a **Note** saying so, because most models cannot run on `NA`. That is fine for fitting a model. It is not fine for reporting a number: an October 2026 inflation rate built on an interpolated October 2025 divides by a price level nobody measured. BLS will not publish that 12-month change for the same reason.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> Whenever `fred()` prints a Note, ask whether the number you are about to quote depends on the filled month.
</Callout>

## Changing frequency: sum, average, or last value?

Models need every series at the same frequency. Often that means converting daily or weekly data to monthly, or monthly data to quarterly. The right way to combine the observations depends on what the series measures:

| Kind of series | What it measures | Combine by | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| <Term def="A quantity measured over a period of time, such as dollars spent per month. Flows over consecutive periods add up.">Flow</Term> | An amount **during** the period | **Sum**, when every period has the same coverage (months of a quarter); **average** weekly flows when comparing months | Retail sales, jobless claims, new orders |
| <Term def="A quantity measured at a point in time, such as the number of people employed. Adding stocks double-counts.">Stock</Term> | A level **at a point** in time | **Average** or **end of period** | Payroll jobs, money supply, inventories |
| Rate or price | A ratio or price at a point in time | **Average** or **end of period** | Interest rates, unemployment rate, CPI |

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Annual rates are already annualized">
Some flows are published at a **seasonally adjusted annual rate** (SAAR): housing starts, vehicle sales, GDP. A monthly SAAR value answers "how many per year, if this month's pace lasted twelve months?" To get a quarterly SAAR value, *average* the three months. Summing them would triple the number.
</Callout>

The explorable below starts from a daily rate, the gap between the 10-year and 3-month Treasury yields (`T10Y3M`, in percentage points). When it drops below zero the yield curve is *inverted*, a classic recession warning: it turned negative before every U.S. recession since 1969 except one, when it only flattened. Chapter 5 will turn it into a recession-probability model.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> Try every rule at every frequency. Then switch to weekly jobless claims, a flow. The sum gives each month's total, but a month holds four or five weeks, so the sum jumps whenever a month gains a week. To compare months, average the weeks (Chapters 8 and 13 come back to this five-week trap).

## Data get revised: vintages and ALFRED

The numbers you download today are not necessarily the numbers that were published at the time. Statistical agencies release a first estimate fast, then revise it as more complete source data arrive.

For GDP, the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) publishes an *advance* estimate roughly four weeks after the quarter ends, then a second and a third estimate as more data arrive, then annual updates each summer and comprehensive updates about every five years.<sup>[5, 6](#refs)</sup> Seasonally adjusted CPI is revised too: each February, BLS recomputes its seasonal factors and revises the previous five years of seasonally adjusted data.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup>

Each published version of a series is called a <Term def="The version of a data series as it was published on a particular date. Real-time forecast evaluation uses the vintage that was available at each forecast origin.">vintage</Term>. The St. Louis Fed keeps every vintage of many FRED series in <Term def="Archival FRED: the St. Louis Fed's archive of past vintages of FRED series, at alfred.stlouisfed.org.">ALFRED</Term>.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Revisions are a hidden form of look-ahead leakage">
If you test a forecasting model on today's revised data, you give it cleaner numbers than any forecaster had at the time. For a fair "how would this have done in 2009?" test, use the vintage available at each forecast origin. Chapter 3 will show how leakage flatters a model, and Chapter 8 will run a pseudo out-of-sample test and explain what a fully real-time test with ALFRED vintages would add.
</Callout>

| View | Formula | Answers | Use it when… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Level** | $y_t$ | How big is it? | You need the actual quantity (dollars, people) |
| **Difference** | $y_t - y_{t-1}$ | How much did it change, in its own units? | Changes are what matter: "payrolls rose by 165,000" |
| **Log** | $\ln y_t$ | Is growth steady? | A series grows by percentages; makes growth linear and seasonal swings proportional |
| **MoM %** | $100\left(\frac{y_t}{y_{t-1}} - 1\right)$ | What happened last month? | The data are seasonally adjusted |
| **YoY %** | $100\left(\frac{y_t}{y_{t-12}} - 1\right)$ | How fast is it growing over a year? | Always safe, even for NSA data; the standard for inflation |
| **Annualized** | $100\left[\left(\frac{y_t}{y_{t-1}}\right)^{12} - 1\right]$ | What if last month's pace lasted a year? | SA data only; comparing with yearly figures such as GDP growth |
| **Index** | $100\,\frac{y_t}{\bar y_{2019}}$ | How does it compare with a base period? | Comparing series in different units on one chart |

## Logs: why and when

The natural log, $\ln y$, does three useful things to a series that grows by percentages, as most economic series do:

1. **Steady percentage growth becomes a straight line.** A series growing 5% a year curves upward in levels, because 5% of a bigger number is more dollars. In logs it is a straight line, so a bend in the log line is a change in the *growth rate*.
2. **Swings that grow with the level become the same size.** A December spike that is always about 15% of sales looks bigger every year in dollars, and the same size in logs. That is why Chapter 2 uses logs to turn multiplicative seasonality into additive seasonality.
3. **Changes in logs are (almost) percent changes.** This is the one you will use every day.

**How to read it.** A change of **0.01 in a log is about a 1% change** in the level. Multiply log changes by 100 and you get "log points", which read like percents for small moves:

| Percent change | Log change × 100 | Gap |
|---|---|---|
| +1% | +0.995 | tiny |
| +5% | +4.88 | 0.1 point |
| +10% | +9.53 | 0.5 point |
| +50% | +40.5 | 9.5 points |
| −50% | −69.3 | 19.3 points |

The shortcut is excellent for monthly and quarterly growth, which is usually a few percent at most, and fails for big moves.<sup>[11](#refs)</sup> Two more properties make logs the forecaster's favorite units. Log changes **add up**: twelve monthly log changes sum exactly to the yearly log change. And log changes are **symmetric**: down 0.2 then up 0.2 brings you back to where you started. Percent changes have neither property.

**How to convert back.** The log undoes with the exponential: $y = e^{\ln y}$. A log change $d$ is a percent change of $100\,(e^{d} - 1)$. In R that is `exp()`; in Python, `np.exp()`.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Read the slope of a log chart as a growth rate">
On a chart of $\ln y$, the slope is the growth rate. U.S. retail sales (excluding food services) totaled \$4.64 trillion in 2015 and \$7.32 trillion in 2025. The log rose by 0.456 over ten years, so sales grew about **4.6 log points a year**, close to the exact compound rate of 4.7% a year (nominal, so part of it is inflation). On the TransformLab's log view for CPI, the line climbs about 0.021 a year from 2000 to 2020, or roughly 2.1% inflation a year, and then visibly steepens in 2021–22. When you see a log chart, ask "where does the slope change?" rather than "how high is the line?"
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="When not to take logs">
- **Zero or negative values.** The log of zero or a negative number does not exist. Net exports, profits, interest-rate spreads and temperature can all be negative. Even oil can: on April 20, 2020, the daily WTI spot price in the bundled data (`DCOILWTICO`) was −\$36.98 a barrel. The monthly average stayed positive, which is why the explorable above uses monthly data.
- **Series that are already percents.** The unemployment rate or an interest rate is already a ratio. Its change is best read in **percentage points** ("unemployment fell from 4.2% to 4.1%"), so use a difference, not a log ([see below](#differences-change-in-the-original-units)).
- **Short, flat series** with no growth and constant swings gain little from logs, and the result is harder to explain.
</Callout>

<Callout type="math" title="Why log differences are (almost) percent changes">
For a small growth rate $g$, $\ln(1+g) \approx g$. Since $y_t / y_{t-1} = 1 + g_t$,

$$
100\,\Delta \ln y_t = 100 \ln\frac{y_t}{y_{t-1}} = 100\ln(1+g_t) \approx 100\, g_t .
$$

The next term of the expansion is $-g^2/2$, which is why the gap grows with the square of the move: for $g = 0.10$ it is about $0.005$. In the lab, January 2026 retail sales fell 19.2%, while the log difference says −21.3.

Log changes add up because $\ln\frac{y_t}{y_{t-12}} = \sum_{k=0}^{11} \ln\frac{y_{t-k}}{y_{t-k-1}}$, and they are symmetric because $\ln\frac{a}{b} = -\ln\frac{b}{a}$. A straight line in logs, $\ln y_t = a + b\,t$, means $y_t = e^{a} e^{b t}$: constant growth of $100\,(e^{b} - 1)\%$ per period.
</Callout>

How a log changes the meaning of a regression coefficient (the log-level and log-log models) is the subject of [Chapter 4](/chapters/regression/). Turning a forecast made in logs back into dollars, and why the result is a median, is covered in [Chapter 10](/chapters/uncertainty/).

## Growth rates: MoM, annualized and YoY

A growth rate divides the change by the starting level, so it has no units: a 1% rise means the same thing for a corner store and for Walmart. There are three common growth rates for monthly data, and each answers a different question.

| Growth rate | Answers | Typical use | Pitfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Month over month** (MoM) | What happened last month? | Latest news, SA data | Noisy; meaningless for NSA data |
| **Annualized** | What if last month's pace lasted a year? | Comparing with yearly targets (the Fed's 2%) | Compounding magnifies noise |
| **Year over year** (YoY) | How much higher than a year ago? | Inflation headlines, NSA data | Slow: it lags turning points |

**Converting between them.** A monthly rate $m$ annualizes to $(1+m)^{12} - 1$: CPI rose 0.35% in October 2026, which annualizes to **4.3%**. To go back, take the 12th root: an annual rate $a$ is a monthly rate of $(1+a)^{1/12} - 1$. Quarterly data use the 4th power and the 4th root. Multiplying by 12 (or 4) is close but always a little too low for positive growth, because it ignores compounding.

**YoY is the last 12 months added up.** In log points, the year-over-year change is *exactly* the sum of the last 12 monthly changes. That one fact explains the two quirks of YoY growth. First, it works on seasonal data: every 12-month window contains exactly one January and one December, so the seasonal swings cancel. Second, it moves for two reasons: when this month's change comes **in**, and when the same month of last year drops **out**. A big month a year ago can move today's YoY rate even if nothing happens this month. Economists call that a <Term def="A change in a year-over-year growth rate caused by an unusual month a year earlier dropping out of the 12-month comparison, rather than by anything happening now.">base effect</Term>.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Headline GDP growth is an annualized quarterly rate">
When BEA reports that real GDP grew 3.4% in the third quarter of 2026 (the bundled `A191RL1Q225SBEA`), that is **not** how much GDP grew in the quarter. GDP grew 0.83% from the second quarter; 3.4% is that quarterly rate compounded over four quarters, $(1.0083)^4 - 1$.<sup>[12](#refs)</sup> Compared with a year earlier, GDP was up only 2.1%. All three numbers are correct. Before you compare a GDP growth rate with anything else (a company's sales growth, another country's GDP, a forecast), check that both are on the same basis: quarterly, annualized or year over year. You will reproduce BEA's 3.4% in the exercises.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="YoY growth is late at turning points">
Because YoY growth averages a whole year of monthly changes, it reacts slowly. When a series turns down, YoY growth keeps looking healthy for months. Analysts who need an early read look at the annualized change over the last three or six months of SA data, a compromise between noise and speed.
</Callout>

## Differences: change in the original units

A <Term def="The change from one period to the next in the series' own units: y(t) minus y(t-1). A seasonal difference subtracts the value from a year earlier instead.">difference</Term>, $y_t - y_{t-1}$, measures the change in the series' own units. It answers "how many more?" rather than "what percent more?". The jobs report is quoted this way: payrolls rose by **165,000** in October 2026. As a growth rate that is 0.10%, a number nobody remembers. The year-over-year version is the *seasonal difference*, $y_t - y_{t-12}$.

Use differences when the unit itself is meaningful (jobs, dollars, units shipped) and for series that are **already rates or percents**. The unemployment rate fell from 4.2% in September to 4.1% in October 2026. That is a change of −0.1 **percentage point**. Saying "unemployment fell 2.4%" is technically a percent change of a percent, and it confuses everyone.

**How to convert back.** Add the changes to a known starting level: $y_t = y_{t-1} + \Delta y_t$, or with many periods, the starting level plus the running total (`cumsum()` in both R and Python). Differencing a trending series to make it stable is a modeling step; [Chapter 7](/chapters/arima/) explains when and why to do it.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Percent or percentage points?">
When the thing you are changing is itself a percent, say **percentage points** (pp). Inflation fell from 9.0% in June 2022 to 3.7% in September 2026: a drop of **5.3 percentage points**. In relative terms the inflation rate fell by 59%, which is true but rarely what anyone means. For interest rates, markets use **basis points**: 1 basis point = 0.01 percentage point, so a quarter-point Fed cut is 25 basis points. Write "pp" or "points" in your table headers whenever a column is a change in a rate.
</Callout>

## Seasonally adjusted or not

A <Term def="Seasonally adjusted: the agency has estimated and removed the regular calendar pattern (holidays, weather, trading days), so consecutive months can be compared directly.">seasonally adjusted</Term> (SA) series has the regular calendar pattern removed by the agency, so you can compare one month directly with the next. A <Term def="Not seasonally adjusted: the raw data, including the regular calendar pattern.">not seasonally adjusted</Term> (NSA) series is the raw count, December spike and all. Chapter 2 shows how the adjustment is done; here is how to *use* the two versions.

| You have | You can | Do not |
|---|---|---|
| **SA** data | Compare consecutive months (MoM), annualize, chart the level | Forget that SA data are revised every year, when the seasonal factors are re-estimated |
| **NSA** data | Compare with the same month a year earlier (YoY), model the seasonality yourself | Compare consecutive months or annualize them |

The lede's headline is the textbook case. In December 2025, NSA retail sales rose **11.8%** from November. The SA series (`RSAFS`, which also includes restaurants) rose just **0.1%**. December beats November every year; the SA number asks whether this December beat a *normal* December.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Never mix an SA number with an NSA number">
Before you compare, subtract or divide two series, check that they are both SA or both NSA (and both at monthly or both at annual rates). Dividing NSA sales by SA prices, or comparing an SA month with an NSA month from the same report, builds the seasonal pattern into your answer. FRED IDs often give it away: `RSXFSN` and `MRTSSM4541USN` end in **N** for NSA, while `MRTSSM4541USS` ends in **S** for SA. When only NSA data exist, use YoY comparisons.
</Callout>

## Real vs. nominal: more stuff, or higher prices?

Retail sales are measured in current dollars. These are <Term def="Measured in the prices of the time. Nominal growth mixes changes in quantity with changes in price.">nominal</Term> values. When prices rise 30%, nominal sales rise even if people buy exactly the same things. To measure the quantity of goods, you divide by a price index. The result is a <Term def="Adjusted for inflation by dividing by a price index; measured in the prices of a fixed base period.">real</Term> series, also called a *deflated* series. Guess first.

For one year of low inflation, subtracting is a fine shortcut (5% nominal growth with 2% inflation is about 3% real); over several years, divide.

**How to read and convert.** Real = nominal ÷ price index × 100 gives values in the dollars of the index's base period: CPI averages 100 in 1982–84, so the result is in "1982–84 dollars". Those are hard to picture, so analysts usually restate real values in **today's dollars**: multiply each month by (CPI today ÷ CPI that month). To go back to nominal, multiply by the price index and divide by 100.

<Callout type="business" title="When a company reports “record sales”">
With inflation running at 3–4%, a company whose nominal sales grew 3% sold *fewer* units than last year. Annual reports, budgets and wage negotiations all hinge on this.
</Callout>

## Per capita: adjusting for population

A country, a state or a customer base that grows will have rising totals even if nobody is better off. Dividing by population gives a <Term def="Per person: a total divided by the population. It removes growth that comes only from having more people.">per-capita</Term> number, which answers "is the *average person* getting more?" Hyndman and Athanasopoulos list population and inflation adjustments among the standard ways to remove a known source of variation before modeling.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Per capita is not always the right denominator">
Per-capita GDP measures average output per resident, not the typical person's income or how it is shared. Use per-worker or per-hour figures for productivity, per-household for housing demand, and per-store for comparing chains. The denominator *is* the question.
</Callout>

## Index numbers and rebasing

An <Term def="A series divided by its value in a base period and multiplied by 100. 112 means 12% above the base period.">index number</Term> divides a series by its value in a base period and multiplies by 100: $\text{index}_t = 100 \times y_t / y_{\text{base}}$. Many official series are published only as indexes: CPI (1982–84 = 100), industrial production (2017 = 100). Index *levels* have no meaning on their own. A CPI of 336.3 does not mean prices are \$336; it means prices are 236% higher than in 1982–84. Only **ratios** of index values carry information.

## Units and scaling

Every bundled series has units, and getting them wrong by a factor of 1,000 is the most common error in student (and professional) work. Retail sales are in **millions** of dollars, payrolls in **thousands** of jobs, GDP in **billions** of chained 2017 dollars at an annual rate, housing starts in **thousands** of units at an annual rate, and CPI is an index.

- **Rescaling** (dividing by 1,000, reporting in billions) changes no information, only the labels. It does change a regression coefficient by the same factor, so the units of the coefficient must always be stated; [Chapter 4](/chapters/regression/) shows how.
- **Pick units your reader can picture**: "\$745 billion a month" beats "745,021 (millions)".
- **Logs and growth rates are unit-free**, which is one reason forecasters like them: switching from millions to billions shifts $\ln y$ by a constant and leaves every log change untouched.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Sanity-check a magnitude in one line">
Before you report any number, check it against something you know. Seasonally adjusted retail and food services sales were \$745 billion in September 2026 (`RSAFS` = 745,021, in millions). Times 12 months is about \$8.9 trillion a year. Divided by 341.8 million people, that is roughly **\$26,000 per person per year**, or about \$2,200 a month. Plausible. If your calculation had said \$26 or \$26 million, you would know a unit went wrong before your manager does.
</Callout>

## Transformation decision guide

Start from the question, not from the data. Find your question in the left column; the row tells you what to compute, how to say the result in words, and how to get back to the original units.

| Your question | Transformation | Read the result as | Back to original units |
|---|---|---|---|
| How big is it? | **Level**, in units a reader can picture | "\$745 billion a month (SA)" | — |
| How many more than last period? | **Difference** $y_t - y_{t-1}$ | "payrolls +165,000"; for rates, "−0.1 percentage point" | Add changes to a starting level (`cumsum`) |
| Is growth steady? Do swings grow with the level? | **Log** $\ln y_t$ | Slope of 0.01 per period ≈ 1% growth per period | $y = e^{\ln y}$ (`exp`) |
| What happened last month? (SA data only) | **MoM %** | "+0.35% from September" | $y_t = y_{t-1}(1 + m)$ |
| What if this pace lasted a year? (SA data only) | **Annualized** $(1+m)^{12} - 1$ | "4.3% at an annual rate" | $m = (1+a)^{1/12} - 1$ |
| How much higher than a year ago? (fine for NSA) | **YoY %** | "+3.7% from a year earlier" | $y_t = y_{t-12}(1 + g)$ |
| More stuff, or higher prices? | **Real** = nominal ÷ price index × 100 | "in 1982–84 dollars" or "in October-2026 dollars" | nominal = real × index ÷ 100 |
| Is the average person better off? | **Per capita** = total ÷ population | "\$26,000 per person per year" | total = per capita × population |
| Compare series in different units | **Index** $100\,y_t / y_{\text{base}}$ | "105 = 5% above the 2019 level" | $y_t = \text{index}_t \times y_{\text{base}} / 100$ |
| Is this month normal for the season? | **SA** series (published) or YoY | "SA, at an annual rate" | Use the agency's NSA series |

**Order of operations.** When you need several transformations, apply them in this order, because each step assumes the one before it:

1. **Units and frequency.** Same units, same frequency; sum flows (average weekly flows to compare months), average stocks and rates; never sum a SAAR series.
2. **Real.** Deflate money values by the matching price index.
3. **Per capita.** Divide by the population (or another denominator that matches your question).
4. **Seasonality.** Use the SA series, or compare with the same month a year earlier.
5. **Logs**, if the series grows by percentages.
6. **Differences or growth rates**, if your question is about change.
7. **Back to original units** for the final report, with a label that says what was done.

## Chapter checklist

- A forecast has a **target**, an **origin**, a **horizon**, an **information set** and an **uncertainty range**. Forecasting is not explaining, and it is not goal-setting.
- Know your data's shape: **cross-section**, **time series** or **panel**. Store series with their dates (`ts` or a `DatetimeIndex`); keep missing months as `NA`.
- When changing frequency, **sum flows** over periods of equal coverage (average weekly flows to compare months), **average or take the last value of stocks and rates**, and never sum a SAAR series. Data are **revised**: test forecasts on the vintage available at the time (ALFRED).
- **Pick the question, then the transformation**, and label every transformed number.
- **Logs**: a 0.01 change ≈ 1%; log changes add up and are symmetric; the shortcut fails for big moves; convert back with `exp()`. No logs of zero, negatives or rates.
- **Growth**: MoM and annualized only for **SA** data; **YoY** works for NSA data but lags turning points and has **base effects**. GDP headlines are **annualized** quarterly rates.
- **Differences** for "how many more" and for rates (in **percentage points**). **Real** = nominal ÷ price index; **per capita** = total ÷ population; growth rates combine by **division**.
- **Index numbers**: only ratios mean something; rebasing changes levels, never growth rates. Check **units** and sanity-check every magnitude.

    '“Published data are final.” GDP for 2008 Q4 went from −3.8% to −8.5%.',
    '“A log change of 0.5 is a 50% rise.” It is a 65% rise; the 0.01 ≈ 1% shortcut is only for small changes.',
    '“A 50% fall followed by a 50% rise gets you back.” It leaves you 25% below where you started.',
    '“GDP grew 3.4% in the quarter.” It grew 0.83%; 3.4% is the annualized rate.',
    '“If YoY inflation falls, prices fell.” Prices can still be rising; a big month may have dropped out of the window.',
    '“CPI rose 12 points, so inflation was 12%.” Index points are not percent.'
  ]}
>

**Session 1 activity: Data detective (20 minutes).** Teams of three each get one FRED ID: `RSXFSN`, `HOUST`, `ICSA`, `T10Y3M` or `GDPC1`.

1. **Identify (10 min).** From the FRED series page, answer the five questions in the tip box: units, frequency, SA/NSA, nominal/real, release schedule.
2. **Aggregate (10 min).** Convert the series to quarterly with the right rule, and state the rule in one sentence.

*Expected results:* `RSXFSN` is monthly, \$ millions, NSA, nominal, so use YoY and quarterly **sums**. `HOUST` is thousands of units at a SAAR, so quarterly **averages**, never sums. `ICSA` is weekly and SA; it is a flow, but months hold four or five weeks, so **average** the weeks to compare months (the sum is a valid monthly total but jumps about 25% in five-week months). `T10Y3M` is a daily rate in percentage points, so use the average or the end of period; the lab finds 36 inverted months by average and 38 by month-end since 2019. `GDPC1` is quarterly, chained 2017 dollars (already real) and SAAR, so quote growth as annualized quarterly or YoY. Keep the team's series for session 2.

**Session 2 activity: The analyst's inbox (22 minutes).** Pairs work through exercises 2–4 in **Your turn** (about 5 minutes each), then write a one-sentence reply to the manager for each, with units. In the last 7 minutes, each Data-detective team opens the “Six ways to read one series” lab, swaps in its own series ID, and names the single view it would put on a slide for executives.

*Expected results:*
- **December headline:** MoM +11.8%, YoY **+3.4%** (December 2024 also jumped 9.4% from November). The SA series rose 0.1%. Reply: “Sales were 3.4% above a year earlier; the 12% is the normal holiday jump. No case for extra hiring.”
- **Gas record:** today \$4.31 (October 2026). Nominal record: June 2022, \$4.93. In October-2026 dollars the record is **June 2008, \$6.29**; June 2022 is \$5.64. Reply: “Gas is high, but below both the 2022 and the 2008 peaks; in today’s dollars it was 46% more expensive in June 2008.”
- **GDP:** 0.83% from the previous quarter, 100 × log change 0.83, annualized **3.37%**, which BEA rounds to 3.4%; YoY 2.1%. Reply: “Both are right: 0.83% in the quarter is 3.4% at an annual rate.”
- **Team views:** `RSXFSN` YoY %; `HOUST` level (it is already an annual rate) or YoY; `ICSA` level or 4-week average; `T10Y3M` level in percentage points with a zero line; `GDPC1` annualized quarterly growth or YoY.
