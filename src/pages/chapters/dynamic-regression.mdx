<p class="lede">Every Thursday at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, the Department of Labor reports how many people filed a <em>new</em> claim for unemployment benefits the week before. The jobs report, the number markets care about most, comes out only once a month. If the weekly number told you where the monthly one was heading, you would know first. <strong>Does it?</strong> This chapter teaches you to answer that question honestly, and to avoid the three traps that make time-series regressions lie.</p>

Economists sort indicators by **timing**. A <Term def="A series whose turning points tend to come before those of the overall economy, so it can be used to forecast it.">leading indicator</Term> turns before the economy does. A <Term def="A series that moves at about the same time as the overall economy, so it tells you where the economy is now.">coincident indicator</Term> moves with it. A <Term def="A series whose turning points come after those of the economy. Useful to confirm a turn, not to predict one.">lagging indicator</Term> turns afterwards. The Conference Board publishes a composite index of each:<sup>[1](#refs)</sup>

| Type | Conference Board components (selection) | In this chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **Leading** (10 components) | average weekly initial claims for unemployment insurance; building permits; average weekly hours in manufacturing; interest rate spread (10-year Treasury minus fed funds); S&P 500; ISM new orders; consumer expectations | `ICSA`, `PERMIT`; `T10Y3M` (10-year minus 3-month) is a close cousin of the spread |
| **Coincident** (4 components) | payroll employment; personal income less transfers; manufacturing and trade sales; industrial production | `PAYEMS`, `INDPRO` |
| **Lagging** (7 components) | includes the average duration of unemployment, the average prime rate and the change in the CPI for services<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> | `UNRATE` behaves like one below |

Notice that **payroll employment is itself a coincident indicator**. So our question, "do claims tell us where jobs are going?", is really the question of whether a leading indicator earns its name. GDP is not one of the components (it is quarterly), but it is the textbook coincident measure of the cycle.

<KeyIdea>A leading indicator is only useful if it leads by a stable amount, is known before the thing you forecast, and improves on a forecast that ignores it. This chapter tests all three.</KeyIdea>

## From correlation to a model: distributed lags

A correlation tells you *that* claims lead. A forecaster needs *how much*: if claims jump 10% this month, how many fewer jobs will be added this month, next month, and the month after? That is the job of an <Term def="Autoregressive distributed lag model: the outcome depends on its own past values (autoregressive part) and on current and past values of a predictor (distributed lag part).">ADL model</Term>:

$$
\Delta \text{jobs}_t = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{2} \phi_i\, \Delta \text{jobs}_{t-i} + \sum_{j=0}^{3} \beta_j\, g_{t-j} + u_t
$$

where $\Delta\text{jobs}_t$ is jobs added in month $t$ (thousands) and $g_t$ is the % change in claims. The $\phi$ terms are the *autoregressive* part; the $\beta$ terms are the *distributed lag* of claims growth. Each $\beta$ is a <Term def="The change in the outcome k periods after a one-time change in the predictor, holding everything else fixed.">dynamic multiplier</Term> on its own; the $\phi$ terms make the effect echo. Try it: the explorer re-estimates the model on 1990–2019 every time you move a slider.

<ImpulseADL client:load />
</div>

With two lags of jobs and three of claims, a lasting 10% rise in claims comes with about **112,000 fewer jobs** added in the same month, a cumulative **843,000** after a year, and about **1.03 million** in the long run. Set *p* to 0 and the response stops dead after *q* months; set it back to 2 and the shock echoes for two years, because job growth itself is persistent.

<Callout type="math" title="Short-run and long-run multipliers">
Write the model as $y_t = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i y_{t-i} + \sum_{j=0}^{q}\beta_j x_{t-j} + u_t$.

- **Impact multiplier**: $\partial y_t / \partial x_t = \beta_0$.
- **Dynamic multipliers** $m_h$: feed a one-time unit change in $x$ through the equation, $m_h = \beta_h + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i m_{h-i}$ (with $\beta_h = 0$ for $h > q$ and $m_{h<0}=0$).
- **Long-run multiplier**: the total effect of a permanent unit change in $x$ once everything has settled, $\displaystyle \sum_{h\ge 0} m_h = \frac{\sum_j \beta_j}{1-\sum_i \phi_i}$, which exists when $\sum_i \phi_i < 1$.

Here $x$ is the *growth* of claims and $y$ the *change* in jobs. So a one-time jump in $x$ is a permanent shift in the *level* of claims, and the running total of $m_h$ is the effect on the *level* of employment.
</Callout>

### Why plain OLS is overconfident

There is a catch in running any regression on time series. OLS standard errors assume each month's error is unrelated to the last. With monthly macro data that is almost never true. In the lab below, a simple regression of job gains on same-month claims growth leaves residuals with an autocorrelation of **0.74** at lag 1. Each month's surprise mostly repeats the previous one, so the 360 months carry far less independent information than 360 coin flips would, and OLS reports more precision than the data contain.

Three fixes, all in the lab:

1. **Keep OLS, fix the standard errors.** <Term def="Heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent standard errors (Newey–West): they allow the regression errors to be correlated over time, so t-statistics are not overstated.">Newey–West (HAC)</Term> standard errors<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>, first met in Chapter 4 and used again in Chapter 5's recession model, allow for autocorrelation. For the claims slope the standard error goes from 2.54 to **4.78**, and the t-statistic from −5.2 to **−2.7**.
2. **Put the dynamics in the model.** Adding lags (the ADL) soaks up most of the autocorrelation. Some remains, so we still report Newey–West errors.
3. **Model the errors directly.** <Term def="A regression y = a + b·x + e in which the error e follows an ARIMA model. Fitted in R with auto.arima(y, xreg = x).">Regression with ARIMA errors</Term> (`auto.arima(y, xreg = x)`)<sup>[10](#refs)</sup> estimates the slope and an ARIMA model for the error together. It is also a forecasting model in its own right: forecast the predictor, and the ARIMA error carries the persistence forward.

Notice how the claims slope changes between methods: −13.1 in the static OLS regression, but −6.4 in the regression with ARIMA(2,0,2) errors. Once the errors are allowed to carry the persistent swings in job growth, less of those swings gets credited to claims. Coefficient estimates, not just standard errors, can shift when you take the time-series structure seriously.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Newey–West is a bandage, not a cure">
HAC standard errors fix inference for the slope you estimated. They do not make a mis-specified model right, and they do nothing about the next problem, which is far worse: a "relationship" that does not exist at all.
</Callout>

## Spurious regression: when two random walks look related

In 1974 Clive Granger and Paul Newbold ran a simple experiment.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> They generated pairs of **independent** <Term def="A series where each value equals the previous value plus a new random shock. It wanders with no tendency to return to a mean, like a stock price.">random walks</Term>, regressed one on the other, and counted how often the usual t-test declared the slope "significant". It should happen about 5% of the time.

Why does it happen? Each random walk drifts away from its starting point and stays there for long stretches. Two such series almost always end up trending in some direction over the sample, so they look related, and the t-test, built for independent errors, cannot tell a shared drift from a real link. The residuals are themselves a random walk, which is the tell-tale sign. Granger and Newbold's rule of thumb: be suspicious whenever $R^2$ is larger than the Durbin–Watson statistic.

The last chart in the lab is the most unsettling: the false-alarm rate climbs from 57% with 25 observations to 88% with 400. More data does not rescue you, because with random walks the t-statistic grows with the square root of the sample size even when there is nothing there.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup>

<KeyIdea>Before regressing one trending series on another, ask whether each one wanders like a random walk. If so, regress changes on changes, or test for cointegration.</KeyIdea>

Two ways out:

- **Difference both series**, as we did with job gains and claims growth in this chapter. Changes of random walks are white noise, and the t-test works again.
- **Test for <Term def="Two random-walk series are cointegrated if some combination of them is stationary: they wander, but together, tied by a long-run equilibrium.">cointegration</Term>.** Sometimes two wandering series really are tied together (consumption and income, or a stock's price in two markets). Differencing would throw away that long-run link. [Chapter 9](/chapters/var/) shows how to test for it and model it with an error-correction model.

## Structural breaks: when the relationship changes

Even a real relationship can change. A <Term def="A change, at some date, in the parameters of a model: the mean, the slope, or the variance.">structural break</Term> is a date after which the old model no longer fits. Forecasting with a model estimated across a break blends two worlds and fits neither.

If you know the date in advance (a policy change, a pandemic), the **Chow test** (Chow, 1960) compares one model fitted to the whole sample with two models fitted before and after. If you don't know the date, compute the Chow F-statistic at **every** candidate date and take the largest: the **sup-F** (or QLR) test of Andrews (1993). The catch: the largest of many F-statistics is large by chance, so it needs its own, higher critical values.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup>

</div>

Two lessons from the break hunter:

- **Annual job growth downshifted around 2000.** The sup-F statistic is 11.2, above its 5% critical value of about 8.6. Average growth was about 2.2% a year from 1960 to 2000 and about 0.7% from 2001 to 2019. The 2008 recession is a deep dip, but it does not show up as a lasting change in the average.
- **COVID looks like a break, but it is a handful of outliers.** Switch to the claims-to-jobs link. The F-statistic jumps to 129 at March 2020 and is below 2 at the months either side of it. A real regime change would raise F over a range of dates; a spike at one date says that one or two extreme observations are pulling the fit.

<Callout type="math" title="Chow and sup-F in one paragraph">
With a candidate break after observation $\tau$, let $\text{RSS}_0$ be the residual sum of squares of one model on all $n$ observations, and $\text{RSS}_1+\text{RSS}_2$ that of separate models before and after $\tau$, each with $k$ coefficients. The Chow statistic is

$$
F(\tau) = \frac{(\text{RSS}_0 - \text{RSS}_1 - \text{RSS}_2)/k}{(\text{RSS}_1+\text{RSS}_2)/(n-2k)}.
$$

At a known date it follows an $F(k, n-2k)$ distribution. The sup-F statistic is $\max_\tau F(\tau)$ over the middle 70% of the sample ("15% trimming"). Its distribution was derived by Andrews (1993); for $k=1$ the 5% critical value is about 8.7 in Andrews's table (strucchange's approximation, used in the lab and the explorer, gives 8.6), compared with about 4 for a single F-test. `strucchange::Fstats` reports $k \cdot F(\tau)$, which is the same as $F(\tau)$ when $k = 1$. Bai and Perron (1998, 2003) extend the idea to several unknown breaks, as `breakpoints()` in the strucchange package does.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

### Handling COVID in a forecasting model

The lab's last step shows how much this matters. Fitted on 2010–2024 with the pandemic months left in, the claims slope is **−30.2**: eight times the −3.84 you get once those months are set aside. Your options:

| Approach | What it does | In the lab |
|---|---|---|
| Ignore it | Lets a few extreme months set the slope for everything | slope −30.2 |
| One dummy for the whole period | Shifts the average during 2020–21 but leaves the slope distorted | slope −30.16 |
| **Pulse dummies**, one per extreme month | Each outlier gets its own intercept, so it no longer pulls the slope | slope −3.84 |
| Drop those months / shorter window | Same estimate as pulse dummies; simplest to explain | slope −3.84 |
| Robust methods | Down-weight outliers automatically, e.g. `tsclean()` or robust regression | not shown |

<Callout type="business" title="Pick a treatment and write it down">
After 2020, forecasters used all of these: dropping the pandemic months, one dummy per month, or models that let the size of shocks jump temporarily. Lenza and Primiceri (2022), for example, show that dropping the extreme months may be acceptable for estimating the parameters, but not for forecasting, because it hides how uncertain the future has become. Their fix keeps the months and models the temporary jump in the size of shocks.<sup>[11](#refs)</sup> Whatever you choose, **say so**: "COVID months excluded from estimation" belongs in the footnote of every forecast you publish, because it changes the numbers.
</Callout>

## Does adding claims beat the AR?

Now the real test. Forget in-sample t-statistics: does adding claims make **next month's** payroll forecast more accurate than a model that only uses past payrolls? We run a <Term def="An evaluation that mimics real-time forecasting: for each target date, the model is estimated only on data before it, then scored on the value it did not see. 'Pseudo' because the data are today's revised figures, not what was available at the time.">pseudo out-of-sample</Term> test: for every month, fit on everything before it, forecast that month, record the error.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Leakage: was the predictor known when you forecast?">
Standing at the end of month $t-1$ to forecast jobs in month $t$, you know claims through month $t-1$, not month $t$. A model that uses same-month claims is a <Term def="An estimate of the current period, made before the official number is published, using data that arrive sooner. Chapter 13 builds nowcasts properly.">nowcast</Term>: legitimate, but only once month $t$'s claims are in. Almost all of a month's weekly claims figures are published before that month's jobs report (which covers the pay period including the 12th).<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> Score a nowcast as if it were a one-month-ahead forecast and you have leaked the future into your test.
</Callout>

So, **do jobless claims tell us where jobs are going?** Honestly: not much, one month ahead, in normal times. Their value is concentrated around turning points, and mostly as a *nowcast* of the current month rather than a forecast of the next. And a model estimated through COVID without care can be worse than ignoring claims entirely. This pattern, indicators that look strong in one period and add little in another, is the main finding of Stock and Watson's large survey of asset prices as leading indicators of output and inflation.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup>

Two more cautions. First, all errors here are around 100,000 to 200,000 jobs, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics itself puts the 90% confidence interval for the monthly payroll change at about ±122,000 from sampling error alone.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> No model can be much more accurate than the data it is scored on. Second, this is *pseudo* out-of-sample: payrolls are revised for months after release, and we used today's revised numbers. A fully real-time test uses archived data vintages (FRED's companion database, ALFRED, stores them).

## Chapter checklist

- Classify indicators by **timing**: leading, coincident, lagging. Find the lead with the **cross-correlation function** and confirm it by shifting the series.
- Write **ADL models** with `dynlm(y ~ L(y, 1:p) + L(x, 0:q))`, and read the **impact**, **dynamic** and **long-run multipliers**.
- Time-series errors are autocorrelated: report **Newey–West** standard errors, add lags, or use **regression with ARIMA errors**.
- Never regress one **random walk** on another in levels: difference both, or test for **cointegration** ([Chapter 9](/chapters/var/)).
- Test for **structural breaks** with Chow (known date) or **sup-F** and `breakpoints()` (unknown date). Treat COVID as outliers with pulse dummies or exclusion, and say so.
- Judge an indicator **out of sample**, against an AR benchmark, using only data **known at forecast time**.

    '“Newey–West standard errors fix spurious regression.” They do not: with two random walks the slope estimate itself is meaningless.',
    '“A huge F-statistic at March 2020 means the economy changed regime.” A spike at one date is the signature of outliers; a regime change raises F over a range of dates.',
    '“Using this month’s claims to forecast this month’s jobs is fine because claims come out weekly.” It is a nowcast; scoring it as a forecast leaks future information.'
  ]}
>

**Indicator draft (30 minutes).** Teams of three each "draft" one indicator in the lead-lag explorer: claims, building permits, industrial production or the unemployment rate.

1. **Predict (5 min).** Each team writes down whether its indicator leads, lags or moves with job growth, and by how many months.
2. **Measure (10 min).** Use the explorer (1990–2019) and record the best lead and its correlation. Then tick *Include 2020 to today* and record both again.
3. **Spurious check (5 min).** Every student clicks *New pair* in the spurious-regression generator five times and counts red lines. Tally the class.
4. **Report (10 min).** Each team gives a 60-second verdict: "Would you put this indicator in a payroll forecasting model?"

*Expected results (1990–2019):* claims lead by 7 months (r = −0.73); building permits lead by 9 months (r = 0.68); industrial production is strongest at a 4-month lead (r = 0.80) and is best called roughly coincident; the unemployment rate lags by 12 months (r = −0.69). With 2020 onward included, the claims lead collapses to 1 month (r = −0.82) and the correlation at 7 months falls to −0.43. In the spurious check, expect about three red lines in every four clicks; across the lab's 1,000 simulations, 75% of levels regressions had |t| > 2. Close with the out-of-sample scorecard: a strong lead-lag correlation did not translate into better one-month-ahead forecasts in the 2010s.
