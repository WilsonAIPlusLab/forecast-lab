<p class="lede">Every Thursday at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, the Department of Labor reports how many people filed a <em>new</em> claim for unemployment benefits the week before. The jobs report, the number markets care about most, comes out only once a month. If the weekly number told you where the monthly one was heading, you would know first. <strong>Does it?</strong> This chapter teaches you to answer that question honestly, and to avoid the traps that make time-series regressions lie.</p>

Economists sort indicators by **timing**. A <Term def="A series whose turning points tend to come before those of the overall economy, so it can be used to forecast it.">leading indicator</Term> turns before the economy does. A <Term def="A series that moves at about the same time as the overall economy, so it tells you where the economy is now.">coincident indicator</Term> moves with it. A <Term def="A series whose turning points come after those of the economy. Useful to confirm a turn, not to predict one.">lagging indicator</Term> turns afterwards. The Conference Board publishes a composite index of each:<sup>[1](#refs)</sup>

| Type | Conference Board components (selection) | In this chapter |
|---|---|---|
| **Leading** (10 components) | average weekly initial claims for unemployment insurance; building permits; average weekly hours in manufacturing; interest rate spread (10-year Treasury minus fed funds); S&P 500; ISM new orders; consumer expectations | `ICSA`, `PERMIT`; `T10Y3M` (10-year minus 3-month) is a close cousin of the spread |
| **Coincident** (4 components) | payroll employment; personal income less transfers; manufacturing and trade sales; industrial production | `PAYEMS`, `INDPRO` |
| **Lagging** (7 components) | includes the average duration of unemployment, the average prime rate and the change in the CPI for services<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> | `UNRATE` behaves like one below |

Notice that **payroll employment is itself a coincident indicator**. So our question, "do claims tell us where jobs are going?", is really whether a leading indicator earns its name.

<KeyIdea>A leading indicator is only useful if it leads by a stable amount, is known before the thing you forecast, and improves on a forecast that ignores it. This chapter tests all three.</KeyIdea>

In levels, claims and consumer prices look like equally perfect partners for jobs, although only one has a real link to hiring. That is because **a correlation of two trending series mostly measures that both trend**: claims drifted down all decade, jobs and prices drifted up. Turn each into a growth rate and the drift is gone. What is left answers a real question: *in months when claims grow faster than usual, do jobs grow slower than usual?* For claims the answer is "yes, a little" (−0.15); for prices, "no" (0.02).

The 12-month row has the "wrong" sign (+0.49): in a decade with no recession, smooth annual changes can line up by accident, and same-month numbers ignore that claims move months *before* jobs. That is why the lead-lag explorer slides the series in time.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Difference before you correlate trending series">
Before you correlate or regress two series, plot them. If either one trends, the levels correlation is close to useless: it rewards any pair that drifts the same way.<sup>[12](#refs)</sup> Use the growth rate (`100 * diff(log(x))` for the monthly % change, `lag = 12` for the year-over-year change) or the plain change (`diff(x)`), whichever has the clearer units. [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#transformations-every-analyst-uses) reviews these transformations, and [Chapter 7](/chapters/arima/#the-cure-differencing) explains why differencing removes a trend. The [spurious-regression section](#spurious-regression-when-two-random-walks-look-related) shows the extreme case.
</Callout>

Which growth rate? The lead-lag explorer and lab use the **12-month % change**, which smooths out noise and makes turning points easy to see (but neighbouring months share eleven of twelve months of data, so correlations look stronger than they are). The ADL model and everything after use **monthly changes**: jobs added (`diff(jobs)`, thousands) and the % change in claims. Each month is then a fresh observation, which is what a regression needs.

### Reading a cross-correlation in words

The lab's headline number is *r* = −0.73 at *k* = −7. Here is how to say it in a report, with units and without overclaiming:

> "The 12-month % change in initial claims is most strongly correlated with the 12-month % change in payroll employment **seven months later** (*r* = −0.73, 1990–2019). When claims have been rising faster than usual, job growth tends to be below average about seven months afterwards."

Four habits make that sentence trustworthy:

1. **Name both transformations and the sample.** "Claims and jobs are correlated" could mean levels, monthly changes or annual changes, and the numbers differ a lot (see the table above).
2. **Say who moves first, in months.** A peak at a negative *k* in R's `ccf(x, y)` means *x* moves first. Translate the sign into words ("claims lead by seven months") so your reader does not have to know the convention.
3. **A correlation is not a slope.** −0.73 does not mean "jobs fall 0.73% when claims rise 1%". It says how closely the two shapes match, not how big the response is. The response size is what the ADL model in the next section measures.
4. **It is not cause and effect, and it is not yet a forecast.** Claims do not *cause* weaker hiring; rising layoffs and slowing hiring are both symptoms of firms cutting back, and the layoffs show up first. Whether the lead improves a forecast is a separate question, answered out of sample at the end of the chapter.

For scale: *r*² = 0.73² ≈ 0.54, so claims seven months earlier line up with about half of the ups and downs in annual job growth. That is a strong lead for macro data, and it still leaves half unexplained.

## From correlation to a model: distributed lags

A correlation tells you *that* claims lead. A forecaster needs *how much*: if claims jump 10% this month, how many fewer jobs will be added this month, next month, and the month after? That is the job of an <Term def="Autoregressive distributed lag model: the outcome depends on its own past values (autoregressive part) and on current and past values of a predictor (distributed lag part).">ADL model</Term>. In words, the one we use says:

> **jobs added this month** (thousands) = a constant + a share of the **jobs added in each of the last two months** + an effect of **claims growth** (% change) this month and in each of the last three months + a surprise.

The "share of last months' job gains" terms (written $\phi$) are the *autoregressive* part: they capture how persistent job growth is. The claims terms (written $\beta$) are the *distributed lag*: the effect of claims spread over several months. Each $\beta$ is a <Term def="The change in the outcome k periods after a one-time change in the predictor, holding everything else fixed.">dynamic multiplier</Term> on its own; the $\phi$ terms make the effect echo. Try it: the explorer re-estimates the model on 1990–2019 every time you move a slider.

</div>

With two lags of jobs and three of claims, a lasting 10% rise in claims comes with about **112,000 fewer jobs** added in the same month, a cumulative **843,000** after a year, and about **1.03 million** in the long run. Set *p* to 0 and the response stops dead after *q* months; set it back to 2 and the shock echoes for two years, because job growth itself is persistent.

<Callout type="math" title="The ADL(2,3) equation, and short-run and long-run multipliers">
The model in this section is $\Delta \text{jobs}_t = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{2} \phi_i\, \Delta \text{jobs}_{t-i} + \sum_{j=0}^{3} \beta_j\, g_{t-j} + u_t$, where $\Delta\text{jobs}_t$ is jobs added in month $t$ (thousands) and $g_t$ is the % change in claims. In general, write the model as $y_t = \alpha + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i y_{t-i} + \sum_{j=0}^{q}\beta_j x_{t-j} + u_t$.

- **Impact multiplier**: $\partial y_t / \partial x_t = \beta_0$.
- **Dynamic multipliers** $m_h$: feed a one-time unit change in $x$ through the equation, $m_h = \beta_h + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i m_{h-i}$ (with $\beta_h = 0$ for $h > q$ and $m_{h<0}=0$).
- **Long-run multiplier**: the total effect of a permanent unit change in $x$ once everything has settled, $\displaystyle \sum_{h\ge 0} m_h = \frac{\sum_j \beta_j}{1-\sum_i \phi_i}$, which exists when $\sum_i \phi_i < 1$.

Here $x$ is the *growth* of claims and $y$ the *change* in jobs. So a one-time jump in $x$ is a permanent shift in the *level* of claims, and the running total of $m_h$ is the effect on the *level* of employment.
</Callout>

### Reading the ADL coefficients, with units

Before you read any coefficient, write down the units of both sides. Here the outcome is **jobs added in the month, in thousands**, and claims enter as a **% change from the previous month**. So every claims coefficient means "thousands of jobs added, per 1% change in claims". For scale, 1% of claims at their 2019 average (218,000 new claims a week) is about 2,200 extra claims a week. These are the ADL(2,3) estimates from the lab below (1990–2019), with Newey–West t-statistics:

| Term | Estimate | t (Newey–West) | In words, holding the other terms fixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jobs added 1 month ago | 0.39 | 10.8 | For every 100,000 extra jobs added last month, about 39,000 more are added this month |
| Jobs added 2 months ago | 0.43 | 10.3 | …and about 43,000 more for every 100,000 extra two months ago |
| Claims growth, same month | −11.2 | −7.4 | A 1% rise in claims this month goes with about 11,200 fewer jobs added this month |
| Claims growth, 1 month earlier | −5.6 | −4.0 | Last month's 1% rise in claims goes with about 5,600 fewer jobs added this month, on top of the echo |
| Claims growth, 2 and 3 months earlier | −2.6, 0.3 | −1.5, 0.2 | Too small to tell apart from zero |

Three ways *not* to read this table:

- **Not "the effect of claims is −11.2".** That is only the *impact*, the same-month effect. Because job gains are persistent (0.39 + 0.43 = 0.81 of last two months' gains carry over), the effect keeps building for months.
- **Not "lags 2 and 3 are near zero, so claims stop mattering after a month".** The *direct* effect fades, but the echo through the job-gain lags keeps going: in month 1 the response is −5.6 plus 0.39 × (−11.2) ≈ −9.9 per 1% rise in claims.
- **Not a causal effect.** "A 1% rise in claims goes with…" is a forecasting statement about what usually follows, not a promise that cutting claims would create jobs.

An ADL gives you at least three honest answers to "what does a 10% jump in claims mean for jobs?", and they differ by a factor of ten: **−112,000** in the same month (impact), **−843,000** after a year (cumulative), **−1.03 million** in the long run, all for a jump that lasts. Then convert to something the reader knows: 843,000 jobs is about 0.56% of the 151.8 million payroll jobs at the end of 2019.<sup>[16](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

One catch before the lab. OLS standard errors assume each month's error is unrelated to the last, and with monthly macro data that is almost never true: a regression of job gains on same-month claims growth alone leaves residuals with a lag-1 autocorrelation of **0.74**, so OLS reports more precision than the data contain. The ADL's own lags soak up most of that. For what remains we report <Term def="Heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent standard errors (Newey–West): they allow the regression errors to be correlated over time, so t-statistics are not overstated.">Newey–West (HAC)</Term> standard errors<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>, met in [Chapter 4](/chapters/regression/#standard-errors-you-can-defend): they keep the estimate and only widen its standard error. ## Spurious regression: when two random walks look related

In 1974 Clive Granger and Paul Newbold ran a simple experiment.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> They generated pairs of **independent** <Term def="A series where each value equals the previous value plus a new random shock. It wanders with no tendency to return to a mean, like a stock price.">random walks</Term>, regressed one on the other, and counted how often the usual t-test declared the slope "significant". It should happen about 5% of the time.

Why does it happen? Each random walk drifts away from its starting point and stays there for long stretches. Two such series almost always end up trending in some direction over the sample, so they look related, and the t-test, built for independent errors, cannot tell a shared drift from a real link. The residuals are themselves a random walk, which is the tell-tale sign.

<KeyIdea>Before regressing one trending series on another, ask whether each one wanders like a random walk. If so, regress changes on changes, or test for cointegration.</KeyIdea>

Two ways out:

- **Difference both series**, as we did with job gains and claims growth in this chapter. Changes of random walks are white noise, and the t-test works again. [Chapter 7](/chapters/arima/#spot-the-random-walk) shows how to spot a random walk.
- **Test for <Term def="Two random-walk series are cointegrated if some combination of them is stationary: they wander, but together, tied by a long-run equilibrium.">cointegration</Term>.** Sometimes two wandering series really are tied together (consumption and income), and differencing would throw away that long-run link. [Chapter 9](/chapters/var/) shows how to test for it.

## Does adding claims beat the AR?

Now the real test. Forget in-sample t-statistics: does adding claims make **next month's** payroll forecast more accurate than a model that only uses past payrolls? We run a <Term def="An evaluation that mimics real-time forecasting: for each target date, the model is estimated only on data before it, then scored on the value it did not see. 'Pseudo' because the data are today's revised figures, not what was available at the time.">pseudo out-of-sample</Term> test: for every month, fit on everything before it, forecast that month, record the error.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Leakage: was the predictor known when you forecast?">
Standing at the end of month $t-1$ to forecast jobs in month $t$, you know claims through month $t-1$, not month $t$. A model that uses same-month claims is a <Term def="An estimate of the current period, made before the official number is published, using data that arrive sooner. Chapter 13 will build nowcasts properly.">nowcast</Term>: legitimate, but only once month $t$'s claims are in. Weekly claims come out each Thursday, five days after the week ends,<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> so a month's last week is usually public just before that month's jobs report. Score a nowcast as if it were a one-month-ahead forecast and you have leaked the future into your test.
</Callout>

So, **do jobless claims tell us where jobs are going?** Honestly: not much, one month ahead, in normal times. Their value is concentrated around turning points, and mostly as a *nowcast* of the current month rather than a forecast of the next. And a model estimated through COVID without care can be worse than ignoring claims entirely. Indicators that look strong in one period and add little in another are the main finding of Stock and Watson's survey of leading indicators.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup>

Why did the COVID months do so much damage? A few extreme months can pull a regression's slope far from its normal value: fitted on 2010–2024 with the pandemic months left in, the claims slope is about eight times what it is without them. Whatever you do with those months (drop them, or give each its own dummy), **say so** in the forecast's footnote. Relationships can also change for good; Beyond the basics below shows how to test for such a <Term def="A change, at some date, in the parameters of a model: the mean, the slope, or the variance.">structural break</Term> and compares ways of handling COVID.

Two more cautions. The Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the 90% confidence interval for the monthly payroll change at about ±122,000 from sampling error alone,<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> so no model can be much more accurate than that. And this is *pseudo* out-of-sample: we used today's revised payrolls, not the first estimates a forecaster saw; a real-time test would be tougher.

## Chapter checklist

- Classify indicators by **timing**: leading, coincident, lagging.
- **Transform before you correlate.** Trending series correlate in levels whatever their link (claims −0.97 and CPI +0.98 with jobs in 2010–2019). Use growth rates or changes, and name the transformation, units and sample in every result.
- Find the lead with the **cross-correlation function**, confirm it by shifting the series, and say it in words ("claims growth leads job growth by about seven months, *r* = −0.73"). A correlation is not a slope and not a cause.
- Write **ADL models** with `dynlm(y ~ L(y, 1:p) + L(x, 0:q))` and read each coefficient in units. Report the **impact**, **cumulative** or **long-run** effect; say which one you mean and whether the shock lasts. Time-series errors are autocorrelated, so report Newey–West standard errors.
- Never regress one **random walk** on another in levels: difference both, or test for **cointegration** ([Chapter 9](/chapters/var/)).
- Judge an indicator **out of sample**, against an AR benchmark, using only data **public on forecast day**. Say how you treated extreme months such as COVID.

#### Why plain OLS is overconfident

There is a catch in running any regression on time series. OLS standard errors assume each month's error is unrelated to the last. With monthly macro data that is almost never true. In the ADL lab above, a simple regression of job gains on same-month claims growth leaves residuals with an autocorrelation of **0.74** at lag 1. Each month's surprise mostly repeats the previous one, so the 360 months carry far less independent information than 360 coin flips would, and OLS reports more precision than the data contain.

Three fixes, all in the ADL lab above:

1. **Keep OLS, fix the standard errors.** <Term def="Heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent standard errors (Newey–West): they allow the regression errors to be correlated over time, so t-statistics are not overstated.">Newey–West (HAC)</Term> standard errors<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>, first met in Chapter 4 and used again in Chapter 5's recession model, allow for autocorrelation. For the claims slope the standard error goes from 2.54 to **4.78**, and the t-statistic from −5.2 to **−2.7**.
2. **Put the dynamics in the model.** Adding lags (the ADL) soaks up most of the autocorrelation. Some remains, so we still report Newey–West errors.
3. **Model the errors directly.** <Term def="A regression y = a + b·x + e in which the error e follows an ARIMA model. Fitted in R with auto.arima(y, xreg = x).">Regression with ARIMA errors</Term> (`auto.arima(y, xreg = x)`)<sup>[10](#refs)</sup> estimates the slope and an ARIMA model for the error together. It is also a forecasting model in its own right: forecast the predictor, and the ARIMA error carries the persistence forward.

Notice how the claims slope changes between methods: −13.1 in the static OLS regression, but −6.4 in the regression with ARIMA(2,0,2) errors. Once the errors are allowed to carry the persistent swings in job growth, less of those swings gets credited to claims. Coefficient estimates, not just standard errors, can shift when you take the time-series structure seriously.

#### Reading Newey–West and plain t-statistics side by side

The ADL lab above prints the static regression twice. Put the two outputs next to each other and read them like this:

| | Estimate | Std. error | t | 95% interval (estimate ± 1.96 × s.e.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plain OLS | −13.1 | 2.54 | −5.2 | −18.1 to −8.1 |
| Newey–West | −13.1 | 4.78 | −2.7 | −22.5 to −3.7 |

- **The estimate is identical.** Newey–West changes only how *sure* you are, never the number itself.
- **The honest interval is almost twice as wide.** In words: "each 1% rise in claims goes with somewhere between about 4,000 and 22,000 fewer jobs added in the same month". Plain OLS claimed 8,000 to 18,000.
- **The conclusion survives, but only just.** A *t* of −2.7 still clears 2, so claims growth is related to job gains. A variable with a plain-OLS *t* of 3 could easily drop below 2 once you correct it.

<Callout type="empirical" title="A big gap between plain and Newey–West errors is a symptom">
In the static regression the Newey–West standard error is 1.9 times the plain one. In the ADL(2,3), where two lags of job gains are in the model, the two are almost the same (for same-month claims, 1.43 plain vs 1.51 Newey–West). That is the pattern to look for: **when plain and robust standard errors differ a lot, the model is missing dynamics** that are hiding in the errors. Add lags and the gap closes. Report the Newey–West numbers either way, and say so in the table note ("HAC standard errors, 6 lags"). [Chapter 4](/chapters/regression/#standard-errors-you-can-defend) introduced robust standard errors.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Newey–West is a bandage, not a cure">
HAC standard errors fix inference for the slope you estimated. They do not make a mis-specified model right, and they do nothing about a far worse problem: a "relationship" that does not exist at all (see the spurious-regression section).
</Callout>

#### Structural breaks: when the relationship changes

Even a real relationship can change. A structural break is a date after which the old model no longer fits. Forecasting with a model estimated across a break blends two worlds and fits neither.

If you know the date in advance (a policy change, a pandemic), the **Chow test** (Chow, 1960) compares one model fitted to the whole sample with two models fitted before and after. If you don't know the date, compute the Chow F-statistic at **every** candidate date and take the largest: the **sup-F** (or QLR) test of Andrews (1993). The catch: the largest of many F-statistics is large by chance, so it needs its own, higher critical values.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup>

- **Annual job growth downshifted around 2000.** The sup-F statistic is 11.2, above its 5% critical value of about 8.6. Average growth was about 2.2% a year from 1960 to 2000 and about 0.7% from 2001 to 2019. The 2008 recession is a deep dip, but it does not show up as a lasting change in the average.

A real regime change would raise F over a range of dates; a spike at one date says that one or two extreme observations are pulling the fit.

<Callout type="math" title="Chow and sup-F in one paragraph">
With a candidate break after observation $\tau$, let $\text{RSS}_0$ be the residual sum of squares of one model on all $n$ observations, and $\text{RSS}_1+\text{RSS}_2$ that of separate models before and after $\tau$, each with $k$ coefficients. The Chow statistic is

$$
F(\tau) = \frac{(\text{RSS}_0 - \text{RSS}_1 - \text{RSS}_2)/k}{(\text{RSS}_1+\text{RSS}_2)/(n-2k)}.
$$

At a known date it follows an $F(k, n-2k)$ distribution. The sup-F statistic is $\max_\tau F(\tau)$ over the middle 70% of the sample ("15% trimming"). Its distribution was derived by Andrews (1993); for $k=1$ the 5% critical value is about 8.7 in Andrews's table (strucchange's approximation, used in the lab and the explorer, gives 8.6), compared with about 4 for a single F-test. `strucchange::Fstats` reports $k \cdot F(\tau)$, which is the same as $F(\tau)$ when $k = 1$. Bai and Perron (1998, 2003) extend the idea to several unknown breaks, as `breakpoints()` in the strucchange package does.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

#### What a break means for a forecaster

A significant break is a statement about which part of the past still describes the future. Read the job-growth result in those terms:

> "Average annual payroll growth fell from about 2.2% (1960–2000) to about 0.7% (2001–2019). The sup-F test rejects a constant average (p = 0.015), so data from before 2001 should not set the baseline for a forecast today."

What changes in practice:

- **The baseline.** The average of all 60 years is 1.73%. Use it as a "no-change" forecast of annual job growth and you would be about 1 percentage point too high every year compared with the post-2000 average. With about 151 million payroll jobs (the 2019 average), that is roughly **1.5 million jobs a year** of over-forecast.
- **The estimation window.** Fit on the data after the break, or at least check that your model's coefficients are similar before and after it. The price is fewer observations: 19 years instead of 60, so wider intervals.
- **The question "why?".** The test dates the change; it does not explain it. Before you rely on the new average, ask whether you can name a reason it should persist.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Breaks near the end of the sample are the hardest to see">
With 15% trimming, the sup-F test never tests the first or last 15% of the dates. In the 60-year job-growth sample, that means **no break after 2010 can be detected**, and those are the years a forecaster cares about most. A recent break shows up first as a run of forecast errors with the same sign, long before any test can confirm it. So track your recent errors (Chapter 3's tools), and when they keep missing in one direction, re-estimate on a shorter, more recent window and compare.
</Callout>

#### Handling COVID in a forecasting model

The break lab's last step shows how much this matters. Fitted on 2010–2024 with the pandemic months left in, the claims slope is **−30.2**: eight times the −3.84 you get once those months are set aside. Your options:

| Approach | What it does | In the lab |
|---|---|---|
| Ignore it | Lets a few extreme months set the slope for everything | slope −30.2 |
| One dummy for the whole period | Shifts the average during 2020–21 but leaves the slope distorted | slope −30.16 |
| **Pulse dummies**, one per extreme month | Each outlier gets its own intercept, so it no longer pulls the slope | slope −3.84 |
| Drop those months / shorter window | Same estimate as pulse dummies; simplest to explain | slope −3.84 |
| Robust methods | Down-weight outliers automatically, e.g. `tsclean()` or robust regression | not shown |

<Callout type="business" title="Pick a treatment and write it down">
After 2020, forecasters used all of these: dropping the pandemic months, one dummy per month, or models that let the size of shocks jump temporarily. Lenza and Primiceri (2022), for example, show that dropping the extreme months may be acceptable for estimating the parameters, but not for forecasting, because it hides how uncertain the future has become. Their fix keeps the months and models the temporary jump in the size of shocks.<sup>[11](#refs)</sup> Whatever you choose, **say so**: "COVID months excluded from estimation" belongs in the footnote of every forecast you publish, because it changes the numbers.
</Callout>

Three facts decide what you know on any forecast day:

- **Publication lags.** Weekly claims come out on Thursday, five days after the week ends on Saturday.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> The jobs report for a month comes out on the third Friday after the week that contains the 12th, which usually lands on the first Friday of the next month.<sup>[13](#refs)</sup> So by jobs-report day, all of the month's claims are normally public: using them is a legitimate *nowcast*. But a forecast made at the end of month *t* − 1 for month *t* can only use claims through *t* − 1.
- **Weekly-to-monthly alignment.** With `fred(..., to = "month")`, a week belongs to the month in which it *ends*. So the week of Sunday, March 29 to Saturday, April 4, 2020 counts entirely as April, even though two of its days were in March. Whatever rule you use, use the same rule when you build the training data and on forecast day.
- **Revisions.** The first estimate of a month is not the last. The payroll survey measures the pay period that includes the 12th, which in March 2020 was *before* the surge in layoffs. The first estimate, published April 3, 2020, said payrolls fell by **701,000**.<sup>[14](#refs)</sup> After the monthly revisions and annual benchmarks since, today's data put the March 2020 drop at **1.40 million**. BLS revises each month's estimate in the next two monthly releases, and once a year re-anchors the whole series to unemployment-insurance tax records.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> In August 2024, for example, its preliminary benchmark lowered March 2024 employment by 818,000; the final benchmark, published in February 2025, came to −598,000 before seasonal adjustment.<sup>[15](#refs)</sup>

In words: a **real-time** data set stores, for each forecast date, the numbers *as they were published by that date*. A **revised** (latest-vintage) data set stores today's numbers for every month. Testing a model on revised data answers "how good is the model if the data had been perfect?"; testing on real-time data answers "how good would it actually have been?". [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#data-get-revised-vintages-and-alfred) shows where the vintages live (ALFRED).

<Callout type="empirical" title="Build the forecaster's data set, not today's">
Before any out-of-sample test, write one line per predictor: *what it measures, when it is released, and which month it is assigned to*. Then shift each predictor so that row *t* holds only what was public on forecast day: in R, `stats::lag(x, -1)` puts last month's value in this month's row; in pandas, `x.shift(1)`. Look-ahead bias is the classic way a model looks brilliant in a back-test and fails live: the model is quietly reading tomorrow's newspaper. If you can only get revised data, say so in the footnote ("pseudo out-of-sample, latest-vintage data"), because the real-time errors would have been larger.
</Callout>

*Expected results:* m0 ≈ −11.0, m1 ≈ −10.0, m2 ≈ −7.5 thousand jobs per 1% rise in claims, so a lasting 10% jump is followed by about 285,000 fewer jobs over three months (ADL(1,1); the ADL(2,3) in the chapter gives about 323,000). Lagged claims add nothing in the calm 2010s (RMSE 116.9 vs 116.7 thousand) and help only a little in the Great Recession (166.2 vs 163.4); a same-month nowcast cuts the Great Recession error by 10% (150.4) but is legitimate only once the month's claims are public. With the COVID months in training, adding claims doubles the 2022–2026 error (417.9 vs 198.3), so the footnote must say they were excluded.
