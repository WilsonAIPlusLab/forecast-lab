## What you just made

The chart has three ingredients that will appear in every chapter:

1. **History**: the data we observed, here U.S. retail sales since 2018.
2. **Point forecast**: the single "best guess" path, the bold line.
3. **Prediction intervals**: the shaded fan. The darker band is where we expect the future to land **80%** of the time, the lighter band **95%** of the time.

<KeyIdea>A forecast is not a number. It is a range of plausible futures, plus our best guess within that range.</KeyIdea>

Think of driving directions. "You will arrive in 20 minutes" is a good guess for a short trip. For a 6-hour trip the guess is still useful, but traffic, a stop for gas and road work can each add or remove time, and those surprises pile up. The longer the trip, the wider the honest range around your arrival time. Forecasts work the same way.

## The vocabulary of forecasting

<dl class="vocab">
  <dt>Time series</dt><dd>Observations of one variable in time order: monthly sales, quarterly GDP, daily prices. The order matters.</dd>
  <dt>Horizon (h)</dt><dd>How far ahead we forecast. `h = 24` with monthly data means two years.</dd>
  <dt>Model</dt><dd>A rule that turns the past into a forecast. `auto.arima()` picked one for us; Chapter 7 opens the box.</dd>
  <dt>Point forecast</dt><dd>The single best guess for each future period: the bold line.</dd>
  <dt>Prediction interval</dt><dd>A range that should contain the future value with a stated probability (80%, 95%).</dd>
  <dt>Trend</dt><dd>The long-run direction of a series: up, down or flat, once you look past the month-to-month wiggles.</dd>
  <dt>Seasonality</dt><dd>A pattern that repeats every year, like the December shopping spike. Chapter 2 is all about it.</dd>
</dl>

<Callout type="business" title="Where this shows up at work">
Retailers forecast sales to plan staffing and inventory. Banks forecast deposits and defaults. The Federal Reserve forecasts inflation and unemployment before every policy meeting. The workflow you just ran (load data, look, fit, forecast, communicate uncertainty) is the same one professional analysts follow. Their tools and data are bigger; the logic is the same.
</Callout>

## Plot before you model

A working analyst never fits a model to data they have not looked at. A plot takes ten seconds and answers three questions a model cannot answer for you: Is there a **trend**? Is there **seasonality**? Is there anything **strange** (a crash, a jump, a gap) that the model should not treat as normal?

<div class="breakout">
<SeeThePattern client:load />

The three views show the same numbers. Each makes a different feature easy to see:

- **Over time** shows the **trend** (the line drifts up) and the one big **surprise** (April 2020, when COVID shutdowns cut sales 11% in a single month).
- **Year by year** puts every year on a January-to-December axis, a picture Hyndman and Athanasopoulos call a *seasonal plot*.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> The **shape** that repeats is the seasonality. The **stacking**, with later years higher, is the trend.
- **Change vs a year ago** compares each month with the same month one year earlier. The seasonal zigzag disappears, because December is compared with December. What is left is growth.

<KeyIdea>Seasonality is a shape that repeats every year. Trend is the slow drift underneath it. Look for both, and for surprises, before you fit any model.</KeyIdea>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Check the units before you read a single number">
The September 2026 value of `RSXFSN` is **635,056**. Before you say anything, answer three questions.

- **What unit?** Millions of dollars. So that is **\$635 billion**, not \$635 thousand.
- **Per what period?** Per **month**. U.S. retail stores sell roughly \$7.3 trillion a *year* (the 12 months of 2025 add up to \$7.32 trillion).
- **Adjusted or not?** **Not** seasonally adjusted (the `N` at the end of the ID). So a December jump is normal, not news.

The trap is real. In September 2026, `HOUSTNSA` (housing starts, not seasonally adjusted) reads **103.8** and `HOUST` (housing starts, seasonally adjusted) reads **1,221**. Same month, same houses. `HOUSTNSA` counts thousands of homes started *that month*. `HOUST` is a *seasonally adjusted annual rate*: roughly "this month's pace, with the season removed, times 12". Compare one with the other and you are off by a factor of twelve. FRED's series page lists the units, frequency and adjustment right under the title; read that line first. [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#transformations-every-analyst-uses) covers units and adjustments in depth.
</Callout>

### A first look at logs and growth rates

The first lab had one mysterious argument: `lambda = 0`, which tells R to model the **logarithm** of sales. Here is the idea in one picture, using numbers you can check in the **Year by year** view. In 2012 the December jump over November was about \$47 billion. By 2025 it was about \$74 billion, more than half as big again. In *percent* it barely moved: 13.0% in 2012, 11.8% in 2025. The seasonal swing grows with the level of sales, but it stays roughly constant as a share of sales.

A log turns "the same *percentage* every year" into "the same *size* every year", which is easier for a model to learn. The model works in logs and R turns the forecast back into dollars for you. Robert Nau's Duke forecasting notes make the same point: logs turn growth and seasonal swings that scale with the level into steady ones.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup>

<Callout type="intuition" title="Dollars grow for two reasons">
Average monthly sales rose from \$352 billion in 2012 to \$610 billion in 2025, up 73%. But consumer prices (the CPI) rose about 40% over the same years. Much of the dollar growth is higher prices, not more stuff sold. Analysts separate the two by *deflating*. You will learn how, along with logs, growth rates and differences, in [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#real-vs-nominal-more-stuff-or-higher-prices). [Chapter 2](/chapters/decomposition/#additive-or-multiplicative) shows how a log turns a growing seasonal swing into a steady one.
</Callout>

## Read the fan in plain words

Now go back to the forecast from the first lab. A fan chart answers three questions, and a good analyst says all three out loud: **what is the best guess**, **how wrong could it be**, and **for which month**. Pick a month and a band below and read the sentence.

<div class="breakout">
<ForecastReader client:load />
</div>

For December 2026, three months after the last data point, the model's best guess is **about \$738 billion**. The 80% band runs from **\$706 billion to \$771 billion**; the 95% band from **\$690 billion to \$789 billion**. In words: "We expect December sales of about \$738 billion. Anything from \$706 to \$771 billion would not surprise us. Below \$690 billion or above \$789 billion would be a real surprise."

Three details are worth noticing.

- **The bands are not symmetric.** For December 2026 the 80% band reaches \$32 billion below the guess but \$33 billion above it. The model works in logs, so it thinks in percentages, and a 5% rise is more dollars than a 5% fall. Strictly, the bold line is the *middle* of the range: half the plausible outcomes lie above it and half below. [Chapter 10](/chapters/uncertainty/) explains this fine print.
- **The bands widen.** One month ahead the 80% band is about \$47 billion wide (7% of the forecast). Twenty-four months ahead it is about \$95 billion wide (14%). Each extra month adds new surprises on top of the old ones.
- **The 95% band is wider than the 80% band.** To be more confident that you have caught the truth, you must cast a wider net. Neither band is "more right"; they answer different questions.

<Callout type="empirical" title="The band is the real message, so report it">
When you send a forecast to a manager, write the **range first-class**, not as a footnote.

- **Round** to what you can defend. "\$738 billion" beats "\$737,835 million". The last digits are noise.
- Give the **unit**, the **month** and the **horizon**: "December 2026, three months ahead."
- Give the **band and its level**: "80% range \$706–771 billion."
- Avoid "sales **will** be \$738 billion." Say "we expect about", and give the range.
- Do not read a wide band as a bad model. An honest wide band is more useful than a confident narrow one that turns out wrong.

FPP3 makes the same point: a point forecast with no interval tells the reader nothing about how accurate it is likely to be.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

Two lessons. First, the bold line is almost never exactly right; even in calm years it missed by 2–5% a month on average. The band did most of the useful work. Second, bands are built from the past. When the world changes suddenly, as in 2020, no band fitted to 2012–2019 can be trusted. A good analyst says so out loud. ## The latest number is a first draft

Every forecast starts from the last data point. That point is usually the least reliable one.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Treat the latest month as preliminary, and say so">
The Census Bureau publishes retail sales about two weeks after a month ends. That first number, the **advance estimate**, comes from a sample of about 4,800 firms. It is revised as the full monthly survey comes in.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> Normal monthly revisions are small (the Census Bureau puts the average at about two-tenths of one percent), but once a year the whole history is re-benchmarked to larger annual surveys.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup>

Real example from this chapter's own data. In September 2026, FRED first reported August 2026 retail sales of **\$676.2 billion**. After the October 2026 annual revision, the same month reads **\$655.9 billion**, 3.0% lower.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> Our data end in September 2026, and that last value is still an advance estimate.

What to do:

- Write the **data vintage** next to every forecast: "based on data through September 2026 (advance estimate), downloaded November 2026."
- Do not over-react to one surprising latest month. It may be revised.
- When the next release comes out, re-run the forecast. It will move, and that is normal.

[Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#data-get-revised-vintages-and-alfred) shows how to download old vintages from ALFRED and see revisions for yourself.
</Callout>

<Callout type="math" title="What did auto.arima actually choose?">
With the bundled data, the printed output reads `ARIMA(2,0,2)(0,1,1)[12] with drift` (the exact label can change when the data are updated). Roughly, it means: compare each month with the same month last year (the seasonal `(0,1,1)[12]` part), use the last two months and the model's last two surprises to predict the next move (the `(2,0,2)` part), and add a steady upward drift. Chapter 7 decodes this notation completely. For now, the point is that a well-chosen model learns both the trend and the seasonal pattern from the data.

The bands come from the model's estimate of how big its one-month surprises are. On the log scale the 80% band is the point forecast plus or minus $1.28$ forecast standard deviations, and the 95% band plus or minus $1.96$. The standard deviation grows with the horizon, which is why the fan widens. Undoing the log ($e^x$) turns the bands back into dollars and makes them lopsided.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

## Chapter checklist

- A forecast is a **range plus a best guess**. Report the point forecast *and* the 80%/95% band, with the month and horizon.
- Read a band in words: "80% band \$706–771 billion" means a miss outside it should happen about **1 time in 5**. One miss is not a broken model.
- Bands **widen with the horizon** because surprises pile up. They also assume the future behaves like the past (remember 2020).
- **Plot before you model.** Look for trend (the drift), seasonality (the repeating shape) and surprises.
- **Check units first:** \$ millions per month, not seasonally adjusted. Watch for annual rates (SAAR) and thousands vs millions.
- With unadjusted data, compare a month with the **same month a year earlier**, not with last month.
- The **latest data point is a first draft**. Write the data vintage on every forecast.

>

**Session 1 activity: the draw-off (15 minutes).** Project the "Draw your forecast" figure. Ask every student to draw their 2023 forecast on their own device and write down their average miss after revealing.

1. Collect the numbers in a quick poll and show the distribution next to the model's error.
2. Ask the students who beat the model what they did. Usually they copied last year's seasonal pattern, which is the seasonal naive method of Chapter 3. Here that strategy genuinely wins: copying 2022 month by month misses 2023 by about \$8 billion a month on average, while the explorable's Holt-Winters model misses by about \$14 billion.
3. Then run the short lab together and change `h` and the series live.

*Expected result:* most hand-drawn forecasts miss the December peak and January drop. A flat line at the 2022 average misses by about \$25 billion a month, about twice the model's error, so smooth drawings lose. Students who copied last year's shape can beat the model. That sets up the course: models are useful because they are disciplined about patterns people overlook, and Chapter 3 shows that a simple benchmark is sometimes hard to beat.

**Session 2 activity: the 30-second forecast brief (15 minutes).** Students run the "brief your manager" exercise, then write a three-sentence brief for December 2026 with the month, unit, a comparison, the 80% band and the data vintage. Two volunteers read theirs aloud; the class checks each against the five ingredients.

1. Give pairs 8 minutes to write, 5 minutes to swap and mark each other's brief (one point per ingredient).
2. Ask: "Which ingredient did most people forget?" (Usually the vintage or the unit.)

*Expected result (R tab):* point forecast about \$738 billion; 80% band \$706–771 billion; 95% band \$690–789 billion; about 6% above December 2025 (\$694 billion). The Python tab gives about \$737 billion and \$704–771 billion. Data end September 2026 (advance estimate). In the time machine, the December 2018 forecast catches 17 of 24 months in its 80% band and 19 in its 95% band; December 2019 catches only 3 and 5.
