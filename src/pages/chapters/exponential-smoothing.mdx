<p class="lede">It is 7:30 on a Monday morning. The supply team needs next quarter's demand for every product by noon, and you have 500 of them. You cannot hand-tune 500 models. You need a method that is fast, automatic, hard to break, and accurate enough that you only have to <strong>look closely at the few forecasts that look wrong</strong>.</p>

That method is **exponential smoothing**. It is the default engine in commercial demand-planning software: SAP's Integrated Business Planning, for example, offers single, double and triple exponential smoothing plus an automated variant that picks the model and its parameters for you.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> In this chapter you will build the same machine yourself, one component at a time, and finish with a batch run over 14 U.S. retail categories that produces the planner's real deliverable: an **exception report**.

<KeyIdea>Exponential smoothing forecasts with a weighted average of the past, where the weights fade the further back you go. Add a smoothed trend and smoothed seasonal indices, and you have the workhorse of business forecasting.</KeyIdea>

## Forecasting with a fading memory

In Chapter 3 you met two extreme benchmarks. The **mean** forecast gives every past month the same weight, as if 2010 mattered as much as last month. The **naive** forecast puts all the weight on the last month and forgets everything else. Most real series need something in between: recent months should count most, but not exclusively.

<Term def="A forecasting method in which the forecast is a weighted average of past observations, with weights that shrink geometrically as observations get older.">Simple exponential smoothing</Term> (SES) does exactly that. It keeps a single number, the **level**, and updates it each month:

$$
\text{new level} = \alpha \times \text{this month's value} + (1-\alpha) \times \text{old level}
$$

The <Term def="The smoothing parameter of SES, between 0 and 1. High alpha reacts fast to new data (short memory); low alpha averages over many months (long memory).">smoothing parameter α</Term> sets how much the newest month moves the level. Unroll the update and you find that the month $j$ periods back gets weight $\alpha(1-\alpha)^j$. Those weights shrink geometrically, which is where the name "exponential" comes from. The forecast for every future month is simply the last level, so **SES forecasts are flat**.

Three things to notice as you play:

- **α is a memory dial.** At α = 0.9, 90% of the weight sits on the last month alone. At α = 0.1, it takes seven months to accumulate half the weight. Watch the cyan bars stretch back as you lower α.
- **The best α depends on the data.** The grid search finds α ≈ 1.00 for gasoline prices, 0.46 for housing starts and 0.23 for monthly inflation. Noisier series need longer memories.
- **Long memory has a price.** Set inflation's best α and look at 2021–2022 (the explorer fills the missing October 2025 CPI, which was never published because of the government shutdown, by interpolation<sup>[11](#refs)</sup>). When inflation surged, SES with α = 0.23 lagged behind for months, because it kept averaging in the calm months that came before. A low α filters noise, but it is slow to notice a genuine change.

<Callout type="math" title="Why the weights are α(1 − α)^j">
Write the level after month $t$ as $\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1}$. Substitute the same formula for $\ell_{t-1}$, then for $\ell_{t-2}$, and so on:

$$
\ell_t = \alpha y_t + \alpha(1-\alpha) y_{t-1} + \alpha(1-\alpha)^2 y_{t-2} + \cdots + (1-\alpha)^t \ell_0 .
$$

The weights $\alpha(1-\alpha)^j$ sum to (almost) 1, so the level is a weighted average. The forecast for any horizon $h$ is $\hat y_{T+h} = \ell_T$. The parameter $\alpha$ and the starting level $\ell_0$ are chosen to minimize the sum of squared one-step errors $\sum_t (y_t - \ell_{t-1})^2$, the SSE in the explorer.
</Callout>

## Adding a trend: Holt's method and the damped trend

SES is flat, so it lags behind any series that grows. Charles Holt's fix (1957) was to smooth a second number, the **slope** $b_t$, with its own parameter β. The forecast becomes a straight line: level plus $h$ times slope.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup>

That straight line is also Holt's weakness. A trend estimated today is extended forever, even though few business trends last forever. Everette Gardner and Ed McKenzie (1985) proposed a **damped trend**: multiply the slope by a factor φ (phi) slightly below 1 at each step, so the forecast bends and eventually levels off.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> With φ = 1 you get Holt's straight line back.

The three origins tell the whole story of trend forecasting. From **December 2020**, the straight line extrapolates a pandemic boom that ended within months, and damping wins easily. From **August 2023**, a gentle φ of 0.98 roughly halves the linear trend's error (about \$1.9 billion vs \$4.1 billion). From **December 2019**, every method is wrong, and the linear trend is least wrong, because the future really did accelerate. Damping is a bet that trends fade. It is usually a good bet, but not always.

<Callout type="business" title="Why damping usually wins in practice">
Gardner and McKenzie tested the damped trend on the 1,001 series of the first M-competition and found it more accurate than smoothing methods that extend a linear trend.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> Gardner's 2006 review of two decades of later studies concluded that it is "still difficult to beat the application of a damped trend to every time series."<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> In the M4 competition (100,000 series), the damped trend on its own was more accurate than either SES or Holt's linear trend, and the official benchmark, called *Comb*, was simply the average of those three methods. Only 17 of the 49 submissions did at least as well.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup> For a planner, the lesson is practical: **a damped trend is a safe default** when you cannot inspect every series.
</Callout>

<Callout type="math" title="Holt's linear and damped trend equations">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Forecast:}\quad & \hat y_{T+h} = \ell_T + (\phi + \phi^2 + \cdots + \phi^h)\, b_T \\
\text{Level:}\quad & \ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + \phi b_{t-1}) \\
\text{Slope:}\quad & b_t = \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta^*)\,\phi b_{t-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

With $\phi = 1$ this is Holt's linear method: the forecast rises by $b_T$ every period. With $\phi < 1$, the sum $\phi + \phi^2 + \cdots$ converges to $\phi/(1-\phi)$, so the forecast levels off at $\ell_T + b_T\,\phi/(1-\phi)$. That is the dashed "levels off near" line in the explorer. (R's `holt()` reports $\beta = \alpha\beta^*$; the explorer uses $\beta^*$.)
</Callout>

## Adding seasons: Holt-Winters

Retail data have a third component: the calendar. Peter Winters (1960) extended Holt's method with a set of **seasonal indices**, one per month, each smoothed with a third parameter γ (gamma).<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> There are two ways to define an index:

| Seasonality | December index means… | Use it when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Additive** | "December adds \$X million to the level" | The seasonal swing stays about the same size in dollars |
| **Multiplicative** | "December is X% above the level" | The seasonal swing grows as the business grows |

E-commerce sales are the textbook case for multiplicative seasonality. In 2001 the December bump was a few billion dollars; today it is tens of billions. Open the seasonal engine and compare the two.

At γ = 0.20, the **additive** model's December index has to climb from about +\$4.0 billion in 2001 to about +\$19.2 billion in 2025, chasing a swing that keeps growing. The **multiplicative** index stays in a narrow band, from about +43% to about +33%, and its one-step MAPE is lower (3.48% vs 4.09%). Two more things to try:

- **Set γ to 0.** The indices freeze at their 2000 values. Neither model can follow the changing calendar, and errors jump to about 6%.
- **Drag the year slider** with multiplicative indices. The holiday peak, measured in percent, has *shrunk* since the mid-2010s. December has become a smaller share of the online year, and a model with γ > 0 notices.

## ETS: the whole family, with error bars

You have now seen the three building blocks: a **level**, an optional **trend** (none, linear, or damped) and an optional **season** (none, additive, or multiplicative). Rob Hyndman and colleagues (2002) showed that each combination is equivalent to a statistical <Term def="A model in which unobserved 'states' (here: level, slope, seasonal indices) evolve over time and generate the data. It gives a likelihood, so parameters can be estimated and prediction intervals computed.">state-space model</Term> with an explicit error term.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup> They named the family **ETS**, for **E**rror, **T**rend, **S**eason, and gave each member a three-letter code:

| Letter | Options | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **E**rror | A, M | Are surprises a fixed size (additive), or a percentage of the level (multiplicative)? |
| **T**rend | N, A, Ad | None, additive (straight line), or additive damped |
| **S**eason | N, A, M | None, additive, or multiplicative |

So SES is ETS(A,N,N), Holt's method is ETS(A,A,N), and multiplicative Holt-Winters with percentage errors is ETS(M,A,M). The error letter does not change the point forecast, but it changes the **prediction intervals**: with multiplicative errors, the intervals widen as sales grow.

Having a statistical model buys you two things that the old recipes could not provide:

1. **Prediction intervals** for every forecast, computed from the model.
2. **Automatic model selection.** Every candidate has a likelihood, so `ets()` can fit them all and keep the one with the lowest <Term def="Akaike's information criterion, corrected for small samples. It rewards a model's fit (likelihood) and penalizes its number of parameters. Lower is better; only differences between models fitted to the same data matter.">AICc</Term>, without ever touching the test set.

<div class="breakout">

`ets()` picks **ETS(M,A,M)**: multiplicative errors, a straight-line trend, and multiplicative seasonality. That is the structure you would have chosen by hand from the explorers above. Its closest rival in the lab, the damped version ETS(M,Ad,M), is 12 AICc points behind; the no-trend ETS(M,N,M) is 37 behind. The estimated σ of 0.042 says a typical one-step miss is about 4%. On the test year, all 12 actual months fall inside the 95% interval, and the point forecast scores a MAPE of 2.27% and a MASE of 0.35.

<Callout type="math" title="The state-space form of ETS(M,A,M)">
Let $\mu_t = (\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})\, s_{t-m}$ be the one-step forecast and let the error be a percentage, $\varepsilon_t \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_t &= \mu_t\,(1 + \varepsilon_t) \\
\ell_t &= (\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})(1 + \alpha\varepsilon_t) \\
b_t &= b_{t-1} + \beta(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})\,\varepsilon_t \\
s_t &= s_{t-m}\,(1 + \gamma\varepsilon_t)
\end{aligned}
$$

The point forecasts are the same as multiplicative Holt-Winters, but now there is a likelihood. `ets()` maximizes it over the parameters and the initial states, then compares models with

$$
\text{AICc} = -2\log L + 2k + \frac{2k(k+1)}{n-k-1},
$$

where $k$ counts estimated parameters and initial states. Prediction intervals come from the same equations, either by formula or by simulating many future sample paths.<sup>[7, 8](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

<Callout type="business" title="Manage by exception">
Forecasting software in practice works like this report. The system forecasts everything automatically and raises an alert only when a forecast error or bias crosses a threshold. Planners spend their time on the alerts: talking to sales, checking for promotions or data errors, and overriding where they know something the model does not. Chapter 3's forecast-value-added idea applies here too: track whether those overrides actually improve accuracy.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="A green badge is not a guarantee">
The badges come from one backtest year. A category can be green because its last 12 months happened to be calm. Before you trust a status, re-run the backtest from a second origin (try ending the training data in August 2024 instead) and see whether the ranking holds. In production, teams track MASE over many weeks, not one snapshot.
</Callout>

## Chapter checklist

- Exponential smoothing is a **weighted average with fading weights**. α sets the memory: high α reacts fast, low α filters noise.
- **Holt** adds a smoothed trend (β). A **damped trend** (φ < 1) lets the trend fade, and is a hard-to-beat default.
- **Holt-Winters** adds seasonal indices (γ). Use **multiplicative** seasonality when the seasonal swing grows with the level.
- **ETS(Error, Trend, Season)** names every combination as a state-space model. That gives **prediction intervals** and **automatic selection by AICc** with `ets()`.
- **Batch forecasting** means writing one recipe and applying it to every series, then ranking by a scale-free metric (MASE) so people only review the **exceptions**.
- ETS must still beat the benchmark: on retail sales it cuts the **seasonal naive error by 30% to 50%** at every horizon under cross-validation.
