## Forecasting with a fading memory

In Chapter 3 you met two extreme benchmarks. The **mean** forecast gives every past month the same weight, as if 2010 mattered as much as last month. The **naive** forecast puts all the weight on the last month and forgets everything else. Most real series need something in between: recent months should count most, but not exclusively.

<Term def="A forecasting method in which the forecast is a weighted average of past observations, with weights that shrink geometrically as observations get older.">Simple exponential smoothing</Term> (SES) does exactly that. It keeps a single number, the **level**, and updates it each month:

$$
\text{new level} = \alpha \times \text{this month's value} + (1-\alpha) \times \text{old level}
$$

The <Term def="The smoothing parameter of SES, between 0 and 1. High alpha reacts fast to new data (short memory); low alpha averages over many months (long memory).">smoothing parameter α</Term> sets how much the newest month moves the level. Unroll the update and you find that the month $j$ periods back gets weight $\alpha(1-\alpha)^j$. Those weights shrink geometrically, which is where the name "exponential" comes from. The forecast for every future month is simply the last level, so **SES forecasts are flat**.

### Reading a fitted α: how many months really matter?

A printout that says "alpha = 0.2" means little to a manager. Translate it into **months**. Two translations are useful. The first is the number of recent months that together hold half of the weight (the explorer's "half the weight" read-out). The second is the **average age** of the information in the level: if you count last month as 1 month old, the weights $\alpha(1-\alpha)^j$ give an average age of exactly 1/α months. Robert Nau's Duke forecasting notes use this second idea, and add a practical corollary: an SES forecast tends to lag behind a turning point by roughly 1/α periods.<sup>[12](#refs)</sup>

| Fitted α | Weight on the latest month | Months holding half the weight | Average age, 1/α | What it says about the series |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.9 | 90% | 1 | 1.1 months | Almost the naive forecast. The series wanders, so last month is nearly all you need (gasoline prices), or the model is being forced to chase an outlier. |
| 0.5 | 50% | 1 | 2 months | Short memory. The fitted ETS model for e-commerce sales has α = 0.50. |
| 0.2 | 20% | 4 | 5 months | Medium memory, common for noisy monthly sales. |
| 0.1 | 10% | 7 | 10 months | Long memory. Most month-to-month moves are noise around a slowly moving level. |
| 0.02 | 2% | 35 | 50 months | Almost a fixed average. The model sees nothing but noise around a stable path. |

<KeyIdea>A fitted α describes the **series**, not the quality of the model. A high α says "recent months carry real news"; a low α says "most of the wiggles are noise". Neither is good or bad.</KeyIdea>

### One surprise, two stories

A fitted α is a compromise between two jobs that pull in opposite directions. When a month is unusual, the model cannot know whether it is a **one-off** (a bulk order, a strike, a data error) or the start of a **lasting change** (a new competitor, a price cut). Drag α below and watch both stories at once.

<div class="breakout">
<ShockResponse client:load />
</div>

At α = 0.3, a one-off spike of 60 thousand units adds 18 thousand units to next month's forecast and about 59 thousand phantom units over the following year: almost the whole spike gets ordered again, spread over 12 months. Push α down to 0.1 and the spike barely registers, but a real shift of 30 thousand units then takes 22 months to absorb 90%, and you under-forecast by about 215 thousand units in the first year. **No single α wins both games.** The fix is not to tune α by hand; it is to deal with the unusual months directly.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Outliers: fix the data, not the α">
When you **know** a month is unusual (a data error, a one-time order, a strike, the April 2020 lockdowns), handle that month instead of hoping α will cope. The fitted α shows how much damage one shock can do. For U.S. clothing-store sales trained from 2015, `ets()` picks ETS(A,N,A) with **α = 1.00**: to fit the 2020 collapse, the level has to jump to every new month, which turns it into little more than last month's level plus a fixed seasonal pattern. Replace March to June 2020 with values interpolated from the months around them and the fitted α falls to **0.71**. Train from January 2021 instead and it falls to **0.33**. (These are native R runs on the chapter's data, through August 2025.) Your options: keep the months and accept a jumpier model, start the training window after the event, or replace the unusual months with sensible values. Write down which one you chose; it is part of the forecast.
</Callout>

<Callout type="math" title="Why the weights are α(1 − α)^j">
Write the level after month $t$ as $\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)\ell_{t-1}$. Substitute the same formula for $\ell_{t-1}$, then for $\ell_{t-2}$, and so on:

$$
\ell_t = \alpha y_t + \alpha(1-\alpha) y_{t-1} + \alpha(1-\alpha)^2 y_{t-2} + \cdots + (1-\alpha)^t \ell_0 .
$$

The weights $\alpha(1-\alpha)^j$ sum to (almost) 1, so the level is a weighted average. The forecast for any horizon $h$ is $\hat y_{T+h} = \ell_T$. The parameter $\alpha$ and the starting level $\ell_0$ are chosen to minimize the sum of squared one-step errors $\sum_t (y_t - \ell_{t-1})^2$, the SSE in the explorer.
</Callout>

## Adding a trend: Holt's method and the damped trend

SES is flat, so it lags behind any series that grows. Charles Holt's fix (1957) was to smooth a second number, the **slope** $b_t$, with its own parameter β. The forecast becomes a straight line: level plus $h$ times slope.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup>

That straight line is also Holt's weakness. A trend estimated today is extended forever, even though few business trends last forever. Everette Gardner and Ed McKenzie (1985) proposed a **damped trend**: multiply the slope by a factor φ (phi) slightly below 1 at each step, so the forecast bends and eventually levels off.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> With φ = 1 you get Holt's straight line back.

The three origins tell the whole story of trend forecasting. From **December 2020**, the straight line extrapolates a pandemic boom that ended within months, and damping wins easily. From **August 2023**, a gentle φ of 0.98 roughly halves the linear trend's error (about \$1.9 billion vs \$4.1 billion). <Callout type="business" title="Why damping usually wins in practice">
Gardner and McKenzie tested the damped trend on the 1,001 series of the first M-competition and found it more accurate than smoothing methods that extend a linear trend.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> Gardner's 2006 review of two decades of later studies concluded that it is "still difficult to beat the application of a damped trend to every time series."<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> In the M4 competition (100,000 series), the damped trend on its own was more accurate than either SES or Holt's linear trend, and the official benchmark, called *Comb*, was simply the average of those three methods. Only 17 of the 49 submitted methods were more accurate than *Comb*.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup> For a planner, the lesson is practical: **a damped trend is a safe default** when you cannot inspect every series.
</Callout>

### Reading a slope and a damping factor in plain words

The **slope** $b$ is measured in the data's own units **per month**. When the ETS model you will fit in Session 2 reports a final slope of 522 for e-commerce sales (in \$ millions), it means: "underlying online sales were growing by about \$522 million a month in August 2025", or about \$6.3 billion a year before seasonal swings. Always say the unit and the period out loud.

The damping factor φ says how fast that growth fades. Think of it as a percentage that is applied every month:

- **φ = 0.98**: next month gets 98% of today's slope, the month after 96%, and so on. Half of the slope is gone after about 34 months, and in total the forecast adds about φ/(1 − φ) = 49 months' worth of today's slope before it flattens.
- **φ = 0.90**: the slope halves in under 7 months, and the forecast adds only about **9 months' worth** of today's growth in total. That is a strong opinion that the trend will not last.
- **φ = 1**: no fading at all. Holt's straight line, forever.

<Callout type="math" title="Holt's linear and damped trend equations">
$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{Forecast:}\quad & \hat y_{T+h} = \ell_T + (\phi + \phi^2 + \cdots + \phi^h)\, b_T \\
\text{Level:}\quad & \ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1-\alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + \phi b_{t-1}) \\
\text{Slope:}\quad & b_t = \beta^*(\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1-\beta^*)\,\phi b_{t-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

With $\phi = 1$ this is Holt's linear method: the forecast rises by $b_T$ every period. With $\phi < 1$, the sum $\phi + \phi^2 + \cdots$ converges to $\phi/(1-\phi)$, so the forecast levels off at $\ell_T + b_T\,\phi/(1-\phi)$. That is the dashed "levels off near" line in the explorer. (R's `holt()` reports $\beta = \alpha\beta^*$; the explorer uses $\beta^*$.)
</Callout>

## Adding seasons: Holt-Winters

Retail data have a third component: the calendar. Peter Winters (1960) extended Holt's method with a set of **seasonal indices**, one per month, each smoothed with a third parameter γ (gamma).<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> There are two ways to define an index:

| Seasonality | December index means… | Use it when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Additive** | "December adds \$X million to the level" | The seasonal swing stays about the same size in dollars |
| **Multiplicative** | "December is X% above the level" | The seasonal swing grows as the business grows |

E-commerce sales are the textbook case for multiplicative seasonality. In 2001 the December bump was a few billion dollars; today it is tens of billions. Open the seasonal engine and compare the two.

At γ = 0.20, the **additive** model's December index has to climb from about +\$4.0 billion in 2001 to about +\$19.2 billion in 2025, chasing a swing that keeps growing. The **multiplicative** index stays in a narrow band, from about +43% to about +33%, and its one-step MAPE is lower (3.48% vs 4.09%). Two more things to try:

- **Set γ to 0.** The indices freeze at their 2000 values. Neither model can follow the changing calendar, and errors jump to about 6%.
- **Drag the year slider** with multiplicative indices. The holiday peak, measured in percent, has *shrunk* since the mid-2010s. December has become a smaller share of the online year, and a model with γ > 0 notices.

**How to read a seasonal index.** A multiplicative index of **1.24** for December (e-commerce, in the ETS model of Session 2) says "December runs 24% above an average month, at whatever the level happens to be". An additive index of **+\$60.6 billion** (total retail sales) says "December adds \$60.6 billion to the level, whatever the level". An index below 1 (or below zero) marks a slow month: e-commerce's index of 0.88 for February means February sells 12% less than an average month. Both kinds of index describe the calendar *after* the level and trend are taken out, so they never contain growth.

**How to read γ.** γ sets how much of each surprise is booked into that month's seasonal index. Each index is updated only once a year, when its month comes round, so the seasonal pattern changes in yearly steps. γ near 0 means the pattern is frozen; a large γ lets each index be rebuilt mostly from the same month last year.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Multiplicative seasonality, or take logs?">
There are two ways to handle a seasonal swing that grows with the business. One is the **multiplicative** model you just used. The other is to take the **log** of sales first: as [Chapter 2](/chapters/decomposition/#additive-or-multiplicative) showed, logs turn a percentage swing into a constant-height swing, so an **additive** model fitted to log sales behaves much like a multiplicative model fitted to dollars. Either is fine; three practical rules decide:

- **Zeros or negative values** (returns, a new product's first months) rule out multiplicative seasonality *and* logs. Use an additive model on the raw data.
- **If you model logs, come back to dollars at the end** with `exp()`. The result is a median forecast, not a mean; [Chapter 10](/chapters/uncertainty/#forecasting-in-logs-and-coming-back-to-dollars) shows how to read it. The general primer on logs is in [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#logs-why-and-when).
- **Keep the scale fixed when you compare models.** AICc values on logs and on dollars cannot be compared; compare test-set errors in dollars instead.
</Callout>

## ETS: the whole family, with error bars

You have now seen the three building blocks: a **level**, an optional **trend** (none, linear, or damped) and an optional **season** (none, additive, or multiplicative). Rob Hyndman and colleagues (2002) showed that each combination is equivalent to a statistical <Term def="A model in which unobserved 'states' (here: level, slope, seasonal indices) evolve over time and generate the data. It gives a likelihood, so parameters can be estimated and prediction intervals computed.">state-space model</Term> with an explicit error term.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup> They named the family **ETS**, for **E**rror, **T**rend, **S**eason, and gave each member a three-letter code:

| Letter | Options | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **E**rror | A, M | Are surprises a fixed size (additive), or a percentage of the level (multiplicative)? |
| **T**rend | N, A, Ad | None, additive (straight line), or additive damped |
| **S**eason | N, A, M | None, additive, or multiplicative |

So SES is ETS(A,N,N), Holt's method is ETS(A,A,N), and multiplicative Holt-Winters with percentage errors is ETS(M,A,M). The error letter does not change the point forecast, but it changes the **prediction intervals**: with multiplicative errors, the intervals widen as sales grow.

Having a statistical model buys you two things that the old recipes, surveyed by Gardner (1985),<sup>[9](#refs)</sup> could not provide:

1. **Prediction intervals** for every forecast, computed from the model.
2. **Automatic model selection.** Every candidate has a likelihood, so `ets()` can fit them all and keep the one with the lowest <Term def="Akaike's information criterion, corrected for small samples. It rewards a model's fit (likelihood) and penalizes its number of parameters. Lower is better; only differences between models fitted to the same data matter.">AICc</Term>, without ever touching the test set.

<Callout type="math" title="The state-space form of ETS(M,A,M)">
Let $\mu_t = (\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})\, s_{t-m}$ be the one-step forecast and let the error be a percentage, $\varepsilon_t \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
y_t &= \mu_t\,(1 + \varepsilon_t) \\
\ell_t &= (\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})(1 + \alpha\varepsilon_t) \\
b_t &= b_{t-1} + \beta(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1})\,\varepsilon_t \\
s_t &= s_{t-m}\,(1 + \gamma\varepsilon_t)
\end{aligned}
$$

The point forecasts are the same as multiplicative Holt-Winters, but now there is a likelihood. `ets()` maximizes it over the parameters and the initial states, then compares models with

$$
\text{AIC} = -2\log L + 2k, \qquad \text{AICc} = \text{AIC} + \frac{2k(k+1)}{n-k-1}.
$$

These are Chapter 4's formulas. There, $-2\log L$ reduced to $T\log(\text{SSE}/T)$ plus a constant, and $k + 2$ counted the slopes, the intercept and the error variance. Here $k$ counts every estimated quantity: the smoothing parameters, the initial states and the error variance. Prediction intervals come from the same equations, either by formula or by simulating many future sample paths.<sup>[7, 8, 10](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

| `beta` | 0.018 | "The slope learns slowly. Only about 4% of each surprise in the level (β/α = 0.018/0.50) is passed on to the slope, so the slope reflects roughly the last two years of growth." |
| `gamma` | 0.47 | "The seasonal pattern adapts quickly. γ is close to its ceiling of 1 − α = 0.50, so each month's index is rebuilt mostly from the same month last year." |
| `sigma` | 0.042 | "A typical one-step miss is about 4% of sales." (With additive errors, σ is in dollars instead.) |
| `AICc` | 2,659 | "On its own, nothing. Only the *difference* from other models fitted to the same data matters." |

R reports β on a scale where it can never exceed α, and γ on a scale where it can never exceed 1 − α. That is why the translations above divide by α or compare with 1 − α.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Read an AICc table like a referee">
- **Look at differences, not levels.** Subtract the best AICc from each model's. A common rule of thumb: under 2 is a tie, 4 to 7 is considerably less support, and more than 10 means essentially none.<sup>[13](#refs)</sup>
- **Compare only like with like:** the same series, the same training window, the same transformation. AICc on logs vs dollars, or on 2015–2025 vs 2021–2025, are different contests.
- **When two models tie, prefer the more cautious one** (usually the damped trend), or report both forecasts side by side.
- **AICc is an in-sample referee.** It never sees the test set. In e-commerce, ETS(M,A,A) is 52 points behind, yet its test-year MAE was slightly *smaller* (\$2.48 billion vs \$2.58 billion). One test year is a noisy referee too; that is why the chapter also cross-validates.
</Callout>

## Choosing the training window: COVID months, outliers and short histories

`ets()` will fit whatever data you hand it. Choosing **which months** to hand it is your job, and it often matters as much as the model. The COVID months of 2020 are the clearest case: retail categories that collapsed and rebounded within weeks leave a scar on every model trained through them. Hyndman and Rostami-Tabar (2025) list the main options for such interrupted series: use a model that adapts fast, treat the disrupted months as missing, replace them with what would plausibly have happened, give them less weight, or combine several approaches.<sup>[14](#refs)</sup> With exponential smoothing, the two simplest are **starting the window after the disruption** and **replacing the disrupted months**.

<Callout type="empirical" title="COVID months make a model jumpy and its intervals too wide">
Clothing stores again (\$ millions, NSA, trained through August 2025). From 2015, `ets()` picks ETS(A,N,A) with α = 1.00 and **σ = \$1,740 million**. From 2021, it picks the same structure with α = 0.33 and **σ = \$876 million**. Because prediction intervals are built from σ, the 2015 model's intervals are about **twice as wide**, all because of a few months in 2020. If your intervals look absurdly wide, look for an outlier in the training window before blaming the model.
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="A shorter window is not free">
Dropping years also drops information about the seasonal pattern. For total retail sales (excluding food services), training from 2015 gives a test-year MAE of about \$14.1 billion a month; training only from January 2022 raises it to about \$21.5 billion. Rules of thumb: keep as many full years as still describe today's business (each December the model sees helps it pin down December), start **after** a disruption rather than in the middle of one, and always compare the choices on the **same test months, in dollars**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="New products and short histories">
A new product has no history to smooth. With only 20 months of e-commerce data (January 2023 to August 2024), `ets()` picks **ETS(M,N,N)**: there are too few observations to estimate 12 seasonal indices, so it quietly drops the season. With 32 months it can fit ETS(M,N,M). In practice, until a product has about two full years of sales, planners use a simple non-seasonal model, apply the seasonal pattern of a similar established product by judgment, and refit every month as data arrive. Say clearly in the report that the forecast rests on a short history.
</Callout>

### Reading the exception report like a manager

A planner who receives the report reads each flagged row with four questions. Here they are, answered with the R numbers.

1. **How bad, in plain words?** Misc. store retailers have MASE 1.98: the forecast missed by about **twice** what a seasonal naive forecast typically misses in the training years. Their MAPE of 9.0% says the same thing in percent: a typical month was off by about 9%.
2. **Bias or noise?** Look at the *direction* of the misses, not just their size. Misc. store sales came in **above** the forecast in all 12 test months (by \$1.39 billion a month on average). That is bias: the level and slope are behind a real change, and the fix is to raise them, not to wait. Health & personal care missed the other way, below the forecast in 9 of 12 months.
3. **How much money?** MASE is scale-free, which is what you want for *ranking*, but decisions are made in dollars. Gasoline stations sit at the line (MASE 1.00), yet their MAE of **\$5.6 billion a month** is the largest dollar miss in the table, with every month under-forecast. Food services is green (MASE 0.33), but its MAE of \$2.1 billion is still larger than misc. stores' \$1.4 billion, simply because the category is six times bigger.
4. **What changed, and does the model know?** A model can only extrapolate what it has seen. A new competitor, a price change or a reclassification is information the planner has and the model does not.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Rank by MASE, then act on dollars and direction">
Use MASE to decide **which rows to read**, because it puts an \$8 billion category and a \$90 billion category on one scale. Then decide **what to do** from two more numbers per category: the average error in dollars (how much is at stake) and the count of months above vs below the forecast (bias or noise). A row with all 12 misses on one side calls for an override or a shorter training window; a row with misses on both sides calls for patience and wider safety stock.
</Callout>

## Chapter checklist

- Exponential smoothing is a **weighted average with fading weights**. Translate α into months: the level's information is on average **1/α months old**, so α = 0.5 means about 2 months and α = 0.1 about 10.
- A fitted α describes the **series** (how much of each wiggle is news), not the model's accuracy. No α handles both one-off outliers and lasting shifts, so **deal with known outliers in the data** instead of tuning α.
- **Holt** adds a slope, measured in **units per month**. A **damped trend** fades it: with φ = 0.9 the forecast adds only about 9 months' worth of today's slope in total. It is a hard-to-beat default.
- **Holt-Winters** adds seasonal indices: multiplicative ("December is 24% above an average month") when the swing grows with the level, additive ("+\$60.6 billion") when it does not. An additive model on **logs** is the alternative to multiplicative; come back to dollars with `exp()`.
- **ETS(Error, Trend, Season)** names every combination as a state-space model, with **prediction intervals** and **automatic selection by AICc**. Read AICc as **differences**: under 2 is a tie, over 10 is ruled out, and only models fitted to the same data can be compared.
- **Read the states** as a receipt: level (underlying sales) plus slope, times or plus the seasonal index, equals the forecast.
- **Choose the training window on purpose.** COVID months can push α to 1 and double σ. A shorter window can help or hurt, so compare windows in **dollars on the same test months**, never by MASE. Seasonal models need a few full years; new products need judgment.
- **Batch forecasting** ranks by a scale-free metric (MASE) so people only review the **exceptions**; then the manager acts on **dollars and direction** (bias vs noise).
- ETS must still beat the benchmark: on retail sales it cuts the **seasonal naive error by about 30% to 50%** at every horizon under cross-validation.
