<p class="lede">Your model says U.S. retail sales will jump from \$621 billion in November to \$690 billion in December. Your manager asks the obvious question: <strong>why?</strong> "Because the algorithm said so" ends the meeting badly. This chapter builds one of the most successful forecasting algorithms of the last decade, gradient boosting, and then takes it apart so that every forecast comes with a reason, in dollars.</p>

In [Chapter 11](/chapters/random-forests/) you turned a time series into a table of features and grew a random forest. A forest grows hundreds of deep trees *independently* and averages them. Boosting does the opposite: it grows small trees *one after another*, and each new tree studies the mistakes of all the trees before it. That idea, implemented in libraries such as XGBoost and LightGBM, sat behind the top entries of the 2020 M5 forecasting competition, which was won by a senior undergraduate student with an average of LightGBM models.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>

<KeyIdea>Boosting builds a forecast from many small corrections. SHAP values take the forecast apart again into those same kinds of pieces: a starting point plus one push per feature, adding up exactly to the number you report.</KeyIdea>

## Boosting: small trees that fix each other's mistakes

A <Term def="A model only slightly better than guessing on its own, such as a decision tree with one or two splits.">weak learner</Term> is a model too simple to be useful alone: a tree that asks one or two yes/no questions. <Term def="An ensemble method that fits weak learners in sequence, each one trained on the errors (residuals) left by the ones before it.">Boosting</Term> turns a crowd of weak learners into a strong one in four steps:

1. Start with a dull forecast: the average monthly growth, the same for every month.
2. Compute the <Term def="What the model got wrong: actual minus prediction.">residuals</Term>, the misses.
3. Fit a small tree to the residuals. It finds the months the current model gets most wrong ("December is badly under-predicted").
4. Add only a fraction of that tree's correction, the <Term def="The share of each new tree's correction that is added to the model. Also called shrinkage or eta. Small values learn slowly and generalize better.">learning rate</Term>, then go back to step 2.

In plain words: boosting is **learning from the residuals**. Picture a sales team revising a forecast. The first analyst says "an average month". The second looks only at where the first was wrong and says "but Decembers are much higher". The third looks at what is *still* wrong and says "and Januaries fall". Nobody redoes the whole forecast; each one fixes part of what is left over. The learning rate is how much each analyst is allowed to change: a cautious team accepts only 10% of every suggested fix and lets the next analyst refine it.

Below, a small boosting engine written in JavaScript runs on the real retail-sales table: the target is this month's growth over last month, and the features are the calendar month, last year's growth in the same month, last month's growth, last month's 12-month growth and the number of weekend days. It trains on 1993 to 2022 and forecasts each held-out month from January 2023 one month ahead.

Three things to notice as you drag:

- **Tree 0 misses every December.** With no trees, every month gets the average growth of about 0.95%, so the held-out error (RMSE) is \$49.4 billion. The very first trees split on the calendar month or on last year's growth in the same month. They find the December peak and the January drop.

This is why practitioners use a small learning rate plus <Term def="Stopping the addition of trees when the error on a validation set has not improved for a set number of rounds.">early stopping</Term>.

### Reading the learning-rate and tree-count dials

The two settings you will touch most are the learning rate and the number of trees, and they are not separate choices. A smaller learning rate takes smaller steps, so it needs more trees to travel the same distance. A useful way to think about it: **trees × learning rate is the learning budget**, roughly how much of the training data's pattern the model has absorbed.<sup>[11](#refs)</sup>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Set the learning rate, let early stopping choose the trees">
Pick a small learning rate (0.05 to 0.1) and treat the number of trees as an output, not an input: let early stopping on a **later** validation period choose it, as Lab 12.1 does. Then sanity-check the result with the budget rule. Lab 12.1's early stopping picks 78 trees at rate 0.05, a budget of about 4; Lab 12.2's tuning lands on 38 trees at rate 0.1, again about 4. Had a rerun at rate 0.1 asked for 400 trees, the right reaction would be to ask what changed (the data, the features, a leak), not to celebrate a smarter model. Report the learning rate and the number of trees together; one without the other means little.
</Callout>

<Callout type="math" title="Gradient boosting is gradient descent in function space">
Write the model after $m$ trees as $F_m(x)$. Boosting updates it as

$$
F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \eta\, h_m(x),
$$

where $\eta$ is the learning rate and $h_m$ is a small tree. Friedman (2001) showed which target $h_m$ should be fitted to: the **negative gradient** of the loss with respect to the current prediction.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> For squared error $L = \tfrac12 (y - F)^2$, that gradient is $-(y - F_{m-1}(x))$, so the negative gradient is exactly the residual. "Fit the next tree to the residuals" is therefore one step of gradient descent, taken not on a parameter but on the whole prediction function. Change the loss (absolute error, pinball loss for quantiles, log loss for recession probabilities) and the same recipe still works. XGBoost (Chen & Guestrin, 2016) adds a second-order (Newton) step and a penalty $\lambda$ on leaf values, and it learns a default direction for missing values at every split.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

### Why the target is growth, not levels

Lab 12.1 predicts **growth**, this month's percentage change over last month, and converts it back to dollars with *forecast = last month × (1 + growth/100)*. [Chapter 11's extrapolation trap](/chapters/random-forests/#the-extrapolation-trap) showed why trees need that: a leaf answers with values built from months it has seen, so a tree trained on sales *levels* cannot forecast a record. Boosting adds trees together, but in practice it hits the same ceiling. The choice of target matters for a second reason in this chapter: it sets the **units and the starting point of every explanation**.

<Callout type="empirical" title="The target's units become the explanation's units">
SHAP values are always in the units of what the model predicts, measured from the model's average prediction. Choose the target with the explanation in mind:
<ul>
<li><strong>Growth in %</strong> (this chapter): pushes are in percentage points of growth. Multiply by last month's sales / 100 to get dollars: on November 2025's \$620.6 billion, 1 point of growth is worth \$6.2 billion.</li>
<li><strong>Level in \$</strong>: pushes are already in dollars, but measured from a 30-year average, and the trees cannot forecast a record.</li>
<li><strong>Log of sales</strong>: pushes are in log points, roughly percent. Converted back to dollars they <em>multiply</em> instead of adding, so convert the total, not each bar.</li>
</ul>
Growth rates themselves are taught in [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#transformations-every-analyst-uses). Whatever you choose, write the unit on every chart and every slide.
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Tune on rolling folds only, and live with what they pick">
Look again at the two XGBoost results. If you had chosen settings by their score on the 2023–2026 test months, you would pick the depth-3 model (MAE \$9.21 billion) and report that number. But you could not have known it in January 2023: the test months are the future. The rolling folds picked depth 2, and \$10.35 billion is the honest number. Three rules:
<ul>
<li><strong>Every validation block comes after its training block</strong> in time, as in [Chapter 3's time-series cross-validation](/chapters/benchmarks/#one-test-period-is-not-enough-time-series-cross-validation). A random 20% of months lets the model learn from months on both sides of the one it is judged on.</li>
<li><strong>Several folds, not one.</strong> One validation year can be odd (2020 was). Averaging over 2019–2022 is noisy too, but less fooled by any single year.</li>
<li><strong>Touch the test set once.</strong> After you look at test scores, any further tuning turns the test set into a validation set, and you need a new test period to report.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

<Callout type="business" title="When is boosting worth it?">
On one long, clean, seasonal series, a well-specified statistical model is hard to beat. Boosting earns its keep when there are <strong>many related series</strong> to learn from, <strong>many drivers</strong> (prices, promotions, holidays, weather) whose effects interact, and <strong>short or noisy histories</strong>. That describes a retailer forecasting thousands of products, which is exactly the M5 setting. You will see the many-series case at the end of this chapter.
</Callout>

## Explaining one forecast: SHAP values

Suppose four colleagues produced a forecast together and you must split the credit fairly. In 1953 Lloyd Shapley answered the general version of that question for cooperative games: give each player their **marginal contribution**, averaged over every order in which the team could have been assembled.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> Lundberg and Lee (2017) applied the idea to model predictions, with features as the players and the prediction as the payout, and called the result <Term def="SHapley Additive exPlanations: each feature's contribution to one prediction, measured as its Shapley value. Contributions plus the base value add up exactly to the prediction.">SHAP values</Term>.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup>

For any single forecast, SHAP gives you an additive receipt:

> **forecast** = **base value** + push from the calendar month + push from last year's growth + … + push from last month's unemployment rate

The <Term def="The model's average prediction over the training data: where every explanation starts before any feature has been taken into account.">base value</Term> is the model's average prediction. Each push (a SHAP value) is how far that feature moved *this* forecast away from the average, up or down. Our model predicts growth, and the dollar forecast is last month's sales × (1 + growth / 100). Multiplying every term by the same number keeps the receipt additive, so we can state every push in billions of dollars.

Read December 2025 from the top. November's sales were \$620.6 billion. An average month adds \$5.8 billion (the base value). Last year's November-to-December jump of 9.4% tells the model a big jump is coming again: **+\$47.3 billion**. The calendar month adds another \$13.2 billion, and the weekend mix \$2.2 billion. The forecast lands at \$689.6 billion; actual sales were \$694.1 billion, a miss of 0.6%. Now pick **Next month**: for October 2026, the biggest pushes are last October's 4.7% growth (+\$9.1 billion), the calendar month (+\$5.5 billion), and a bounce-back after September's 3.2% drop (+\$4.5 billion), for a forecast of \$659.0 billion.

**How to say it.** Every bar has the same sentence shape: *"Because [feature] was [value], the model moved the forecast [up/down] by \$X billion compared with an average month."* For December 2025: "Because last December jumped 9.4%, the model expects a similar jump, which adds \$47.3 billion." Notice what the sentence does *not* say: it does not say that last year's December *caused* this one, and it does not say what would happen if the feature were different. It describes the model's arithmetic for one forecast.

<Callout type="math" title="How XGBoost computes exact SHAP values">
The Shapley value of feature $j$ in a model with feature set $N$ is

$$
\phi_j = \sum_{S \subseteq N \setminus \{j\}} \frac{|S|!\,(|N| - |S| - 1)!}{|N|!}\Big[f_{S \cup \{j\}}(x) - f_S(x)\Big],
$$

where $f_S(x)$ is the model's expected prediction when only the features in $S$ are known. The weights count how many orderings put exactly the features in $S$ before $j$. With 7 features there are $2^6 = 64$ subsets per feature; with 100 features the sum is hopeless. Lundberg and Lee's **Kernel SHAP** estimates these values for any model by sampling feature subsets and fitting a weighted regression: general, but slow and approximate.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup> For tree ensembles, **TreeSHAP** computes them exactly, in time polynomial in the number of leaves and the depth, by pushing all subsets through each tree at once.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> It defines $f_S(x)$ by following the tree where a feature in $S$ decides the split and averaging both branches, weighted by training cover, where it does not. That is why correlated features can share credit in ways that depend on how the trees happened to split. XGBoost has TreeSHAP built in: `predict(..., predcontrib = TRUE)` in R and `predict(..., pred_contribs=True)` in Python. The four properties that make Shapley values the unique "fair" split are efficiency (they add up to the prediction), symmetry, dummy (a feature the model never uses gets zero) and additivity across models.
</Callout>

- **Seasonality dominates.** Last year's growth in the same month accounts for 66% of the total absolute SHAP across all months since 2005, and the calendar month for another 19%. High values (red) push right, low values (blue) push left.
- **Two features, one story.** Switch to the dependence view for the calendar month: January is pushed down by about 7.5 points of growth, December up by about 2. But the bigger December push sits on *last year's growth*, which carries the same seasonal information. When features are correlated, SHAP splits the credit between them in a way that depends on how the trees happened to split. Do not read too much into the split; read the sum.
- **A surprise.** The dependence plot for weekend days slopes *down*: months with 10 weekend days get about −0.6 points on average, months with 8 get about +0.4. Is that because people shop less on weekends, or because of something else that happens to line up with the calendar? The model cannot tell you. SHAP explains the **model**, not the economy.

### Correlated features split the credit

Calendar month and last year's growth in the same month carry nearly the same information: both say "it is December". How much credit each one gets depends on which of them the trees happened to split on, a well-known weakness of SHAP and of most other explanation methods.<sup>[12](#refs)</sup> To see how fragile that split is, refit the tuned model four more times with one common XGBoost setting changed: each tree may look at only a random half of the features (`colsample_bytree = 0.5`), with a different random seed each time.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Three ways SHAP explanations mislead">
<ul>
<li><strong>Explanation is not causation.</strong> A large SHAP value means the model leaned on that feature, not that changing the feature would change sales. "Unemployment pushed the forecast down \$2 billion" is a statement about the model.</li>
<li><strong>Correlated features share credit unpredictably.</strong> Calendar month and last year's growth both encode seasonality. Refit with a different seed and the credit moves from one to the other far more than the forecast does (see the refits above).</li>
<li><strong>Explanations are only as good as the model.</strong> SHAP will explain a bad forecast just as confidently as a good one. Always show accuracy next to the explanation.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Sanity-check every explanation against the economics">
Before an explanation goes on a slide, ask four questions of it.
<ul>
<li><strong>Is the sign sensible?</strong> January's calendar push is large and negative (−\$47.6 billion in January 2023): shoppers spend less right after the holidays. Good. A bounce-back after a weak month (+\$4.5 billion for October 2026, after September fell 3.2%) is plausible for unadjusted monthly data. The weekend-days push slopes <em>down</em>: that one needs a story you can defend, or a warning.</li>
<li><strong>Is the size plausible?</strong> Turn each push into a share of the month's sales. Weekend days lowering November 2025 by \$4.2 billion is about 0.7% of a \$620 billion month: small enough to be believable, even if the story behind it is unclear. A macro feature moving one month's forecast by \$40 billion would need a recession-sized story.</li>
<li><strong>Is it stable?</strong> Refit with another seed or another year of data. Pushes that flip sign or swap between correlated features are not findings.</li>
<li><strong>Is it missing something you expected?</strong> Consumer sentiment gets exactly zero. That is a finding about this model and this target (one month ahead, seasonality known), not proof that confidence never matters for spending.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

## SHAP, coefficients and importance: three different questions

You now have three ways to talk about "what matters" in a model, and they answer different questions. Mixing them up is a common and costly interpretation mistake.

| | Regression coefficient ([Chapter 4](/chapters/regression/#reading-a-regression-like-an-analyst)) | Gain importance (Lab 12.1) | SHAP value (Lab 12.3) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question it answers | How does the prediction change per one-unit change in X, holding the others fixed? | Which features did the trees split on most profitably during training? | How far did each feature move *this* forecast from the average forecast? |
| Units | Target units per unit of X | Share of training-loss reduction (no units) | Target units (here, points of growth, or \$ after conversion) |
| Direction | Yes (sign) | No | Yes, month by month |
| One number for… | The whole model (same for every month) | The whole model | Every forecast and every feature |
| Our model | Not available: trees have no coefficients | Last year's growth 66%, calendar month 30% | Mean absolute SHAP over the test months: last year's growth \$27.1bn, calendar \$8.9bn, last month's growth \$3.2bn, weekend days \$2.6bn |

Here the two importance rankings agree, but only SHAP is in the forecast's own units, has a direction, and can be broken down month by month. Split-based importance can also favor features with many distinct values. For a manager, use SHAP.

  question="A linear regression forecasts growth with a coefficient of **0.5** on last year's growth in the same month. On average, last year's growth is 1%. This month, last year's growth was 10%. What is this feature's SHAP value for this month?"
  options={['0.5 points: the coefficient', '5 points: 0.5 × 10', '4.5 points: 0.5 × (10 − 1)', 'It cannot be computed for a linear model']}
  answer={2}
  explain="For a linear model, a feature's SHAP value is its coefficient times how far the feature is from its average: 0.5 × (10 − 1) = 4.5 points. The coefficient is the slope; SHAP is the slope applied to this month's unusual value. That is why a feature with a big coefficient can have a SHAP value near zero in a month when it sits at its average."
/>

<Callout type="math" title="SHAP for a linear model">
For a linear model $\hat y = b_0 + \sum_j \beta_j x_j$ with features treated as independent, the Shapley value of feature $j$ is

$$
\phi_j = \beta_j \,\big(x_j - \bar x_j\big),
$$

and the base value is the average prediction, $\phi_0 = b_0 + \sum_j \beta_j \bar x_j$.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup> Adding them up gives back $\hat y$ exactly. So SHAP generalizes "coefficient × deviation from average" to models, like boosted trees, that have no single coefficient: a tree's push for the same change in $x_j$ can differ from month to month, because it depends on the other features too.
</Callout>

## What the M competitions teach

The M competitions, organized by Spyros Makridakis since 1982, are the forecasting field's large-scale reality checks. Three recent ones bear on this chapter:

- **M4 (2018), 100,000 series.** The winner was a hybrid of exponential smoothing and a neural network (Smyl), and the runner-up used XGBoost to *weight* the forecasts of standard statistical methods. Both used machine learning, and both leaned on statistical, series-by-series building blocks.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup>
- **M5 (2020), 42,840 series of Walmart unit sales.** 5,507 teams from 101 countries took part. Almost all of the top 50 entries that described their methods used LightGBM, a fast gradient-boosting library.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> <sup>[8](#refs)</sup> The winning entry, an equal-weighted average of LightGBM models built by an undergraduate, was 22.4% more accurate than the best benchmark (bottom-up exponential smoothing). Among the organizers' findings: simple ML methods were superior, combining helped, cross-learning helped, explanatory variables such as prices and promotions helped, and effective cross-validation strategies mattered.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>
- **M6 (2022 to 2023), 50 S&P 500 stocks and 50 ETFs.** Over twelve four-week rounds, 226 teams forecast returns and made investment decisions. The organizers stressed how hard it was to beat the market consistently, and found only a weak link between forecasting accuracy and investment returns.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup>

<Callout type="intuition" title="What this chapter's numbers add">
Your results echo the competitions' pattern. On one series, boosting did not beat ETS, but a combination helped. Across 14 related series with short histories, one global boosted model clearly beat 14 local ones (an M5-style result). The method that wins depends on the data you have, which is why you keep a benchmark scorecard.
</Callout>

## Chapter checklist

- Boosting is **learning from the residuals**: small trees fitted **in sequence**, each to what the model so far still gets wrong, with only a fraction (the **learning rate**) of each one added.
- Read the learning rate and the number of trees **together**: trees × learning rate is the learning budget. Halve the rate, roughly double the trees. Small rates give a wide, forgiving valley; let **early stopping on a later period** pick the trees.
- Tune on **rolling folds** only, touch the test set once, and report the honest number even when a setting you rejected would have scored better.
- Predict **growth, not levels**: trees cannot forecast a record, and the target's units become the explanation's units. Convert growth pushes to dollars with *last month's sales / 100*.
- On one clean series, boosting may not beat ETS; a **combination** often beats both. Boosting shines with **many related series** (global models) and rich drivers.
- **SHAP values** split each forecast into a base value plus one push per feature, adding up exactly. Say them in the forecast's units: "because last December jumped 9.4%, the model adds \$47.3 billion compared with an average month".
- SHAP ≠ coefficient ≠ importance: a coefficient is a slope, gain importance is a training statistic, SHAP is this forecast's push in the forecast's units.
- SHAP explains the **model**, not the world. Report **correlated features as a group**, check signs, sizes and stability against the economics, and never present a push as a causal effect.
- Explain a forecast in **three sentences**: the number, the reasons in dollars, and how far to trust it.
