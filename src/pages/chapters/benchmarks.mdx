<p class="lede">A vendor shows you a slick AI forecasting tool. Its chart looks impressive. Before you sign anything, ask one question: <strong>is it more accurate than simply assuming this month will look like the same month last year?</strong> You will be surprised how often the answer is no.</p>

In 2018 the M4 forecasting competition pitted 61 forecasting methods against each other on 100,000 real time series.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> The six "pure" machine-learning entries did poorly: **none** was more accurate than a simple average of three exponential-smoothing methods, and only one beat a seasonally adjusted naive forecast.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> Sophistication is not the same as accuracy. This chapter gives you the tools to tell them apart, and, just as important, to **read** an accuracy number the way a careful analyst does.

<KeyIdea>Never judge a forecast on its own. Judge it against a simple benchmark, on data the model has never seen.</KeyIdea>

## Four benchmarks

A <Term def="A deliberately simple forecasting rule used as a yardstick. A model that cannot beat it adds no value.">benchmark</Term> is a forecasting rule so simple it needs no statistics. Four of them cover almost every situation:

| Method | Rule | R function | Good when… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mean** | Forecast = average of all past values | `meanf()` | The series fluctuates around a stable level |
| **Naive** | Forecast = last observed value | `naive()` | The series wanders like a stock price |
| **Seasonal naive** | Forecast = value from the same season last year | `snaive()` | There is a strong seasonal pattern |
| **Drift** | Draw a line from the first to the last observation and extend it | `rwf(drift = TRUE)` | There is a steady trend |

Play with them on real U.S. data. Pick a series, then drag the **forecast origin**: you are choosing the date you stand on when making the forecast. Everything to the right is hidden from the methods and used only to score them.

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why the naive forecast is so hard to beat">
Many economic series behave like a <em>random walk</em>: tomorrow's value is today's value plus an unpredictable shock. For a pure random walk, the best possible forecast <strong>is</strong> the last value. That's why exchange rates and stock prices are notoriously hard to forecast. Any model that claims to beat naive on such series should make you suspicious.
</Callout>

## The golden rule: split by time

To score a forecast fairly we need data the model has never seen. With time series, the test set must come **after** the training set:

<div class="split-demo" aria-hidden="true">
  <div class="sd-train">Training set: the model learns from this</div>
  <div class="sd-test">Test set: hidden, used only for scoring</div>
</div>

Why so strict? Because in real life you forecast from today, and tomorrow is unknown. A test that lets the model see *any* month after the one it is predicting is a test of a situation that never happens. Machine-learning tutorials usually split data at random, so it is worth seeing how badly that goes with time series.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Look-ahead leakage">
Leakage means letting information from the future sneak into the forecast. It makes a model look brilliant on paper and useless in practice. Three common ways it happens:
<ul>
<li>Splitting the data <strong>randomly</strong> (as in standard machine learning), so the model trains on 2024 to "predict" 2023. The explorer above shows how large the illusion can be.</li>
<li>Using a predictor whose value <strong>wasn't known</strong> at forecast time, such as this month's unemployment rate to forecast this month's retail sales.</li>
<li>Choosing your model by peeking at the test set, then reporting the test score as if it were honest.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

## Keeping score: error metrics

Once we have forecasts $\hat y$ and the actual values $y$ for the test period, each forecast error is $e_t = y_t - \hat y_t$, **actual minus forecast**. A positive error means the forecast was too low. The question is how to summarize a list of errors in one number. Each metric answers a slightly different question, and choosing between them is a business decision as much as a statistical one.

Drag the purple forecast dots below and watch the four metrics react.

<div class="breakout">
<ErrorPlayground client:load />
</div>

| Metric | How it is computed | In plain English | Units | Watch out |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ME** (bias) | Average of the errors | Do we miss high or low on average? | Data's units | Positive and negative errors cancel |
| **MAE** | Average of the error sizes (signs dropped) | How big is a typical miss? | Data's units | Can't compare across series with different units |
| **RMSE** | Square the errors, average, take the square root | Like MAE, but big misses count much more | Data's units | Sensitive to a few outliers |
| **MAPE** | Average of each miss as a % of the actual value | Miss as a % of the actual value | Percent | Explodes when the actual value is near zero; favors forecasts that are too low |
| **MASE** | MAE ÷ the in-sample MAE of seasonal naive | Below 1 = smaller errors than seasonal naive made on the training data | None (a ratio) | Slightly harder to explain to a manager |

<Callout type="math" title="The five metrics in formulas">
With $n$ test errors $e_t = y_t - \hat y_t$:

$$
\text{ME} = \frac{1}{n}\sum e_t, \qquad \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum \lvert e_t\rvert, \qquad \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum e_t^2}, \qquad \text{MAPE} = \frac{100}{n}\sum \left\lvert \frac{e_t}{y_t}\right\rvert .
$$

MASE divides the MAE by the in-sample MAE of the seasonal naive forecast; its full formula is in the MASE box below.
</Callout>

## Reading accuracy numbers in plain words

A metric is only useful if you can say what it means in one sentence, with units. Here is how to read each one, using the retail-sales scorecard you will build in a moment (test period January 2023 onward, data in millions of dollars).

| Method | ME | MAE | RMSE | MAPE | MASE |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mean | 187,151 | 187,151 | 191,431 | 30.8% | 7.87 |
| Naive | −36,841 | 44,208 | 54,565 | 7.7% | 1.86 |
| Seasonal naive | 22,174 | 24,824 | 31,168 | 4.1% | 1.04 |
| Drift | −91,002 | 91,002 | 97,575 | 15.5% | 3.83 |

### ME: the sign tells you which way you miss

Seasonal naive has ME = **+22,174**: on average it was **too low** by about \$22 billion a month. Read the sign carefully. Because an error is actual minus forecast, a **positive ME means under-forecasting** and a negative ME means over-forecasting. Drift's ME of −91,002 means it was too high by about \$91 billion a month on average.

Now compare ME with MAE. When |ME| is close to MAE, nearly every miss goes the same way. For drift, ME = −91,002 and MAE = 91,002 exactly: drift was too high in **every** test month. That is a fixable problem (the forecast is biased), and it is invisible if you only report MAE.

### MAE and RMSE: the typical miss and the big misses

MAE is the easiest number to say out loud: "seasonal naive misses retail sales by about **\$25 billion a month**, on average." RMSE is always **at least as large** as MAE, because squaring makes big errors count more than small ones. They are equal only if every miss has the same size. So the **ratio RMSE ÷ MAE** is a quick reading of how uneven the misses are:

- close to **1.0**: all misses are about the same size (drift: 1.07, mean: 1.02, both steadily wrong in one direction);
- around **1.25**: what you would expect from ordinary bell-shaped errors (seasonal naive: 1.26);
- well **above 1.3**: a few very large misses dominate. Find them before you trust the average.

<Callout type="math" title="Why RMSE ≥ MAE, and why 1.25 is the bell-curve value">
RMSE is the square root of the mean of $e_t^2$; MAE is the mean of $|e_t|$. Since the mean of squares is never smaller than the square of the mean, $\frac{1}{n}\sum e_t^2 \ge \left(\frac{1}{n}\sum |e_t|\right)^2$, so RMSE ≥ MAE, with equality only if all $|e_t|$ are equal. If the errors are normal with mean 0 and standard deviation $\sigma$, then RMSE ≈ $\sigma$ and MAE ≈ $\sigma\sqrt{2/\pi}$, so RMSE ÷ MAE ≈ $\sqrt{\pi/2} \approx 1.25$.
</Callout>

### MAPE: easy to say, easy to fool

"We are 4.1% off" needs no explanation, which is why managers love MAPE. But it divides each miss by the actual value, and that causes two problems.

1. **Near zero it explodes.** Divide a tiny miss by a tiny actual value and you get a big percentage. In the lab below, the naive forecast of the federal funds rate missed by only about 0.01 percentage points a month in 2010–15, when the rate sat near 0.1%. That is about nine times smaller than its misses in 2004–07, yet its MAPE was **more than twice as large** (10.3% vs 3.9%). For series that can be zero or negative (net exports, profits, growth rates), MAPE is meaningless.
2. **It is lopsided.** A forecast that is too low can miss by at most 100% (forecast 0), but a forecast that is too high can miss by 200%, 500%, anything. So MAPE quietly rewards forecasts that err on the **low** side.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>

For comparison, MAE is lowest for any forecast between 3 and 4 (the median) and RMSE for 3.5 (the mean). Judge forecasters on MAPE alone and they learn to forecast low, which empties shelves. ### MASE: below 1 or above 1

MASE divides the test MAE by the typical miss that seasonal naive made **inside the training data** (for monthly data: the average size of a year-over-year change). Its units cancel, so a MASE of 0.6 means the same thing for dollars, homes or percentage points.

- **MASE below 1**: the forecast missed by less, on average, than the benchmark's typical in-sample miss. Drift on e-commerce sales scores 0.47: about half.
- **MASE above 1**: it missed by more. Seasonal naive on retail sales scores 1.04: its test errors were about 4% larger than its own typical in-sample miss, because sales grew faster after 2022 than before.
- MASE above 1 does **not** automatically mean "worse than seasonal naive on this test": seasonal naive itself scores 1.04 here, and it is the best of the four. Compare MASEs of different methods **on the same test window**.

<Callout type="math" title="Why MASE is scale-free">
MASE (Hyndman & Koehler, 2006)<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> divides the test-set MAE by the average absolute error that a seasonal naive forecast made *inside the training data*:

$$
\text{MASE} = \frac{\frac{1}{h}\sum_{t=T+1}^{T+h} |y_t - \hat y_t|}{\frac{1}{T-m}\sum_{t=m+1}^{T} |y_t - y_{t-m}|}
$$

where $m$ is the seasonal period (12 for monthly data). Both numerator and denominator are in the same units, so the units cancel. With non-seasonal data, set $m = 1$ (naive instead of seasonal naive). R's `accuracy()` uses $m$ = the frequency of the series, so it uses 12 for every monthly series, seasonal or not; that is the convention in this chapter's labs.
</Callout>

## One test period is not enough: time-series cross-validation

The scorecard above rests on a single test period starting in January 2023. Pick a different start date and the ranking might change. You saw this in the explorer when you dragged the origin. A more reliable approach repeats the experiment many times:

1. Train on the first $k$ observations and forecast $h$ steps ahead.
2. Record the error.
3. Move the origin forward by one period and repeat until the data run out.

This is **time-series cross-validation**, also called *evaluation on a rolling forecasting origin*.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup> Each row of dots below is one repetition.

Three lessons jump out of the "Result on real data" chart:

- **Error grows with the horizon.** For payroll jobs, the naive forecast misses by about 200,000 jobs one month ahead and by about 2.4 million twelve months ahead. Forecasting next month is far easier than forecasting next year.
- **The best benchmark depends on the data.** Payrolls trend upward, so **drift** wins at every horizon. Retail sales are dominated by seasonality, so **seasonal naive** wins by a wide margin from 1 to 11 months ahead.
- **Beware of coincidences.** Switch to retail sales and look at horizon 12: naive suddenly ties seasonal naive, and drift even beats it. Twelve months ahead, the "last observed value" is from the same month a year earlier, so naive briefly *becomes* seasonal naive (and drift becomes seasonal naive plus a year of trend). Cross-validation by horizon exposes this kind of illusion; a single test score would hide it.

### How to read an error-by-horizon chart

1. **Read the y-axis in the data's units.** "Seasonal naive misses retail sales by about \$14 billion at every horizon from 1 to 12 months" is a sentence a manager can use.
2. **Read the slope.** A steep curve means information fades fast: next month is easy, next year is hard (naive on payrolls). A flat curve means the method's error barely depends on the horizon. Seasonal naive is flat on retail sales because, at any horizon up to 12 months, it copies a value that is less than a year old.
3. **Look for crossings.** If two lines cross, the winner depends on the horizon. Pick the method for the horizon the business needs: a store ordering stock three months ahead cares about horizon 3, not horizon 1.
4. **Distrust odd bumps.** A sudden dip at one horizon (drift at horizon 12 on retail) is usually a coincidence of the calendar, not skill.

## How big a difference is meaningful?

Suppose drift beats naive by 5% on MAE. Is drift better, or was it lucky in this test period? Think of each test month as one **vote**: compute the gap between the two methods' misses that month. If drift is truly better, the votes lean the same way month after month. If not, they scatter around zero, and a small average gap is just noise.

Two things decide whether an average gap is convincing:

- **How big it is compared with the month-to-month scatter of the gaps.** A gap of 90 with gaps scattering by ±1,000 is noise; a gap of 90 with gaps scattering by ±100 is real.
- **How many months you average.** The noise in an average shrinks as you add months (by the square root of their number), so a gap that is unconvincing over 12 months can be convincing over 96.

The **Diebold–Mariano (DM) statistic** puts these together: the average gap divided by its standard error. Roughly, if |DM| is above 2, the gap is unlikely to be luck (p < 0.05). Try it on three real comparisons of one-month-ahead naive and drift forecasts, 2012–2019.

<Callout type="math" title="The Diebold–Mariano test in one paragraph">
Let $d_t = L(e_{1,t}) - L(e_{2,t})$ be the loss differential between methods 1 and 2 for some loss $L$. `dm.test` uses squared errors by default (`power = 2`), as the lab above does; the explorer uses absolute errors (`power = 1`), which keeps the gap in the data's units. If the two methods are equally accurate, $E[d_t] = 0$. The DM statistic is $\bar d / \widehat{\text{se}}(\bar d)$, where the standard error is corrected for autocorrelation in $d_t$ (for one-step-ahead forecasts no correction is needed, and $\widehat{\text{se}} = s_d/\sqrt n$). Under the null it is approximately standard normal (`dm.test` adds a small-sample correction and compares it with a $t$ distribution).<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> A p-value below 0.05 says the accuracy gap is unlikely to be luck.
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Beware a single lucky test period, and don't split hairs">
One test year is an anecdote. Before you announce a winner: (1) score on many origins (cross-validation), or at least several separate test years; (2) check whether the gap is large next to its noise (DM, or the band in the explorer); (3) ask whether the gap matters in money. Robert Nau's rough guide is useful: a 30% lower error is a big improvement, 10% is worth noticing, and a 2% difference is probably not worth a more complicated model.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup> When two methods are close, keep the simpler one.
</Callout>

## Choose the metric that matches the business loss

Every metric is a judgment about which mistakes hurt. MAE says every \$1 million of miss costs the same, whichever way it goes. RMSE says one big miss is worse than several small ones. MAPE says a miss on a small month matters more than the same miss on a big month. None of them says "running out of stock hurts more than having too much," yet that is how many businesses lose money.

So write down the business's own cost of a mistake and score with that. Suppose each \$1 million of retail sales you fail to stock (an under-forecast: empty shelves, lost sales) costs $r$ times as much as \$1 million of leftover stock (an over-forecast). With $r = 1$ the cost is exactly the MAE. Move $r$ and watch the winner change.

<Callout type="business" title="Which metric should you report?">
Pick the metric that matches the <em>cost</em> of a mistake. If being 10 units off costs twice as much as being 5 units off, use <strong>MAE</strong>. If one big miss is a disaster (a stock-out on Black Friday, a hospital running out of beds), use <strong>RMSE</strong>. If running short costs more than running over (or the reverse), score with an <strong>asymmetric cost</strong> like the one above. Managers like <strong>MAPE</strong> because "we're 3% off" needs no explanation, but remember it favors forecasts that are too low. Analysts prefer <strong>MASE</strong> because it works across any series and has a built-in benchmark. Always report <strong>bias</strong> too: a forecast that is always 5% too low leads to chronic under-stocking even if its MAE looks fine. <a href="/chapters/uncertainty/#from-uncertainty-to-a-decision-the-newsvendor">Chapter 10</a> shows how to choose the right buffer properly, from the forecast's uncertainty and the two costs.
</Callout>

## Transform, forecast, then score in original units

Analysts often forecast a transformed series: the log, a growth rate, a seasonally adjusted version (see [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#transformations-every-analyst-uses) for why). That is fine for **modeling**. But the business lives in dollars, homes and jobs, so the **scoring** must happen in those units:

1. transform the data (for example, take logs);
2. forecast the transformed series;
3. transform the forecast back (for logs, take `exp()`);
4. compute the errors against the **original** actual values.

<Callout type="empirical" title="An MAE in logs is not comparable with an MAE in dollars">
In the last exercise below, a drift forecast of log e-commerce sales has an MAE of 0.115 on the log scale. That looks tiny next to the 1,958 (\$ millions) of a drift forecast on the original data, but the two numbers are in different units and cannot be compared. Transformed back to dollars, the log forecast misses by about \$13,400 million a month: far worse. Always score every candidate on the same, original scale. (Back-transforming a log forecast gives a median rather than a mean forecast; <a href="/chapters/uncertainty/#forecasting-in-logs-and-coming-back-to-dollars">Chapter 10</a> explains the small correction.)
</Callout>

## From benchmark to business value

In companies, the benchmark idea becomes a metric called <Term def="The reduction in forecast error achieved by a step in the forecasting process (a model, an analyst's adjustment), compared with a naive benchmark.">forecast value added (FVA)</Term>. Each step in the forecasting process (the statistical model, the analyst's adjustments, the sales team's "gut feel") is scored against the naive benchmark. Steps that make the forecast worse get removed.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> The results are often humbling: a study of more than 60,000 forecasts at four supply-chain companies found that large manual adjustments usually helped, but small ones often made forecasts *worse*, and upward adjustments were much less likely to help than downward ones.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup>

## Chapter checklist

- Benchmarks first: **mean, naive, seasonal naive, drift**. A model earns its place only by beating the best of them, **on the same test window**.
- Split by time, never randomly: a random split lets the model peek at the future and can make errors look roughly 20 to 30 times smaller than they are. Guard against **leakage**.
- Read every metric in words and units. **ME**: positive means too low (actual − forecast). **MAE**: the typical miss. **RMSE ≥ MAE**; a ratio well above 1.25 means a few big misses. **MASE** below 1: smaller errors than seasonal naive's typical in-sample miss.
- **MAPE** is easy to say but explodes near zero and rewards forecasts that are too low. Use MAE in percentage points or MASE for rates; use MASE or MAPE, never MAE, to compare series.
- Prefer **time-series cross-validation** to a single split, and report accuracy **by horizon**. One lucky test year proves nothing.
- Ask whether a gap is real: compare it with its noise (**Diebold–Mariano**, |DM| > 2), and whether it is big enough to matter.
- Score with the **business's cost** of a mistake, and score transformed forecasts **after transforming back** to the original units.
