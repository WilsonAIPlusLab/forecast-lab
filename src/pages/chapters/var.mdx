<p class="lede">The Federal Reserve has just raised its policy rate by more than markets expected. Your CFO wants to know two things before Monday's planning meeting: <strong>will this push up unemployment, and when?</strong> And will inflation actually come down, or could it rise first? A single regression can't answer this, because rates, jobs and prices all push on each other. This chapter gives you the tool economists built for exactly that question.</p>

In earlier chapters, one variable did the forecasting work for another: claims predicted payrolls, the yield curve predicted recessions. The arrow pointed one way. In the macroeconomy the arrows point **both ways**. The Fed raises rates *because* inflation is high, and inflation later falls *because* rates went up. Unemployment drives the Fed's decisions, and the Fed's decisions drive unemployment. Treat any one of these as "the" cause and you get the story wrong.

A <Term def="Vector autoregression: a system of regressions in which every variable is explained by its own past and the past of every other variable in the system.">VAR</Term> (vector autoregression) handles the feedback by refusing to choose. Every variable gets its own equation, and every equation contains the past of every variable. Christopher Sims proposed this in 1980 as an alternative to large models whose restrictions he called "incredible".<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> Forty years later VARs are standard at central banks, and Sims shared the 2011 Nobel Prize for this work.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup>

<KeyIdea>A VAR lets every variable talk to every other. Its forecasts come for free; its stories about cause and effect need extra assumptions, and you must say which ones you made.</KeyIdea>

## Every variable on lags of every variable

We will use the small system Stock and Watson used to introduce VARs in the *Journal of Economic Perspectives*: inflation, unemployment and the federal funds rate, quarterly, 1960 to 2000, four lags.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> (They measured inflation with the GDP price index; we use the CPI, which is seasonally adjusted, as is unemployment.) A VAR(4) for these three series is just three regressions:

| Equation | Left-hand side | Right-hand side |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | inflation this quarter | constant + 4 lags of inflation + 4 lags of unemployment + 4 lags of the funds rate |
| 2 | unemployment this quarter | the same 13 regressors |
| 3 | funds rate this quarter | the same 13 regressors |

Because every equation has the same regressors, each can be estimated by ordinary least squares, one at a time. That is all `VAR()` in R's `vars` package does.<sup>[12](#refs)</sup> This form is called the <Term def="The VAR written with only lagged variables on the right-hand side. Its errors are forecast errors, which are usually correlated across equations.">reduced form</Term>: nothing on the right-hand side is dated "this quarter", so it is a pure forecasting model.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Why inflation enters as a rate, but unemployment and the funds rate as levels">
Look at how each series goes into the VAR, because it sets the units of every answer later.

- **Prices enter as inflation.** The CPI is an index that has climbed for decades (1982–84 = 100). A VAR needs series that keep coming back to a typical level, so we use its growth rate: `400 * diff(log(cpi))` is the quarterly percent change at an **annual rate** (log change × 4 quarters × 100). That is the same "% per year" unit as an interest rate, so a 5% funds rate and 5% inflation can be compared directly. ([Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#transformations-every-analyst-uses) covers logs and growth rates; [Chapter 7](/chapters/arima/#the-cure-differencing) explains why a log difference is a growth rate.)
- **Unemployment and the funds rate enter as they are.** Both are already rates in percent, with no built-in upward trend. Differencing them would answer a different question ("how fast is unemployment *changing*?") and throw away where the level is.
- **Every response is then in percentage points.** A response of +0.12 for unemployment means 4.00% becomes 4.12%. A response of +0.28 for inflation means the *annualized inflation rate* is 0.28 points higher for that quarter. It does **not** mean prices are 0.28% higher; you will convert to the price level later in the chapter.
- **Monthly data were turned into quarterly averages.** `fred(..., to = "quarter")` averages the three months in each quarter (see [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#changing-frequency-sum-average-or-last-value)). So "one lag" means one quarter, and four lags reach back one year.
</Callout>

<Callout type="math" title="The VAR(p) in matrix form">
Stack the variables in a vector $y_t = (\pi_t, u_t, R_t)'$. A VAR($p$) is

$$
y_t = c + A_1 y_{t-1} + A_2 y_{t-2} + \dots + A_p y_{t-p} + e_t, \qquad \text{Var}(e_t) = \Sigma,
$$

where each $A_j$ is a $3\times 3$ matrix of coefficients. Row $i$ of the system is equation $i$ in the table above. The errors $e_t$ are the one-step-ahead forecast errors. They are serially uncorrelated but usually correlated **with each other**: $\Sigma$ is not diagonal. That off-diagonal correlation is the root of the identification problem later in this chapter.

The number of coefficients grows fast: $k$ variables and $p$ lags give $k(kp+1)$ coefficients. Three variables and four lags give 39; six variables and twelve lags would give 438. That is why practical VARs stay small or use Bayesian shrinkage.
</Callout>

How many lags? The same information criteria you met for ARIMA apply. `VARselect()` fits VAR(1) through VAR(8) and reports which lag each criterion prefers. Here all four criteria pick 3. We use 4 to match Stock and Watson; the conclusions below barely change.

## Reading VAR output: why we rarely read coefficients one by one

The lab also printed the first rows of the unemployment equation. Pick one coefficient: the second lag of the funds rate, **+0.058**. Read it the way [Chapter 4](/chapters/regression/#reading-a-regression-like-an-analyst) taught you: *holding the other 12 regressors fixed*, a funds rate 1 point higher two quarters ago goes with an unemployment rate 0.058 points higher this quarter.

That sentence is grammatically fine and economically useless, for three reasons:

1. **"Holding the others fixed" never happens.** The funds rate one, two and three quarters ago move almost together. You cannot raise one lag and freeze its neighbours.
2. **The neighbours point the other way.** The coefficients on lags 1 to 4 are −0.013, +0.058, −0.023 and +0.005. A hike shows up in all four over the following year, so the four partly cancel.
3. **The effect also travels through the other equations.** A hike changes inflation and the funds rate itself next quarter, and *those* feed back into unemployment through their own lags. One coefficient sees none of that.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Choosing the lag length sensibly">
- **Start from the calendar.** With quarterly data, 4 lags cover a year; with monthly data you need 12 for the same year.
- **Let the criteria narrow it down.** `VARselect()` reports AIC, HQ, BIC (`SC`) and FPE. When they disagree, AIC usually wants more lags and BIC fewer; Hyndman and Athanasopoulos note that BIC is often the safer choice for VARs, because each extra lag adds one coefficient per variable to every equation (9 more in our three-variable VAR).<sup>[14](#refs)</sup> Here all four pick 3.
- **Check the answer survives a neighbour.** If your conclusion holds at p − 1 and p + 1 (it does here, from 2 to 8), say so. If it flips, report that honestly.
- **Check stability.** `roots(m)` prints one number per "echo" in the system; each must be below 1 for a surprise to fade away. Ours peak at 0.95: shocks fade, slowly. A root at or above 1 means responses never die out, a sign that a trending series went in without being turned into a rate or difference.
</Callout>

### Keep the system small

Every variable you add brings a new lag of itself into *every* equation. The count grows with the square of the number of variables, while the data stay the same.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Quarterly or monthly? More data points is not more information">
Switching from quarterly to monthly data triples the observations, but to reach back one year you need 12 lags instead of 4, so each equation's coefficients triple too. The three-variable VAR has about 12 observations per coefficient at quarterly frequency (13 coefficients, 160 quarters) and about 13 at monthly frequency (37 coefficients, 480 months). Choose the frequency for the **question** (monthly if timing within the quarter matters, as when you argue that the Fed cannot react to this month's data), not to "get more data". And before you add a fifth or sixth variable, ask whether it helps forecast the others: that is exactly what a Granger test, next, measures.
</Callout>

## Granger causality: does X help forecast Y?

The first question you can ask of a VAR is purely about forecasting: *do past values of X improve forecasts of Y, once Y's own past is already in the model?* If yes, X is said to <Term def="X Granger-causes Y if past values of X improve forecasts of Y beyond what past values of Y (and any other included variables) already provide. It is a statement about predictive content, not about true cause and effect.">Granger-cause</Term> Y, after Clive Granger, who formalized the idea in 1969.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> The test is an ordinary F-test that all lags of X have zero coefficients in Y's equation.

Below, every arrow is one such test in a two-variable VAR, for four series: inflation, unemployment, the funds rate and the term spread (the 10-year minus 3-month Treasury yield, which you met in Chapter 5).

With 4 lags, 9 of the 12 links are significant at the 5% level in 1960–2000, but only 5 of 12 in 1985–2019. The term spread stops predicting unemployment (p = 0.004 before, 0.12 after), and unemployment stops predicting inflation. One link that **strengthens** is funds rate → spread. <Callout type="pitfall" title="Granger causality is not causality">
"Granger-causes" means "helps predict". Nothing more. Weather forecasts Granger-cause rain: yesterday's forecast helps predict today's rain better than past rain alone. But the forecast does not cause the rain. In macroeconomics the trap is subtler: the funds rate may Granger-cause unemployment simply because the Fed reacts to information about the coming slowdown that the VAR does not contain. Use Granger tests to choose predictors, never to claim a policy works.
</Callout>

In R, `causality(m, cause = "ffr")` tests whether the funds rate helps predict the *other variables jointly* inside the three-variable VAR.

### Reading a Granger test: what it does and does not tell you

| A small p-value (say, below 0.05) **does** tell you | It does **not** tell you |
|---|---|
| Past X carries information about future Y that Y's own past (and the other variables in the VAR) did not | That X *causes* Y, or that changing X on purpose would change Y |
| X is worth keeping as a predictor of Y in this system | How *big* the effect is, or even its *sign*: the test pools all lags, some positive and some negative |
| The link held **in this sample, with these lags** | That it will hold next decade (unemployment → inflation vanished after 1985) |

And a large p-value does not prove X is useless: with few observations and many lags, the test has little power to see a real but small link.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Make Granger tests behave">
- **Test on rates, not trending levels.** Two series that both trend (say, the CPI level and nominal GDP) can appear to "predict" each other just because both go up. Use growth rates or other non-trending versions, as this chapter does.
- **Fix the lag length first, then test.** Different lags can give different verdicts: in the graph above, switch between 2, 4 and 8 lags. Pick the lags with `VARselect()` *before* you look at p-values, not the lag that gives the answer you hoped for.
- **Re-test on recent data** before you rely on an old relationship, and report both samples if they disagree.
</Callout>

## Impulse responses: follow one surprise through time

Granger tests say *whether* there is a link. An <Term def="The path a variable follows, period by period, after a one-time surprise (shock) to one equation of the VAR, holding all other shocks at zero.">impulse response function</Term> (IRF) says *how big* and *when*. Feed the VAR a one-time surprise in one equation, set all other future surprises to zero, and iterate the system forward. Because every variable depends on every other's past, the surprise ripples through the whole system.

Read the funds-rate shock carefully, because this is the chapter's question. A surprise hike of 0.76 percentage points (one standard deviation of the funds-rate residual) is followed by:

- **Unemployment:** almost nothing for a year, then a slow rise to **+0.12 percentage points after eight quarters** (two years). The 90% band excludes zero from quarter 4 to quarter 12. Monetary policy works with long lags.
- **Inflation:** up 0.28 points in the next quarter, then, from about the sixth quarter on, roughly 0.1 point *below* baseline. That later decline is not statistically distinguishable from zero in this small VAR.
- **The funds rate itself:** the hike fades by half within two quarters and has almost disappeared after three years (0.04 points left).

Stock and Watson find the same slow build with these three variables, using a structural VAR in which the Fed sets its rate in response to recent inflation and unemployment (a backward-looking "Taylor rule"): after a one-point hike, inflation eventually falls by about 0.3 points and unemployment rises by about 0.2 points, with most of the action in the third year.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The price puzzle: why a hike seems to raise inflation">
In the early 1990s Christopher Sims noticed that small VARs often show prices *rising* after a monetary tightening.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup> His explanation: the Fed is forward-looking. It raises rates when it sees inflation coming, using information (commodity prices, surveys, staff forecasts) that a three-variable VAR does not contain. The VAR cannot tell a "hike because inflation is coming" apart from a "surprise hike", so some of the inflation that was coming anyway gets blamed on the hike. The standard remedy is to add commodity prices to the VAR. Try it: add `400 * diff(log(q("PPIACO")))` as a fourth variable before `ffr`. In our sample this shrinks the puzzle only a little (0.28 to 0.25 points), a reminder that a small VAR's "policy shock" is not a clean experiment.
</Callout>

### Reading an impulse response out loud

An impulse-response chart is only useful once you can say it in one sentence. A good sentence answers five questions:

1. **How big was the surprise, in the shock's own units?** `irf()` uses a one-standard-deviation shock: 0.76 percentage points for the funds rate. Nobody at the Fed thinks in 0.76-point moves, so rescale. Responses are *linear*: divide by 0.76 to get "per 1-point hike", multiply by 0.25 for a quarter-point hike.
2. **In what units is the response?** Percentage points of unemployment; percentage points of *annualized* inflation; percentage points of the funds rate. These units come straight from how each variable entered the VAR.
3. **Compared with what?** Every number is a gap from the path the economy would have taken **without** the surprise. "+0.12" does not mean unemployment rises to 0.12%, or rises by 0.12 *in total*; it means 0.12 points above where it would otherwise have been, at that quarter.
4. **When?** Count quarters from the shock (quarter 0). Say when the response peaks and how long it lasts.
5. **How sure?** Read the band at that quarter, in words.

For the CFO, the baseline answer becomes: *"A 1-percentage-point surprise rise in the funds rate is followed by an unemployment rate about 0.15 points higher two years later than it would otherwise have been, peaking after 8 quarters. The 90% band excludes zero from quarter 4 to quarter 12."*

<Callout type="empirical" title="Confidence bands, in words">
A 90% band is the range of responses that fit the data reasonably well. Say it like this: *"two years out, unemployment is between 0.07 and 0.22 points higher per 1-point hike, with 90% confidence"*. Three habits keep you honest:

- **If the band straddles zero at a quarter, say "we cannot rule out no effect at that horizon"**, not "there is no effect". The inflation decline after year one is in this category.
- **Don't read the wiggles.** After a one-s.d. hike the inflation response zig-zags (+0.28, +0.18, −0.13, −0.01 …) inside a band about 0.4 points wide. Report the overall shape, not each turn.
- **Bootstrap bands move a little from run to run.** With `runs = 100` the edges shift by a few hundredths each time you run the lab; use more runs (500 or more) for anything you publish.
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="A growth-rate response is not a level response">
Because inflation entered the VAR as a growth rate, its impulse response is about the *rate* at which prices rise. To talk about the price **level**, add the inflation responses up and divide by 4 (each one is an annualized quarterly rate). Choose "Price level" in the reader: per 1-point hike, the CPI is about **0.15% higher** after two quarters and about **0.11% lower** after three years, and the band includes zero from the third quarter on. The same logic applies to any variable entered as a growth rate, such as GDP growth: the cumulated response, divided by 4 for annualized quarterly rates, is the effect on the level.
</Callout>

## The identification problem: who moves first?

There is a catch hidden in the word "surprise". The reduced-form errors are correlated: −0.43 between unemployment and the funds rate. When the funds-rate equation surprises, unemployment typically surprises too. So what is a "pure" funds-rate shock? To trace one, we have to split the correlated forecast errors into uncorrelated **structural shocks**, and the data alone cannot tell us how. This is the <Term def="The problem that many different structural models (different assumptions about which variables affect which within the same period) imply exactly the same reduced-form VAR, so the data cannot choose among them.">identification problem</Term>.

**Why ordering matters, in plain words.** Picture a quarter in which unemployment came in higher than forecast *and* the funds rate came in lower. Two stories fit. Story A: the economy weakened, and the Fed cut rates in response. Story B: the Fed cut rates by surprise, and (somehow) unemployment rose in the same quarter. The VAR only sees that the two surprises happened together; it cannot tell who moved first. An ordering is your answer to that question: it hands the shared part of the surprise to whichever variable you put **earlier**. If the surprises were uncorrelated there would be nothing to hand out, and the ordering would not matter. Ours are correlated (−0.13, +0.25 and −0.43 across the three pairs), so it does.

The most common solution is a recursive ordering, known as a <Term def="Factoring the error covariance matrix as Σ = PP′ with P lower-triangular. Applied to a VAR, it assumes the first variable reacts to no other shock within the period, the second reacts only to the first, and so on.">Cholesky decomposition</Term>. You list the variables in order. The first may react within the quarter to its own shock only; the second to the first and its own; the last to everything. Our baseline puts the Fed **last**: the Fed sees this quarter's inflation and unemployment before setting the rate, while prices and hiring respond to the rate only with a lag. That is an economic assumption, not a statistical result. Change it and see what happens.

</div>

Two things should surprise you:

1. **Put the Fed first and the story flips.** A "hike" now raises inflation by 0.33 points on impact and 0.70 points the next quarter, and *lowers* unemployment for a year. Nothing in the data changed. The Fed-first ordering simply credits the funds rate with the same-quarter co-movement that the Fed-last ordering credits to inflation and unemployment.
2. **Only the Fed's position matters for the Fed's shock.** With the Fed last (or first), swapping the other two variables leaves the funds-rate responses *exactly* unchanged. In general, the responses to a shock depend only on which variables come before it, not on their order among themselves.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Defending an ordering in practice">
- **Order by speed.** Put slow-moving variables (prices, employment, output) first and fast-moving ones (policy rates, then financial prices such as bond yields or stock indexes) last. A variable placed later is allowed to react to everything earlier *within the period*.
- **Frequency changes how believable an ordering is.** "The Fed cannot affect prices and jobs within the period" is more plausible within a month than within a quarter, which is one reason many monetary-policy VARs use monthly data.
- **Report the alternative.** If the headline result (unemployment rises after a hike) survives the other orderings, say so; if only the short-run sign depends on the ordering (as here), say that too. Never shop among orderings for the answer you like.
</Callout>

<KeyIdea>Impulse responses to a "pure" shock are only as believable as the ordering behind them. Always state the ordering, justify it with economics, and check whether your conclusion survives a different one.</KeyIdea>

<Callout type="math" title="From reduced form to structural shocks">
Write the reduced-form errors as $e_t = B\,\varepsilon_t$, where $\varepsilon_t$ are uncorrelated structural shocks with unit variance. Then $\Sigma = BB'$. A symmetric $3\times3$ matrix $\Sigma$ has 6 distinct elements, but $B$ has 9 unknowns, so we need $9 - 6 = 3$ extra restrictions. The Cholesky choice sets $B$ lower-triangular: the three zeros above the diagonal are the three restrictions (the shaded zeros in the impact table above). These are **short-run restrictions**: they say what cannot happen *within the period*.

The response of $y_{t+h}$ to $\varepsilon_t$ is $\Psi_h B$, where $\Psi_h$ are the moving-average coefficients of the VAR ($\Psi_0 = I$, $\Psi_h = \sum_{j=1}^{\min(h,p)} A_j \Psi_{h-j}$).

An alternative is **long-run restrictions** (Blanchard and Quah, 1989): for example, assume demand shocks have no permanent effect on output, and let the data determine the short run.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup> Structural VARs with such restrictions are estimated in R with `vars::SVAR()` and `vars::BQ()`.
</Callout>

How sure are we about any single response? `irf(..., boot = TRUE)` answers with a <Term def="A resampling method: rebuild many artificial data sets from the fitted VAR and its resampled residuals, re-estimate the VAR on each, and use the spread of the resulting impulse responses as a confidence band.">bootstrap</Term>. It resamples the VAR's residuals to create artificial histories, re-estimates the VAR on each, and reports the middle 90% of the resulting responses.

## Variance decomposition: which shocks matter most?

Impulse responses follow one shock at a time. A <Term def="Forecast error variance decomposition: the share of the variance of the h-step-ahead forecast error of each variable that is due to each structural shock.">forecast error variance decomposition</Term> (FEVD) adds them up. It asks: when the VAR's forecast of unemployment *h* quarters ahead turns out wrong, how much of the miss is due to each shock?

Read an FEVD share like a pie chart of *surprises*: "eight quarters ahead, 8% of the uncertainty in our unemployment forecast comes from funds-rate surprises". Each row adds to 100%. A share says nothing about the *sign* of an effect, and nothing about the Fed's predictable reactions, only about its surprises.

<Predict client:load
  question="With the Fed ordered **last**, what share of unemployment's **one-quarter-ahead** forecast error comes from funds-rate shocks?"
  options={['Exactly 0%', 'About 7%, the same as five years out', 'About a third, since there are three shocks']}
  answer={0}
  explain="Exactly zero, by assumption. Ordering the Fed last says unemployment cannot react to a funds-rate shock within the quarter, so one quarter ahead none of unemployment's forecast miss can come from it. Shares at short horizons often just repeat the ordering; the informative numbers are at longer horizons."
/>

Three lessons stand out:

- **Horizon changes everything.** One quarter ahead, 98% of unemployment's forecast error is its own shock. Five years (20 quarters) ahead, inflation shocks account for 55%, unemployment shocks for 38% and funds-rate shocks for 7%.
- **Policy surprises are a small source of fluctuations.** Funds-rate shocks never explain more than about 3% of inflation's forecast error, and at most 11% of unemployment's. This is a common VAR finding, and it does not mean policy is unimportant: most of what the Fed does is a *systematic* response to the economy, which the VAR attributes to the shocks the Fed is responding to.
- **The FEVD inherits the ordering.** Switch to "Fed first": the funds-rate shock's share of unemployment's 20-quarter forecast error jumps from 7% to 27%.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Three mistakes people make with FEVD shares">
- **"Funds-rate shocks explain only 7%, so the Fed doesn't matter."** No: the FEVD counts *surprises*. The Fed's systematic reaction to inflation and unemployment is filed under those shocks.
- **Reading horizon-1 shares as findings.** At short horizons the shares mostly restate the ordering (the 0% above). Look at 8 and 20 quarters.
- **Quoting a share without the horizon and the ordering.** "Inflation shocks explain 55% of unemployment's forecast error variance *five years ahead, with the Fed ordered last*" is a result; "55%" alone is not.
</Callout>

Professional VAR forecasting systems cope by using more variables plus Bayesian shrinkage toward simple models.

<Callout type="business" title="Where VARs earn their keep">
In practice VARs are prized less for raw accuracy than for **coherent scenarios**. A bank's stress test needs a path for rates, unemployment and inflation that hangs together. A VAR delivers that: condition on a rate path, and it produces consistent paths for the rest. Central banks also use VAR impulse responses as the yardstick that bigger models must reproduce.
</Callout>

<Callout type="intuition" title="Where this fits: from ADL to VAR">
Each VAR equation is an autoregressive distributed lag (ADL) model, the dynamic regression of Chapter 8. The difference is one assumption. In Chapter 8, claims could move jobs but jobs could not move claims: the input was treated as *external*. A VAR drops that assumption and lets every variable respond to every other one.
</Callout>

## Cointegration: a drunk and her dog

So far every series has been stationary or close to it. Many important economic series are not. Interest rates at different maturities, consumption and income, prices of the same good in two cities: each pair **wanders** (it behaves like a random walk), yet the two never drift far apart. Michael Murray's classic illustration: a drunk wanders randomly out of a bar, and so does her dog. Each path is unpredictable, but whenever they drift apart she calls, he barks, and they move back toward each other.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup>

Two series are <Term def="Two nonstationary (I(1)) series are cointegrated if some linear combination of them, y − βx, is stationary. The combination is the long-run equilibrium; deviations from it are temporary.">cointegrated</Term> when each one wanders on its own, but a gap between them (one series minus β times the other) keeps coming back to a typical level. That gap is the leash. Interest rates are the textbook case: the expectations theory of the term structure says long rates are averages of expected future short rates, so the long rate and the short rate should move one-for-one in the long run.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup> Find the leash yourself.

<div class="breakout">
<CointLeash client:load />
</div>

The explorable scores each gap with the ADF unit-root test from [Chapter 7](/chapters/arima/): the more negative the statistic (τ), the stronger the evidence that the gap keeps coming back. At β = 0 the "gap" is just the 10-year yield, with an ADF statistic of −1.17: it wanders, rising from under 5% in 1960 to over 15% in 1981 and falling for decades after. Near β = 1 the statistic reaches −4.18, far below the −2.86 critical value: the spread is stationary.

Notice also what the τ curve teaches about testing: against −2.86 the test rejects for a broad range of β, from about 0.7 up to about 1.9. A unit-root test can tell you that a leash exists, but it is a blunt tool for finding β. To estimate β, use a regression or Johansen's method, as below.

There are two standard ways to test for cointegration:

- **Engle–Granger, two steps** (Engle and Granger, 1987).<sup>[9](#refs)</sup> First regress one series on the other to estimate β. Then test the residuals for a unit root. The catch: because β was estimated to make the residuals look as stationary as possible, the usual ADF critical values are too lenient. With two variables, use about −3.34 at 5%, not −2.86 (MacKinnon).<sup>[10](#refs)</sup>
- **Johansen** (1991) tests inside a VAR and can find several cointegrating relations among many series, for example the 3-month, 1-year and 10-year yields together.<sup>[11](#refs)</sup> The **trace test** asks "are there at most *r* relations?" for *r* = 0, 1, …

If series are cointegrated, the right model is a <Term def="Vector error-correction model: a VAR in differences plus a term that pulls the variables back toward their long-run (cointegrating) relation.">VECM</Term>: a VAR in differences plus an **error-correction term** that pulls the system back to the leash.

<Callout type="math" title="The VECM in two lines">
For two I(1) series cointegrated with vector $(1, -\beta)$,

$$
\Delta y_t = \alpha_y\,(y_{t-1} - \beta x_{t-1} - \mu) + \text{lags of } \Delta y, \Delta x + e_{y,t}
$$
$$
\Delta x_t = \alpha_x\,(y_{t-1} - \beta x_{t-1} - \mu) + \text{lags of } \Delta y, \Delta x + e_{x,t}
$$

The term in parentheses is last period's distance from equilibrium. The **adjustment speeds** $\alpha$ say who moves to close the gap. Engle and Granger showed that cointegration and error correction are two sides of the same coin: if the series are cointegrated, an error-correction representation exists. A VAR fitted to the levels is still consistent, but a VAR fitted only to the differences throws the long-run information away.
</Callout>

The results line up:

- **Engle–Granger** estimates GS10 ≈ 2.23 + 0.84 × TB3MS, and its residual ADF statistic of −3.81 beats the −3.34 critical value: the two yields are tied together.
- **Johansen's trace test** finds exactly one cointegrating relation (31.5 for *r* = 0 against a 1% critical value of 24.6; 2.6 for *r* ≤ 1 against 7.5 at 10%), with β = 0.99, close to the one-for-one relation theory predicts.
- **Reading the adjustment speeds.** Both yields do some of the work. When the spread is above its long-run level of about 1.5 points, next month the 10-year yield falls and the 3-month rate rises, each by about 2.5% of the gap. In words: the leash is real but loose. With both yields moving, about 5% of the gap closes each month, so half of any gap takes roughly a year to disappear.

<Callout type="tip" title="Consumption and income">
The same logic applies to household consumption and disposable income, the other classic cointegrated pair: income wanders, but consumption cannot drift arbitrarily far from it for long. To try it, use real series in logs for both (the bundled `DSPIC96` is real disposable income; `PCE` is *nominal* spending, so it would need deflating first).
</Callout>

## Chapter checklist

- A **VAR(p)** regresses every variable on *p* lags of every variable. Choose *p* with `VARselect()` (start from the calendar: 4 quarterly or 12 monthly lags per year), check that `roots()` are all below 1, and fit with `VAR()`.
- Enter trending series as **rates** (inflation = `400 * diff(log(cpi))`, annualized %) and series that are already rates (unemployment, interest rates) as they are. That choice sets the **units** of every impulse response.
- Don't read VAR coefficients one by one: they flip sign across lags and are rarely significant alone. Read **joint tests, impulse responses and variance decompositions**.
- **Keep the system small.** Each added variable puts new lags in *every* equation; monthly data need three times the lags to cover the same year.
- **Granger causality** (`causality()`) means "helps forecast", not "causes", and says nothing about size or sign. Fix the lags before testing, and re-test on recent data.
- **Read an impulse response out loud:** the shock's size and units (rescale the one-s.d. shock to 1 point or 25 basis points), the response's units, "compared with no surprise", the timing of the peak, and the band in words ("we cannot rule out no effect" when it straddles zero).
- An **inflation** response is not a **price-level** response: add up the responses and divide by 4 to get the % change in the price level.
- Impulse responses to "pure" shocks depend on the **Cholesky ordering**: the shared part of correlated surprises goes to whichever variable comes first. State the ordering, justify it by speed of reaction, and report the alternative. Beware the **price puzzle**.
- **FEVD** (`fevd()`) shares split forecast *surprises* by shock; quote them with the horizon and the ordering, and don't read horizon-1 shares as findings.
- A VAR's forecasts must still beat **univariate benchmarks**; often they don't.
- **Cointegrated** series wander together. Test with Engle–Granger (stricter critical values) or Johansen (`ca.jo()`), and model with a **VECM**.
