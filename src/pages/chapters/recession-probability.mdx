<p class="lede">You run planning at a manufacturer. Next year's budget depends on one question: <strong>will there be a recession?</strong> Nobody can answer yes or no with confidence. But you can attach a <em>probability</em> to it, check how good past probabilities were, and decide in advance how high that probability must be before you cut hiring.</p>

In May 2023 the gap between the 10-year Treasury yield and the 3-month bill rate fell to **−1.57 percentage points**, its lowest monthly reading since 1981. That gap, the *yield-curve spread*, had turned negative ahead of every U.S. recession since 1969 except one (before 1990 it only flattened to about +0.1). Many forecasters expected a downturn. Yet through the latest data in this chapter (October 2026), the NBER has not declared one. In this chapter you build the famous model behind those warnings, learn to score probability forecasts properly, and find out exactly how much that two-year false alarm cost it.

<KeyIdea>A recession forecast should be a probability, not a yes or no. Judge it by how well it ranks months (AUC) and whether its probabilities mean what they say (Brier score, calibration), on data it has never seen.</KeyIdea>

## A forecast for a yes-or-no event

Most targets in this course have been numbers: sales, jobs, inflation. Many business questions are instead <Term def="An outcome with only two possible values, coded 1 (the event happens) and 0 (it does not).">binary outcomes</Term>. Will this customer default? Will the shipment arrive late? Will the economy be in recession a year from now?

[Chapter 5](/chapters/nonlinear-regression/) introduced the tools for such outcomes on bank-marketing data: the logit, odds ratios, marginal effects, confusion matrices and ROC curves. This chapter reviews them briefly and puts them to work on a harder problem: a rare economic event, forecast a year ahead, where the test data must come after the training data.

For a binary outcome, the useful forecast is a probability between 0 and 1. A model that says "30% chance" is not wrong when the event fails to happen; it would be wrong if events it called 30% happened 90% of the time. That idea, called <Term def="A probability forecast is calibrated when events it gives probability p happen about a fraction p of the time.">calibration</Term>, will matter a lot by the end of the chapter.

Our event is a U.S. recession. The official referee is the <Term def="The National Bureau of Economic Research's Business Cycle Dating Committee. It dates U.S. business-cycle peaks and troughs after the fact.">NBER</Term> dating committee, which defines a recession as a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> FRED turns its dates into a monthly indicator, `USREC`: 1 in every recession month, 0 otherwise.

Our predictor is the <Term def="The 10-year Treasury yield minus the 3-month Treasury bill rate, in percentage points. Positive = normal upward-sloping yield curve; negative = inverted curve.">term spread</Term>: the 10-year Treasury yield (`GS10`) minus the 3-month bill rate (`TB3MS`), both monthly averages in percent. When short rates rise above long rates, the yield curve is <Term def="A yield curve on which short-term interest rates are higher than long-term ones, so the term spread is negative.">inverted</Term>.

<Callout type="intuition" title="Why would bond yields predict recessions?">
Two stories fit together. First, a long-term yield roughly reflects the short rates that investors expect over the coming years. If markets expect the Fed to cut rates, usually because they expect the economy to weaken, long yields fall below short ones. Second, an inverted curve often means monetary policy is tight right now: the Fed has pushed short rates up to fight inflation, and tight money slows the economy with a lag of a year or so.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> Neither story is a law of nature, which is why we test the relationship rather than assume it.
</Callout>

## Build the target: a recession 12 months from now

The question in the chapter title is about the future, so the target has to look forward. We follow the New York Fed: for each month *t*, the target is **whether month *t* + 12 is a recession month**.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> In code, that means shifting `USREC` back by 12 months, so that each row pairs *this* month's spread with the economy's state *a year later*. The last 12 rows have no answer yet, and those are exactly the months we want to forecast.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The referee announces the score late">
The NBER dates turning points only once it is confident, often many months later. It announced the December 2007 peak on December 1, 2008, and the July 1990 peak in April 1991. The fastest call in recent history, the February 2020 peak, came on June 8, 2020.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> Two consequences for you:
<ul>
<li>The most recent zeros in `USREC` are <strong>provisional</strong>. If the NBER later declares a recession that started in 2026, the last few targets in this chapter flip from 0 to 1.</li>
<li>A real-time forecaster never knows the latest outcomes. When you backtest, use only outcomes that would have been known, or accept that your test is a little too kind.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

The first lab builds the target and tries the simplest possible model: an ordinary regression of the 0/1 target on the spread. This is called the <Term def="An ordinary least-squares regression whose dependent variable is 0 or 1. Its fitted values are read as probabilities.">linear probability model</Term> (LPM).

## From a straight line to an S-curve

The <Term def="A regression for a 0/1 outcome in which the probability is the logistic function of a linear index: p = 1 / (1 + e^-(a + b·x)). It always lies between 0 and 1.">logit</Term> model passes a straight-line index $a + b \cdot \text{spread}$ through an S-shaped function that squeezes any number into the range 0 to 1. Instead of least squares, it is fitted by <Term def="Choosing the parameters that make the observed data most probable. For 0/1 data the log-likelihood adds log(p) for every 1 and log(1 − p) for every 0.">maximum likelihood</Term>: the best curve is the one that gives high probabilities to months that were followed by recession and low probabilities to months that were not.

Try it yourself before the computer does it.

Three things to notice:

- **The curve slopes down.** A steeper yield curve means a lower chance of recession a year later. If you make the curve flat (steepness 0), it predicts the same probability for every month, and the log-likelihood is at its worst.
- **The fitted curve crosses 50% at a spread of about −1.1 points.** Even a mildly inverted curve gives "only" 20 to 40%. Recessions are rare (about 12% of months since 1959 were followed by one 12 months later), so the model needs strong evidence before it calls one likely.
- **Most dots overlap.** Plenty of months with a negative spread were *not* followed by recession 12 months later. The spread is informative, not decisive.

<Callout type="math" title="Logit, probit and the likelihood">
The logit model says

$$
P(y_{t+12} = 1 \mid s_t) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(a + b\,s_t)}}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\log\frac{p}{1-p} = a + b\,s_t ,
$$

so the coefficient $b$ is the change in the **log-odds** for a one-point change in the spread $s_t$. The **probit** model replaces the logistic curve with the standard normal cumulative distribution, $p = \Phi(a + b\,s_t)$. The two curves have almost the same shape; logit coefficients are typically about 1.6 to 1.8 times probit ones.

Both are fitted by maximizing the log-likelihood

$$
\ell(a,b) = \sum_t \Big[ y_t \log p_t + (1 - y_t) \log (1 - p_t) \Big].
$$

There is no closed-form solution. `glm()` uses iteratively reweighted least squares, which is Newton's method for this problem. The explorer above runs the same algorithm in your browser.
</Callout>

## The yield-curve recession model

This model has a pedigree. Arturo Estrella and Gikas Hardouvelis showed in 1991 that the slope of the yield curve predicts U.S. output growth and NBER recessions.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> Estrella and Frederic Mishkin then compared many financial indicators and found that beyond about two quarters ahead, the yield-curve slope was the best single predictor of recessions, and out of sample it usually did better alone than combined with other variables.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup>

The Federal Reserve Bank of New York publishes a monthly recession probability based on this work.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> Its recipe:

- **Predictor:** the 10-year Treasury yield minus the 3-month Treasury bill rate, monthly averages. The New York Fed converts the bill rate to a *bond-equivalent* basis; FRED's `TB3MS` is on a discount basis, so our spread differs slightly.<sup>[6](#refs)</sup>
- **Target:** whether the economy is in recession **12 months later**, the same target you just built.
- **Model:** a probit estimated on January 1959 to December 2009, with intercept −0.5333 and slope −0.6330.

Its November 5, 2026 update put the October 2026 spread at 0.98 points and the probability of recession in October 2027 at **12.4%**.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup>

Now look at the whole history. Each forecast is drawn at the month it is *about*, so a good model's peaks should land on the grey recession bars.

What the radar shows, with the model frozen at the end of 1999 and a 30% threshold:

- **2001 and 2008: right direction, imperfect timing.** The spread inverted in 2000 and again in 2006–07, and probabilities rose to about 44 to 49%. But the spread's warning comes early and fades: by 2008 the Fed was cutting rates, the curve was steep again, and the model's probabilities had dropped right when the recession months arrived. - **2020: a lucky call?** The curve briefly inverted in mid-2019 and the model rose to about 42% for mid-2020. A recession did come, but it was caused by a pandemic that no bond market predicted. Count it as a hit in the scoring, but not as proof.
- **2023–24: the long false alarm.** From November 2022 to November 2024 the spread was negative for 25 straight months, the longest run since 1959 in this data. In total, 39 of 282 calm months were flagged at the 30% threshold.

Our logit gives a **9.7%** chance that October 2027 is a recession month. The New York Fed's coefficients applied to our spread give 11.2%, and its own published figure is 12.4%. The gap between those two is entirely the spread measure (0.98 points on the New York Fed's bond-equivalent basis, 1.08 on ours); the rest of the gap to our 9.7% comes from logit versus probit and the different estimation sample. <Callout type="pitfall" title="Has the curve stopped working?">
Nobody knows yet, and you should be wary of confident answers in either direction. In the middle of the 2023–24 episode, economists were already warning that the nominal spread "could be less informative than it has been in the past", for example because long-run and short-run inflation expectations had diverged unusually.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup> Others have argued that a near-term forward spread, built from expected short rates over the next 18 months, is a cleaner signal than the 10-year minus 3-month spread.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup> And the NBER lag means the final verdict on 2024–26 is not in. What you *can* say precisely is how much the episode hurt the model's track record. That is the next two sections.
</Callout>

## From probability to decision: pick a threshold

A probability is not yet an action. Your CFO wants to know: **do we freeze hiring or not?** To turn a probability into a yes/no warning you choose a <Term def="The probability above which a forecast is treated as a 'yes' (here: warn of recession).">threshold</Term>. Each choice produces a <Term def="A 2×2 table counting hits, misses, false alarms and correct quiet calls for a given threshold.">confusion matrix</Term>:

| | Recession followed | No recession followed |
|---|---|---|
| **Warned** | Hit (true positive) | False alarm (false positive) |
| **No warning** | Miss (false negative) | Correct quiet call (true negative) |

Two rates summarize it. The <Term def="Hits ÷ all months followed by recession. Also called the true positive rate, sensitivity or recall.">hit rate</Term> is the share of pre-recession months you flagged. The <Term def="False alarms ÷ all months not followed by recession. Also called the false positive rate.">false alarm rate</Term> is the share of calm months you flagged by mistake.

The natural first guess, a 50% threshold, is a poor choice for rare events. In sample, only 21 of 802 months ever reached 50%, so the model flagged just **7 of 95** pre-recession months (a 7% hit rate). Lowering the threshold to 20% catches 56% of them, at the price of flagging 14% of calm months.

The <Term def="Receiver operating characteristic curve: hit rate plotted against false alarm rate for every possible threshold.">ROC curve</Term> on the left shows every threshold at once. Sliding the threshold only moves you *along* the curve; a better model shifts the whole curve toward the top-left corner. The cost chart on the right is where the business decision lives: if a missed recession costs your firm five times as much as a false alarm, the cheapest in-sample threshold is about **14%**. At that setting the model catches 74 of 95 pre-recession months and raises false alarms in a quarter of calm ones.

<Callout type="math" title="Why the best threshold is 1 / (1 + k)">
Suppose a false alarm costs 1 and a missed recession costs $k$. If you warn, your expected cost is $(1-p) \cdot 1$. If you stay quiet, it is $p \cdot k$. Warning is cheaper when $(1-p) \lt p\,k$, that is, when

$$
p \gt \frac{1}{1+k}.
$$

With $k = 5$, warn above $1/6 \approx 17\%$. This rule assumes the probabilities are well calibrated. The data-driven optimum in the threshold game (about 14% in sample, 16% out of sample) is close but not identical, because the model's probabilities are not perfectly calibrated.
</Callout>

<Callout type="business" title="Who should choose the threshold?">
Not the analyst alone. The threshold encodes a value judgment about the cost of errors. A bank deciding whether to raise loan-loss reserves, a retailer deciding on holiday inventory and a central bank deciding on policy face very different costs. Your job is to deliver a well-calibrated probability **and** to show decision makers the trade-off, ideally as a chart like the threshold game above. Report the probability itself, not just "recession: yes".
</Callout>

## Scoring the probabilities themselves

The confusion matrix depends on one threshold. Two scores judge the whole set of probabilities instead.

The <Term def="Area under the ROC curve. It equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive case gets a higher forecast than a randomly chosen negative case. 0.5 = coin flip, 1 = perfect ranking.">AUC</Term> (area under the ROC curve) measures **ranking**: the chance that a randomly chosen pre-recession month gets a higher probability than a randomly chosen calm month. In sample, the yield-curve logit scores **0.820**. A coin flip scores 0.5.

The <Term def="Mean squared difference between the forecast probability and the 0/1 outcome. Lower is better; 0 is perfect.">Brier score</Term> measures **accuracy and calibration together**: the average of $(p_t - y_t)^2$. It was proposed by the meteorologist Glenn Brier in 1950 for weather forecasts.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup> On its own the number is hard to read, so compare it with <Term def="A constant forecast equal to the historical frequency of the event (here: the share of months followed by recession).">climatology</Term>, a forecast that always says the base rate. In sample, the logit's Brier score is **0.091** against **0.104** for climatology. The <Term def="1 − (Brier of the model ÷ Brier of the benchmark). Positive = better than the benchmark.">Brier skill score</Term> is therefore 1 − 0.091 / 0.104 ≈ **13%**: the model removes about an eighth of the benchmark's squared error.

## The honest test: out of sample

Everything so far was scored on the same months the model was fitted to. As Chapter 3 insisted, that flatters any model. So freeze the model at the end of 1999 and forecast 2000 onward, a period with three recessions and one long inversion that the model has never seen. (The last training rows, dated 1999, have targets in 2000, so no test outcome leaks into the fit. A strict real-time forecaster would also have lacked those 2000 outcomes, so the test is still slightly kind to the model.)

Two lessons:

1. **A model can rank well and still lose to a constant.** Even over the full period the AUC is 0.73, well above a coin flip. But the Brier score punishes confident mistakes hard: a forecast of 80% for a month with no recession adds $0.8^2 = 0.64$ to the squared error, about eight times the climatology forecast's average (0.084). The largest squared errors came from forecasts made in 2007 and 2008, which missed the timing of the Great Recession, and from those made in 2023 and 2024, the long false alarm.
2. **The verdict depends on the window.** Through 2019 the yield curve looked like a superb indicator; six more years of data reversed the Brier comparison. Always say which period your accuracy numbers come from.

## Can other indicators help?

The yield curve is only one signal. Three others are popular: the <Term def="Moody's Baa corporate bond yield minus the 10-year Treasury yield (FRED: BAA10Y). It widens when investors fear defaults.">credit spread</Term> (`BAA10Y`), initial jobless claims (`ICSA`, seasonally adjusted, the subject of Chapter 8) and University of Michigan consumer sentiment (`UMCSENT`, not seasonally adjusted). Adding them to a logit is easy: `glm(y ~ spread + baa + claims, ...)`. Whether they *help* is an out-of-sample question. Because the credit spread starts in 1986, this test trains on 1986–2005 and evaluates on 2006 onward, and it asks the question at two horizons.

The horizon decides everything. **Three months ahead**, the spread alone does worse than a coin flip, because the curve usually re-steepens just before and during recessions as the Fed cuts rates. Jobless claims, which jump as soon as layoffs start, lift the AUC to 0.85. **Twelve months ahead**, none of the extra predictors moves the AUC by more than 0.005. That is the same pattern Estrella and Mishkin reported: other indicators help at short horizons, while the yield curve dominates further out.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup> Note also that this test window is small (two recessions in the test set), so treat differences in the third decimal as noise.

## Chapter checklist

- A binary forecast should be a **probability**. Build the target carefully: here, `USREC` shifted 12 months ahead, with the last 12 rows unknown and the latest zeros provisional because of the NBER's dating lag.
- The **linear probability model** can predict probabilities below 0 or above 1. **Logit** and **probit** (`glm(..., family = binomial(link = "logit"/"probit"))`) stay between 0 and 1 and give almost identical probabilities.
- Read a logit slope as an **odds ratio** ($e^b$) or as a **marginal effect at a chosen value** ($b\,p(1-p)$), never as a direct change in probability.
- A **threshold** turns probabilities into warnings. For rare events, 50% is usually far too high; the cost-minimizing threshold is about $1/(1+k)$ when a miss costs $k$ times a false alarm.
- Score probability forecasts with **AUC** (ranking), the **Brier score** against a climatology benchmark, and a **calibration** table.
- Test **out of sample**. The yield-curve model beat a constant through 2019 but lost to it once the 2022–24 false alarm entered the test window.
- Extra predictors help at **short horizons** (jobless claims, 3 months ahead) but barely at **12 months**, where the spread dominates.
