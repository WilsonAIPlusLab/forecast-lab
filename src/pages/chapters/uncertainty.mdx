<p class="lede">It is late summer and you plan December inventory for a chain of clothing stores. The forecast says December sales will be about \$40 billion. Your buyer asks the real question: <strong>should we order exactly the forecast, or more, and if more, how much more?</strong> A single number cannot answer that. You need to know how wrong the forecast could be, and what each kind of mistake costs.</p>

Every forecast so far in this course has come with a fan of shaded bands. This chapter takes those bands seriously. You will learn what they promise and how to say it in plain words, bring them back to dollars from a model fitted in logs, check whether they keep the promise on real data (often they don't), turn them into a stocking decision, and see why an average of two forecasts often beats both.

<KeyIdea>A forecast is not a number. It is a range of possible futures, each with a probability. Decisions come from the range, not from the middle.</KeyIdea>

## A forecast is a cloud of possible futures

A <Term def="A range that should contain a future observation with a stated probability, such as 80% or 95%, given the information available now.">prediction interval</Term> says: "with 80% probability, next December's sales will fall between A and B." The easiest way to see where such a range comes from is to let the model imagine the future many times. Each imagined future is a <Term def="One possible future trajectory, generated by running the model forward and adding a randomly drawn shock at each step.">sample path</Term>.

Watch what happens when you switch from **Paths** to **Fan**. The fan is not a different object: it is a summary of the cloud. At each month, the 80% band keeps the middle 80% of the simulated values and drops the 10% at each end. The paths also explain why fans widen. A shock in month 1 carries into month 2, which adds its own shock, and so on, so the spread grows with the <Term def="How many periods ahead you are forecasting, written h.">horizon</Term>.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="A prediction interval is not a confidence interval">
A <strong>confidence interval</strong> is about a fixed unknown <em>parameter</em>, such as average monthly growth. Collect more data and it shrinks toward zero width. A <strong>prediction interval</strong> is about a future <em>observation</em>. It includes the unavoidable month-to-month noise, so it never shrinks to zero, however much history you have. When a manager asks "how sure are we about next December?", the answer is a prediction interval. Reporting a confidence interval for the mean instead makes the forecast look far more certain than it is.
</Callout>

There are three standard ways to compute intervals from a fitted model, and the lab compares all three on the December 2019 example:

| Method | How it works | R |
|---|---|---|
| **Formula** | Variance grows with $h$; assume normal errors | `forecast(fit, level = c(80, 95))` |
| **Bootstrap** | Replay the model's own past errors in random order | `forecast(fit, bootstrap = TRUE)` |
| **Simulated paths** | Draw many futures, take percentiles at each month | `simulate(fit, nsim = h, future = TRUE)` |

## Read an interval out loud

Most managers never see the math behind a band. They see a chart and ask "so what number should I plan for?" Your job is to turn the band into a sentence they can act on. Here is a reliable template, close to the way the Bank of England explains its fan charts:<sup>[10](#refs)</sup> state the point, state the range, and state **how often a range like this catches the truth**.

> "Our best guess for September 2027 retail sales is about \$666 billion. We expect sales between \$645 and \$687 billion: a band like this catches the truth about 8 times out of 10. About 1 time in 10 sales will come in lower, and about 1 time in 10 higher."

Three habits make that sentence honest:

- **Use frequencies, not "confidence".** "8 times out of 10" is easier to grasp than "80% confident", and it reminds everyone that 2 times out of 10 the truth lands *outside*. An 80% band that is never wrong is not a good band. It is too wide.
- **Say which way the misses go.** An 80% band leaves 10% below and 10% above. If shortages hurt more than surpluses, the top of the band matters more than the middle (session 2 turns that into a number, with the newsvendor).
- **Pick the level for the decision.** An 80% band suits routine planning: staffing, ordering, budgets you revise every month. A 95% band suits "what is the worst we should prepare for?": credit lines, warehouse capacity, risk reports. A higher level buys more certainty with a wider, less useful range.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Four things a band does not say">
<ul>
<li><strong>"The model is 80% sure it is right."</strong> The band describes where future values should fall <em>if</em> the model's assumptions hold. It says nothing about the chance that the model itself is wrong.</li>
<li><strong>"Sales cannot go outside the band."</strong> They will, about 2 times in 10. Plan for it.</li>
<li><strong>"Every value inside the band is equally likely."</strong> Values near the point forecast are more likely than values near the edges.</li>
<li><strong>"The band includes shocks like 2020."</strong> It includes only the kinds of surprises in the training data. As the spaghetti chart showed, a pandemic was not one of them.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Report the band, not just the point">
A point forecast alone invites false precision: "\$666 billion" sounds like a promise. Put the 80% band in the same sentence, in the same units, rounded to what the data can support (\$645 to \$687 billion, not \$645.39 billion). Say which level you used. When you forecast a whole year, show the band for every month: its widening is information your manager needs.
</Callout>

## Forecasting in logs, and coming back to dollars

Many models in this course were fitted to the **log** of a series, not the series itself. [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#transformations-every-analyst-uses) explained why: in logs, steady percentage growth becomes a straight line, seasonal swings that grow with the level become constant, and a forecast can never go below zero. A useful bonus, stressed in Robert Nau's Duke forecasting notes:<sup>[11](#refs)</sup> errors in log units read as **percentage errors**. A log-scale error spread of 0.02 means a typical miss of about 2%.

But the manager wants dollars. Coming back from logs takes `exp()`, and three rules cover everything you need:

1. **The point forecast: `exp()` it, and call it the median.** In logs the forecast distribution is a symmetric bell, so the log point forecast sits in the middle: half the outcomes below, half above. `exp()` keeps numbers in the same order, so exp(log point) still has half the outcomes below and half above. That makes it the **median** in dollars, not the mean.
2. **The band: `exp()` each endpoint.** A value that falls inside the log band also falls inside the dollar band, so an 80% band stays an 80% band. But it is no longer symmetric: the top end stretches further from the point than the bottom.
3. **The mean: needs a small correction.** The dollar distribution leans right (big upward surprises are possible, but values cannot fall below zero), and the long right tail pulls the mean above the median. Multiply the median by about 1 + σ²/2, where σ is the log-scale spread of the forecast (the math box below shows where this comes from). Hyndman & Athanasopoulos call this a **bias adjustment**.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup>

Start with **crude oil** in **log units**. The bell is symmetric, and the point, the median and the mean are one and the same line. Switch to **dollars**. The bell leans right, the mean (gold, dashed) separates from the median (violet), and the band becomes lopsided. Twelve months ahead the median is about \$101 a barrel, the mean about \$109 (7.7% higher), and the 80% band runs from about \$62 to \$166: about \$40 below the median but \$65 above it. Drag the horizon to 24 months: the gap grows to about 16%, because it depends on the *square* of the spread. Now switch to **retail sales**. Its log spread is so small (about 0.02 to 0.04) that the median and the mean differ by less than 0.1%. For calm series the adjustment hardly matters. For volatile ones it does.

<Callout type="math" title="Where the 1 + σ²/2 comes from">
If the log forecast is normal, $\log Y \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then $Y$ is log-normal with

$$
\text{median}(Y) = e^{\mu}, \qquad E[Y] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2} = e^{\mu}\, e^{\sigma^2/2} \approx e^{\mu}\left(1 + \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right).
$$

Quantiles survive any increasing transformation: if $P(\log Y \le q) = 0.9$ then $P(Y \le e^{q}) = 0.9$. That is why interval endpoints transform cleanly and the median is simply $e^\mu$. Means do not survive: $E[e^{X}] \ne e^{E[X]}$ (Jensen's inequality), so the mean needs the correction. Here $\sigma$ is the $h$-step forecast spread in log units, which grows with the horizon, so the correction grows too. R applies it with `forecast(fit, biasadj = TRUE)` for models fitted with `lambda = 0` (Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, 2021, section 5.6).<sup>[9](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Logs in, dollars out: transform the endpoints, then choose median or mean">
<ul>
<li><strong>Never add a ± in dollars</strong> to a model fitted in logs. Compute the band in logs, then <code>exp()</code> each end. The dollar band should be lopsided; if yours is symmetric, you probably transformed the width instead of the endpoints.</li>
<li><strong>Use the median</strong> for "a typical month", and when a miss in either direction costs the same.</li>
<li><strong>Use the mean</strong> whenever numbers will be <em>added up</em>: a yearly budget built from twelve monthly forecasts, or a national total built from regional ones. Medians do not add up; means do.</li>
<li><strong>Check the size of the gap</strong> before you worry about it. If the log spread is below about 0.1, the mean is less than 0.5% above the median.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

## Audit the promise: coverage

An 80% interval makes a checkable promise. Over many forecasts, about 80% of actual values should land inside. The share that does is the interval's <Term def="The share of actual outcomes that fall inside the prediction intervals, measured out of sample. A calibrated 80% interval has coverage near 80%.">coverage</Term>. You audit it the same way you audited accuracy in Chapter 3: roll the forecast origin forward, forecast, wait, and record a hit or a miss.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Audit coverage on recent data, and count the misses by side">
<ul>
<li><strong>Audit the last 12 to 24 months every quarter</strong>, out of sample: for each month, was the actual inside the band you published <em>before</em> it happened? Report it as "X of N".</li>
<li><strong>Allow for luck.</strong> With 24 months, anything from 16 to 22 hits is consistent with an honest 80% band (Beyond the basics below shows why). Act on counts outside that range, or on a trend over several audits.</li>
<li><strong>Count misses above and below separately.</strong> An honest band misses about equally on both sides. In the December 2019 lab above, 18 of the 21 misses were above the band: the forecast was not just too uncertain, it was too low. Lopsided misses call for fixing the point forecast, not just widening the band.</li>
<li><strong>Say which window the band remembers.</strong> A band estimated on data that include 2020 will look "safe" for years (too wide), and one estimated on calm years will break in the next shock.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

## From uncertainty to a decision: the newsvendor

A forecast distribution can be summarized by its <Term def="The value below which a given share of outcomes falls. The 0.9 quantile of December sales is the level that sales stay below with 90% probability.">quantiles</Term>. The 0.9 quantile says "the truth will be below this value 9 times out of 10." The endpoints of an 80% band are the 0.1 and 0.9 quantiles, and the median is the 0.5 quantile. A stocking decision needs exactly one of these numbers. Which one depends on what each kind of mistake costs.

Back to the buyer. Suppose every dollar of demand you cannot serve loses 60 cents of margin, and every dollar of leftover stock loses 25 cents in markdowns and holding costs. Ordering the forecast exactly is a coin flip: half the time you run out. Because running out costs more than having leftovers, you should order more. The classic <Term def="A model for a one-time stocking decision under uncertain demand: choose the quantity that minimizes the expected cost of overstocking and understocking.">newsvendor model</Term> says exactly how much more.

<KeyIdea>Stock the quantile of the demand forecast at the **critical ratio**: the cost of being one unit short, divided by (cost of one unit short + cost of one unit left over).</KeyIdea>

With a shortage cost of 0.60 and a leftover cost of 0.25, the critical ratio is 0.60 / (0.60 + 0.25) = 0.60 / 0.85 ≈ 0.71. You stock the 71st percentile of December demand, and accept running out in about 29% of Decembers. The critical ratio *is* the <Term def="Here, the probability that stock covers all demand in the period (sometimes called the cycle service level).">service level</Term> that this cost structure justifies. Stock above the point forecast is <Term def="Inventory held above expected demand to protect against forecast errors.">safety stock</Term>.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Asymmetric costs call for an asymmetric target">
<ul>
<li><strong>Ask for two numbers, not one.</strong> Before you forecast for a decision, ask what one unit short costs and what one unit left over costs. Their ratio, short ÷ (short + over), tells you which quantile to deliver.</li>
<li><strong>Short costs more → aim above the point.</strong> Call-center staffing, spare parts, a store's best seller in December. The department store's ratio of 0.71 means stocking the 71st percentile.</li>
<li><strong>Leftovers cost more → aim below the point.</strong> Fresh food, fashion, anything marked down after the season. The discount chain in the exercise below has a ratio of 0.40 and stocks below the median.</li>
<li><strong>Equal costs → the median.</strong> That is the only case where "order the forecast" is right, and only if the forecast is the median.</li>
<li><strong>Report the target with its service level</strong>: "stock \$40.5 billion; this covers December demand about 7 years in 10." A manager can argue with a service level; nobody can argue with a bare number.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

## Don't pick, average: forecast combination

You now have several models on the scorecard: ETS, ARIMA, seasonal naive. Choosing the winner from one test period is risky, because the ranking changes with the period. Bates and Granger (1969) showed that a weighted average of two forecasts can have a smaller error variance than either one.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> Fifty years of evidence since then says a *simple* average is hard to beat. In the M4 competition, 12 of the 17 most accurate methods were combinations, mostly of statistical models.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup>

Start with **Furniture**. Exponential smoothing and seasonal naive score almost the same alone (MAE about \$356 and \$354 million), but the 50/50 blend cuts the error to \$211 million. The reason is in the panel: smoothing ran \$339 million too high on average, seasonal naive \$295 million too low, and averaging cancels much of both. Now try **Sporting goods**, where both methods erred in the same direction: blending cannot help. On **Total retail**, seasonal naive is so much worse that the 50/50 blend loses to smoothing alone (MAE \$11,823 against \$7,796 million). Combination is insurance, not magic.

### Why errors cancel, and how to read a weight

Think of two forecasters guessing the same number. If one tends to guess high when the other guesses low, their average lands closer to the truth than either guess. If both guess high together, averaging only gives you a high guess with a different name. Combination helps exactly as much as the two forecasts' errors **disagree**. In the furniture example the errors had opposite signs on average, so they cancelled; in sporting goods they pointed the same way, so they could not.

A combination weight is easy to misread. A blend of "70% smoothing, 30% seasonal naive" says that, in this test window, the best mix leaned on smoothing. It does **not** say smoothing is 70% right, or that it "explains" 70% of sales. The explorable makes the point: the best weight on smoothing was 52% for furniture, 70% for health and personal care, 0% for sporting goods and 92.5% for total retail, all found *after* seeing the answers.

## Chapter checklist

- **Read a band out loud:** point, range and frequency. "We expect \$645 to \$687 billion; a band like this catches the truth about 8 times in 10." Misses go both ways, about 1 in 10 each side for an 80% band.
- A 95% band is about **1.5 times as wide** as an 80% band. Bands **widen with the horizon** because shocks pile up. Pick the level for the decision.
- A prediction interval covers a future **observation** and never shrinks to zero; a confidence interval covers a **parameter**.
- Intervals come from a formula, a residual bootstrap or simulated paths. All share the model's assumptions, and all can fail together when the world changes.
- **Back from logs:** `exp()` of the log forecast is the **median**; `exp()` each band endpoint (the band stays 80% but becomes lopsided); the **mean** needs a bias adjustment of about $1 + \sigma^2/2$, which matters only for volatile series. Use the mean when numbers will be added up.
- **Audit coverage** out of sample, by horizon and by era, and on the **last 12 to 24 months**. Allow for luck (16 to 22 hits of 24 is normal for an 80% band) and count misses **above and below** separately. "80% on average" can hide 58% in a crisis and 88% afterward.
- **Asymmetric costs, asymmetric target:** stock the demand quantile at short ÷ (short + over). That ratio is your service level, and safety stock can be negative.
- A **simple average** of forecasts helps when the errors disagree and cannot help when they point the same way. A combination weight describes one test window, not a model's importance.

#### Why the fan widens, in formulas

<Callout type="math" title="Why the fan widens: h-step variance">
For a random walk $y_t = y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$ with shock variance $\sigma^2$, the $h$-step forecast error is the sum of $h$ independent shocks, so its variance is $h\sigma^2$ and its standard deviation grows like $\sqrt{h}$. For simple exponential smoothing, ETS(A,N,N), the variance is

$$
\sigma_h^2 = \sigma^2\left[1 + (h-1)\alpha^2\right],
$$

and a normal interval is $\hat y_{T+h|T} \pm z\,\sigma_h$, with $z = 1.28$ for 80% and $1.96$ for 95%. Models with trend and seasonality have longer formulas of the same kind (Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, 2021, section 8.7).<sup>[9](#refs)</sup> Three assumptions hide inside: the model is right, its parameters are known exactly, and the shocks are normal and as large as in the past. Chatfield (1993) showed that such intervals tend to be too narrow in practice, precisely because real forecasts break these assumptions.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

#### Coverage in words, and how much luck to allow

Coverage is easy to say in plain words: "our 80% bands caught the actual value in 20 of the last 24 months." The hard part is judging that sentence. Twenty-four months is a small sample. Even a perfectly honest 80% band does not catch exactly 19.2 months out of 24; like a coin that lands heads 80% of the time, it scores a little more in some years and a little less in others. So before you call a band broken, ask how many hits luck alone could explain.

#### Score a whole distribution: quantiles and pinball loss

A quantile forecast at level 0.9 says "the truth will be below this value 9 times out of 10." To score a quantile forecast we need a loss that rewards *the right quantile*, not the closest guess. That is the <Term def="Loss for a quantile forecast q at level τ: τ × (y − q) if the truth y is above q, and (1 − τ) × (q − y) if it is below. Minimized on average by the true τ quantile.">pinball loss</Term>.

The easiest way to understand pinball loss is as the **cost of a stocking decision**. Treat the 0.9 quantile forecast as the amount of stock you put on the shelf. If demand comes in *above* it, every dollar of missed sales costs you 0.9. If demand comes in *below* it, every dollar of leftover stock costs you 0.1. Running short is 9 times as expensive as overstocking, so the best policy is to aim high: stock enough to cover demand 9 times out of 10. That is exactly the 0.9 quantile: the newsvendor rule from the main text. At level 0.5 both kinds of miss cost the same, the loss is half the ordinary absolute error, and the best answer is the median. Pinball loss is the bill for safety stock that was too thin or too thick.

Averaging pinball loss over many levels (0.1, 0.2, …, 0.9) scores a whole forecast distribution at once. It approximates half the <Term def="Continuous ranked probability score: a proper score for a whole forecast distribution. Lower is better; for a point forecast it reduces to the absolute error.">CRPS</Term>, the standard score for a full forecast distribution (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007).<sup>[4](#refs)</sup>

Pinball loss catches bias in a distribution, not only spread.

<Callout type="math" title="The pinball loss formula, and why it picks out the quantile">
For a quantile forecast $q$ at level $\tau$ and an outcome $y$,

$$
L_\tau(y, q) = \begin{cases} \tau\,(y - q) & \text{if } y \ge q \\ (1-\tau)\,(q - y) & \text{if } y < q \end{cases}
$$

Let $F$ be the distribution of $Y$. The expected pinball loss of a guess $q$ is $\tau\,E[(Y-q)^+] + (1-\tau)\,E[(q-Y)^+]$. Its derivative in $q$ is $-\tau\,(1 - F(q)) + (1-\tau)\,F(q) = F(q) - \tau$. Setting it to zero gives $F(q) = \tau$: the true $\tau$ quantile minimizes expected pinball loss. Scores with this property, where honest reporting is optimal, are called **proper**. This is the same loss that defines quantile regression (Koenker & Bassett, 1978).<sup>[5](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

#### The newsvendor, in formulas and with a longer history

<Callout type="math" title="Deriving the critical ratio">
Write $c_u$ for the cost of one unit short (understock) and $c_o$ for the cost of one unit left over (overstock). The expected cost of stocking $Q$ is $c_u\,E[(D-Q)^+] + c_o\,E[(Q-D)^+]$. Its derivative is $-c_u\,(1 - F(Q)) + c_o\,F(Q)$. Setting it to zero gives

$$
F(Q^*) = \frac{c_u}{c_u + c_o}.
$$

Compare with the pinball-loss derivation above: the newsvendor cost is exactly $(c_u + c_o)$ times the pinball loss at $\tau = c_u/(c_u+c_o)$. A good quantile forecast and a good stocking decision are the same problem. The model goes back at least to Arrow, Harris & Marschak (1951).<sup>[6](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

<Callout type="math" title="When does a combination beat both forecasts?">
Let the two forecast errors have variances $\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2$ and correlation $\rho$. The combined error $w e_1 + (1-w) e_2$ has variance $w^2\sigma_1^2 + (1-w)^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w(1-w)\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$, minimized at

$$
w^* = \frac{\sigma_2^2 - \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}.
$$

The gain is largest when the two forecasts are about equally accurate and their errors are not strongly correlated, or have opposite biases. In practice $w^*$ must be estimated, and estimation error often makes "optimal" weights worse than equal weights: the so-called forecast combination puzzle.
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Read combination weights as a hint, and default to equal weights">
<ul>
<li><strong>A weight near 50%</strong> means both forecasts carry useful, different information. Keep both.</li>
<li><strong>A weight near 0% or 100%</strong> means one forecast added nothing in this window. Check on a second window before dropping it: rankings change.</li>
<li><strong>Weights tuned on the test period are hindsight.</strong> Equal weights need no estimation and are hard to beat in practice (the "forecast combination puzzle" in the math box above).</li>
<li><strong>Before combining, check the errors.</strong> Plot the two forecasts' errors over the same months. Opposite signs, or a low correlation, mean the blend will help; errors that move together mean it will not.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

#### Intervals for any model: split conformal prediction

The coverage audit showed that model-based intervals can be badly calibrated, and many models (machine-learning models, combinations, judgmental forecasts) have no interval formula at all. <Term def="A method that turns any point forecast into a prediction interval using the forecast's own past errors on data it was not fitted to.">Conformal prediction</Term> builds intervals from the model's track record instead of its assumptions.

In plain words: **use past errors as the ruler.** Look at how far off the model was on, say, the last 36 months it had not seen. Sort those misses from smallest to largest. If 80% of them were within 2.8% of the forecast, then an 80% band for next month is the forecast ± 2.8% (that is what the lab below finds for retail sales). No assumption about normal errors, no formula for the model: only its record. The simplest version, **split conformal**, has four steps:

1. Fit the model on a training set.
2. Forecast a separate **calibration set** the model has not seen, and record the size of each miss as a percentage of the forecast.
3. Sort the misses and find the one that 80% of them stay below (rounded up a little, to be safe). Call it $q$.
4. For a new forecast, the band is the forecast ± $q$ percent.

<div class="breakout">
<ConformalBand client:load />
</div>

With calibration ending in December 2018 (36 months, 80%), the band covers 9 of the 12 months of 2019. Slide the calibration window to end in December 2019 and the 2020 test year breaks it: only 5 of 12 inside. Then shrink the calibration set to 12 months and raise the level to 95%: the rule asks for the 13th smallest of 12 scores, and the band becomes infinite. Honest uncertainty needs enough history.

<Callout type="math" title="The guarantee, stated carefully">
In symbols, each calibration score is $s_t = |y_t - \hat y_t|/\hat y_t$. With $n$ scores and target coverage $1-\alpha$, $q$ is the $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil$-th smallest score, and the interval is $\hat y \times (1 \pm q)$. The "$+1$" is the "rounded up a little" in step 3.

If the $n$ calibration scores and the new score are <strong>exchangeable</strong> (their joint distribution does not change when you reorder them; independent and identically distributed scores qualify), then the split-conformal interval satisfies

$$
P\left(Y_{\text{new}} \in \text{interval}\right) \ge 1 - \alpha,
$$

in finite samples, for any model and any error distribution. If the scores have no ties, coverage is also at most $1 - \alpha + 1/(n+1)$ (see Angelopoulos & Bates, 2021).<sup>[7](#refs)</sup> Two caveats matter for forecasting. First, the probability is **marginal**: it averages over the random calibration set and the new point. It does not promise 80% coverage in any particular year. Second, time series are usually **not exchangeable**: errors are correlated over time and their size drifts, as 2020 showed. Then the guarantee does not apply. Recalibrating on a rolling window of recent errors is a practical fix with no finite-sample guarantee; adaptive methods such as Gibbs & Candès (2021) give long-run coverage guarantees even under drift.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>
