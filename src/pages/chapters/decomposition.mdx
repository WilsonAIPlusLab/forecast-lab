## Pulling a series apart

A monthly sales series is the product of three forces. Pull them apart and each one answers a different business question.

- The <Term def="The long-run movement of the series once the calendar's ups and downs are smoothed away. Some textbooks call it the trend-cycle because it also contains slower business-cycle swings.">trend</Term>: is the business growing?
- The <Term def="A pattern that repeats every year (or week, or day) because of the calendar: holidays, weather, school years, tax deadlines.">seasonal component</Term>: what does a normal month look like?
- The <Term def="Whatever is left after removing trend and seasonality: one-off shocks, surprises and noise. Also called the irregular component.">remainder</Term>: what was unusual this month?

The biggest remainders in the story mark real events: the April 2020 lockdowns, and the spring of 2021, when the third round of federal stimulus payments began arriving within days of the American Rescue Plan becoming law.<sup>[10](#refs)</sup> The remainder is where a forecaster looks for news.

<KeyIdea>Seasonal movement is not news. To judge a month, compare it with what that month normally looks like: remove the seasonal component, or compare with the same month a year earlier.</KeyIdea>

<Callout type="math" title="Additive and multiplicative decompositions">
Write $y_t$ for the data, $T_t$ for the trend, $S_t$ for the seasonal component and $R_t$ for the remainder.

$$
\text{Additive: } y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t \qquad\qquad \text{Multiplicative: } y_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t
$$

In the additive form, $S_t$ is measured in the data's units ("December adds \$60 billion"). In the multiplicative form, $S_t$ is a factor around 1 ("December is 1.12 times the trend"). The **seasonally adjusted** series removes only the seasonal part: $y_t - S_t$ (additive) or $y_t / S_t$ (multiplicative). Taking logs turns the second form into the first:

$$
\log y_t = \log T_t + \log S_t + \log R_t
$$

That is why the scroll story runs STL on $\log y_t$ and reports the layers in percent.
</Callout>

## Three plots before any model

Every analysis of a monthly series starts with three pictures. The first lab draws all three for retail sales, then runs the oldest decomposition method there is.

Lines stacked above each other mean trend.

It shows whether a month's effect is stable or changing.">seasonal subseries plot</Term>: all the Januaries together, all the Februaries together, and so on. It shows whether each month's effect is stable or drifting.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Three weaknesses of classical decomposition">
`decompose()` is easy to understand, but it has three flaws you can see in the lab output. It produces **no trend for the first and last six months**, which is exactly the recent data you care about. It forces the **seasonal pattern to be identical every year**. And a single shock such as April 2020 pulls the moving average down for a whole year around it. STL, below, fixes all three.
</Callout>

### How to tell which one you have

You don't need a model to decide. Ask one question of the time plot: **does the seasonal swing grow when the level grows?**

- If December adds about the same number of *dollars* every year, whatever the size of the business, the seasonality is **additive**. Model the series in its own units.
- If December adds about the same *percentage* every year, so the dollar swing grows and shrinks with the business, it is **multiplicative**. Take logs, and the swing becomes a constant height again.

The explorer below makes the check concrete. For every year it measures the seasonal swing (the highest month minus the lowest, both measured against a 12-month moving-average trend) twice: once in the data's own units and once in percent of the trend. Whichever version stays flatter is the right scale.

Read the three series the way an analyst would.

- **E-commerce** grew about fourfold between 2001–03 and 2016–18 (×4.2). Its seasonal swing in dollars grew with it (×4.5, from \$4.7 billion to \$21.2 billion a month), while the swing in percent hardly changed (45% → 49%). Textbook multiplicative.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Spot multiplicative seasonality in ten seconds">
1. Plot the raw series. If the December spikes (or summer peaks) get taller as the line rises, the seasonality is multiplicative.
2. Plot it again on a **log scale** (or plot `log(y)`). If the spikes now have roughly the same height everywhere, logs fixed it: model `log(y)`.
3. Sales, revenue and production are usually multiplicative: the same *share* of customers shops for gifts whatever the size of the business. Series that are already rates in percent (an unemployment rate, an interest rate) are often closer to additive.

The choice matters most when the level jumps. In 2020 the Bureau of Labor Statistics switched most of its household-survey series (including unemployment) from multiplicative to additive seasonal models: when unemployment jumped in April 2020, a multiplicative model would have made the seasonal adjustment swell in proportion to it, so additive models were expected to track the seasonal movements more accurately.<sup>[11](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="The log rule of thumb, and how to read log results">
If the seasonal swings grow as the level grows, take logs first and use an additive method on $\log y$. Every method in this course (STL, ETS, ARIMA, regression) then works in percentages. [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#logs-why-and-when) covers logs and the other everyday transformations in general; here is what they mean for seasonality.

- A seasonal effect of **0.11** on the log scale means "about 11% above the trend". For an exact figure, use `exp(0.11) − 1` = **11.6%**. For effects under about 10% the two readings are close; for big ones (−0.30 is −26%, not −30%) always convert.
- Two caveats: logs need strictly positive data, and results come back in log units, so use `exp()` to return to dollars.
</Callout>

## STL: a decomposition that bends

<Term def="Seasonal and Trend decomposition using Loess (Cleveland et al., 1990). It estimates trend and seasonal components with repeated local regressions and can down-weight outliers.">STL</Term> stands for *Seasonal and Trend decomposition using Loess*.<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> <Term def="Locally weighted regression: to estimate the value at one date, fit a small regression to nearby points only, with closer points weighted more.">Loess</Term> fits a small regression around each point, using only nearby observations, so STL's trend and seasonal pattern are both allowed to change slowly over time. Two windows control how slowly.

- **`s.window`**, the seasonal window, is measured in *years*. Each month's seasonal effect is smoothed across that many Januaries (or Februaries…). Small values let the pattern change quickly. `"periodic"` freezes it.
- **`t.window`**, the trend window, is measured in *months*. Larger values give a smoother trend. R picks a default from the period and `s.window`.
- **`robust = TRUE`** runs STL repeatedly. After each pass it down-weights months with huge remainders, so a shock like COVID stays in the remainder instead of bending the trend and the seasonal pattern.

The explorer runs the real algorithm in your browser. It is a line-by-line port of the code R's `stl()` calls, checked against R to eleven decimal places.

| `t.window` | 7 months | 21 (R's default) | 35 | 61 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Trend growth, last 12 months | +6.1% | +5.6% | +4.4% | +3.0% |

## How the official numbers are made

The **SA** and **NSA** labels on FRED series matter. <Term def="Not seasonally adjusted: the raw values, with all the calendar's ups and downs.">NSA</Term> data show the calendar's fingerprints. <Term def="Seasonally adjusted: the seasonal (and usually calendar) component has been estimated and removed, so consecutive months can be compared directly.">SA</Term> data have them removed, so you can compare one month with the next.

- The **Census Bureau** adjusts retail sales with **X-13ARIMA-SEATS**, its own seasonal adjustment program. The adjustment covers seasonal variation and also **trading-day and holiday differences**, but not price changes.<sup>[3](#refs),[4](#refs)</sup>
- The **Bureau of Labor Statistics** also uses X-13ARIMA-SEATS, for the CPI and for the payroll employment survey.<sup>[5](#refs),[6](#refs)</sup>
- X-13 fits an ARIMA model (Chapter 7) to **extend the series with forecasts** before filtering. That reduces the end-point problem you saw with `decompose()`.<sup>[4](#refs)</sup>
- Adjustment is **concurrent**: every new month re-estimates the factors, so recent seasonally adjusted values get revised.<sup>[3](#refs)</sup>
- Some series are also **annualized**. `HOUST` (housing starts, SA) is a seasonally adjusted *annual rate*: the monthly pace multiplied by 12. `HOUSTNSA` is the raw monthly count. Divide `HOUST` by 12 before comparing the two.

### Reading a seasonal factor

Agencies publish the seasonally adjusted series, but you can recover the factor they used yourself: divide the raw number by the adjusted one. For a multiplicative adjustment like Census's, that ratio is the **seasonal factor**, and it is the easiest thing in this chapter to explain to a manager. Read it like a percentage of a normal month, the way classic business-forecasting texts read seasonal indices.<sup>[13](#refs)</sup>

- A factor of **1.121** for December 2025 means December's sales ran **12.1% above a typical month**, purely because of the calendar.
- In dollars: raw sales were \$694.1 billion and seasonally adjusted sales \$619.1 billion, so about **\$75 billion** of December's sales were "just December".
- A factor below 1 works the same way: February 2025's **0.881** means February ran 11.9% *below* a typical month.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Say a seasonal factor out loud">
- **Multiplicative factor** (Census, BLS, or `exp()` of a log-scale STL seasonal): "December is **12% above** a typical month." Not "December grows 12%": the factor compares December with the year's normal level, not with November.
- **Additive component** (STL or `decompose()` in the data's units): "December adds about **\$62 billion**" (the classical figure from the first lab).
- **From raw to adjusted:** SA = raw ÷ factor. **From adjusted back to raw:** raw = SA × factor. That second step is how you turn a seasonally adjusted forecast back into the raw monthly numbers a store manager plans with.
- **Factors drift.** December's factor fell from **1.162** in 2015 to **1.101** in 2023 (1.121 in 2025). The STL explorer showed the same slide in December's effect. Do not hard-code last decade's December factor into this year's plan.
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Never compare a seasonally adjusted month with a raw one">
Suppose a colleague divides January 2026 *seasonally adjusted* sales by December 2025 *raw* sales. They get **−11.4%** and announce a collapse. The right comparisons tell a calmer story:

| Comparison | Jan 2026 vs Dec 2025 |
|---|---|
| SA vs SA (the underlying pace) | **−0.7%** |
| Raw vs raw (mostly calendar) | −19.2% |
| SA vs raw (**meaningless**) | −11.4% |

The mixed number removes January's seasonal dip from one month but leaves December's holiday boost in the other. Sometimes the mix looks harmless by luck: SA December over raw November gives −0.2%, close to the right answer, only because November's factor (1.004) happens to be near 1. Check the SA/NSA label of every series before you divide.
</Callout>

<Callout type="intuition" title="Calendar effects: the calendar is not the same every year">
Seasonality is the effect of the *month*. Calendar effects are the extra wrinkles that come from the *particular year's* calendar.

- **Length of month.** February has 28 days, about 8% fewer than the average month (28 ÷ 30.4 ≈ 0.92). Turn on *per day* in the fingerprint game below and much of February's dip disappears for restaurants and grocery stores. Leap years show up directly: Census's February factor is about 3–4% higher in 2016, 2020 and 2024 than in the years around them.
- **Trading days.** November 2025 had five Saturdays and five Sundays; December 2025 had four of each. For stores whose sales depend on the day of the week, the same month can be bigger or smaller depending on how its weekdays fall.
- **Moving holidays.** Easter falls anywhere from March 22 to April 25, shifting spring spending between March and April. Census research finds elevated sales before Easter in many retail series.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup> Thanksgiving is the fourth Thursday of November (November 22–28). When it is late, as on November 27, 2025, Cyber Monday falls in December (December 1, 2025), and the holiday shopping season is shorter.

X-13 models these effects with regression variables, alongside the seasonal pattern. STL does not, which is one reason our homemade adjustment differs from the official one in individual months.
</Callout>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="Three ways to get seasonal adjustment wrong">
<ul>
<li><strong>Mixing SA and NSA.</strong> Never compute growth from an NSA month to an SA month, or plot them as one series.</li>
<li><strong>Adjusting twice.</strong> Running STL on an SA series finds almost nothing, and what it finds is noise.</li>
<li><strong>Trusting the last few points too much.</strong> Every method is least certain at the end of the series, and official figures are revised as new data arrive.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

To compare seasonality across series, you need a number. Hyndman and Athanasopoulos define the <Term def="F_S = max(0, 1 − Var(R) / Var(S + R)). Close to 1 means the seasonal pattern dominates the remainder; close to 0 means little seasonality.">seasonal strength</Term> $F_S$ of a decomposition, a score between 0 and 1.<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> If the remainder is tiny compared with the seasonal swings, $F_S$ is close to 1. If the remainder is as big as everything else, $F_S$ is close to 0.

<Callout type="math" title="The seasonal strength formula">
$$
F_S = \max\left(0,\; 1 - \frac{\text{Var}(R_t)}{\text{Var}(S_t + R_t)}\right)
$$

It compares how much the remainder varies with how much the seasonal component and the remainder vary together. Because it is a ratio of variances, a few huge remainders can drag it down.
</Callout>

<Callout type="business" title="Why a planner cares about seasonal strength">
Seasonal strength tells you where calendar-based planning pays off. A clothing chain can staff and stock for December months in advance, because December is predictable. A gas station chain can't plan its revenue that way: prices, not the calendar, drive its sales. High seasonal strength also means a **seasonal naive** forecast (Chapter 3) will be hard to beat.
</Callout>

## Is this month good or bad?

The question you will be asked most often in a business job is not "decompose this series" but "was last month good?" There are four common ways to answer, and they can disagree.

<div class="breakout">
<MonthVerdict client:load />
</div>

For December 2025, the raw change is +11.8%. A typical December over the previous five years rose 9.1% (the median), so December 2025 beat its seasonal norm by about 2.8 points. Sales were 3.4% above December 2024. The Census seasonally adjusted change was +0.2%. Three different questions, three different answers, all of them more honest than the headline.

| Method | What it answers | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Seasonally adjusted, month over month | Is the underlying pace speeding up right now? | Noisy; revised later; depends on the adjustment method |
| Year over year (same month last year) | How do we compare with a year ago? | Lags turning points by months; distorted if last year was unusual (2021 vs 2020) |
| Raw change vs the seasonal norm | Did this month beat a normal version of itself? | The "norm" depends on which years you average |

### Year over year: the free, slow seasonal fix

Comparing a month with the same month a year earlier needs no model at all: December is compared with December, so the holiday boost cancels. That is why company reports and many news stories use it. ([Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#growth-rates-mom-annualized-and-yoy) introduced year-over-year growth and base effects; here the question is how it compares with seasonal adjustment.) But it answers a different question from the seasonally adjusted month-over-month change, and the explorer shows why.

Year-over-year growth is all twelve monthly moves of the past year multiplied together. For December 2025 that is +0.2% (December's own seasonally adjusted move) on top of +1.6% from the eleven months before it, giving +1.8%. So a year-over-year number is mostly old news, which has two consequences.

- **It turns late.** In May 2020, seasonally adjusted sales jumped **18.5%** from April as stores reopened, yet year-over-year growth still read **−0.7%**. A reader who watched only the year-over-year line would have missed the rebound for a month.
- **It echoes last year's shocks.** A strange month shows up twice: once when it happens, and again twelve months later as a base effect, in the opposite direction (April 2021 above).

<Callout type="empirical" title="Use year over year as a cross-check, not a verdict">
- **When it shines:** no seasonally adjusted series exists (a company's own sales, a regional series), or you need a quick, model-free answer. It removes most seasonality with one division.
- **When it misleads:** right after a turning point (it lags; analysts prefer seasonally adjusted month-over-month changes for spotting turns quickly<sup>[12](#refs)</sup>) and twelve months after any shock (base effects). Look at the same month two years back, or at the seasonally adjusted series, before you call a boom.
- **It does not remove calendar effects.** December 2025 raw sales were **3.4%** above December 2024, but seasonally adjusted sales only **1.8%**. The two Decembers' calendars and seasonal factors differ (1.104 in 2024, 1.121 in 2025), and a raw year-over-year comparison keeps that difference. A leap-year February is the extreme case: about 3.6% of its year-over-year "growth" is the extra day.
- **Never mix the two frequencies in one sentence:** "+0.2% this month" (month over month) and "+1.8% on the year" (year over year) are both true, and they answer different questions.
</Callout>

### Removing trend and seasonality: two routes

Some methods want a series with no trend and no seasonality. You can get one in two ways, and the next lab compares them.

- **Regression on time** fits a deterministic trend plus month dummies (Chapter 4) and keeps the residuals.
- **Differencing** subtracts the value 12 months earlier, leaving year-over-year growth. ARIMA models (Chapter 7) are built on this idea.

## Chapter checklist

- **Plot first:** a time plot, a seasonal plot and a subseries plot show trend, seasonality and outliers before any model.
- **Additive or multiplicative?** Ask whether the seasonal swing grows with the level. If it does, model `log(y)`: a log-scale seasonal effect of 0.11 reads as "about 11% above trend" (exactly `exp(0.11) − 1` = 11.6%).
- **Classical decomposition** is a good teaching tool but loses the ends, freezes the seasonal pattern and is hurt by outliers.
- **STL** lets trend and seasonality change: `s.window` sets how fast the seasonal pattern moves, `t.window` how smooth the trend is, `robust = TRUE` keeps shocks in the remainder. Check the remainder for leftover pattern.
- **Seasonally adjusted = data minus the seasonal component** (or divided by it). Official SA series come from X-13ARIMA-SEATS and also remove trading-day and holiday effects.
- **Windows change answers.** `s.window` decides how fast "normal for the month" can drift; `t.window` decides how smooth the trend is, and can halve or double a quoted trend growth rate. Name the window when you quote a trend.
- **Read a seasonal factor out loud:** factor = raw ÷ SA, so 1.12 means "12% above a typical month", and raw − SA is the seasonal effect in dollars. Raw = SA × factor takes you back.
- **Never mix SA and NSA** in one growth rate, and remember calendar effects (leap years, trading days, Easter, Thanksgiving) and that recent factors get revised.
- **Year over year** is a free, model-free seasonal fix, but it lags turning points, echoes last year's shocks (base effects) and keeps calendar differences.
- Judge a month against its seasonal norm, against the same month last year, or with SA data. **Never by the raw month-to-month change.**
