<p class="lede">It is mid-November 2026. Your company must decide this week whether to keep hiring through the holidays. The official November jobs report won't arrive until <strong>December 4</strong>. The first estimate of fourth-quarter GDP won't arrive until around the end of January. But the Labor Department published fresh jobless claims on Thursday, and it will do so again next Thursday. <strong>What is the economy doing this month, before the data are out?</strong></p>

Forecasting the present sounds like a contradiction. It isn't. Official statistics arrive weeks or months after the period they describe, and each one arrives on its own day. <Term def="Estimating the current (or just-finished) period's value of a slowly published statistic, such as GDP or payrolls, from faster data that are already available. A blend of 'now' and 'forecast'.">Nowcasting</Term> fills that gap with faster, noisier data. Central banks, investment banks and large retailers all run nowcasts. This chapter shows you how they work, what "big data" really changes in forecasting, and where the gains are smaller than the hype.

<KeyIdea>A nowcast is only as good as what it knows <em>on the day you make it</em>. Track exactly which data exist on that date, and judge the model only with information that was really available.</KeyIdea>

## Three kinds of "big"

"Big data" in forecasting usually means one of three problems. Each needs a different tool.

| The problem | Example | Why ordinary regression struggles | Tool in this chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Many predictors** (*p* large relative to *n*) | 20 or 200 monthly indicators, but only ~400 months of history | Too many coefficients to estimate. The fit chases noise. | Principal components (one factor) |
| **Many series** | 13 retail series (11 categories and two totals), or 30,000 store × product series | You can't hand-build 30,000 models. Their forecasts must add up. | Coherent forecasts (reconciliation) |
| **High frequency, mixed timing** | Weekly claims, daily spreads, monthly payrolls, quarterly GDP | Series arrive on different days and at different frequencies | Bridge equations, the release calendar |

## The release calendar and the ragged edge

Every statistic has a release schedule. These are the ones that matter most for U.S. nowcasting:

| Statistic | Agency | Frequency | When it comes out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial jobless claims (`ICSA`) | Labor Department (DOL) | Weekly | Thursdays at 8:30 a.m. ET (Wednesday in Thanksgiving week), for the week ending the previous Saturday<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> |
| Nonfarm payrolls (`PAYEMS`) | Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) | Monthly | Usually the first Friday of the next month, for the pay period that includes the 12th<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> |
| Retail sales (`RSAFS`) | Census Bureau | Monthly | About ten business days after the month ends<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> |
| Real GDP growth (`A191RL1Q225SBEA`) | Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) | Quarterly | Advance estimate near the end of the month after the quarter ends, then a second and third estimate a month apart<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> |

Line them up on any given day, and the newest observation of each series falls in a different month. Claims are current to last Saturday. Payrolls are current to last month. GDP is current to last quarter. This uneven end of the dataset is called the <Term def="The uneven end of a dataset at any moment: because series are published on different schedules, the latest available observation falls in a different period for each one.">ragged edge</Term>, and every nowcasting model must handle it.

In plain words, each series has a <Term def="The time between the end of the period a statistic describes and the day it is published. Claims: about 5 days. Payrolls: about 1 week. Retail sales: about 2 weeks. GDP: about 4 weeks after the quarter ends.">publication lag</Term>: the wait between the end of the period and the day the number comes out. Claims for the week ending Saturday, November 7, came out five days later. October's payrolls came out on Friday, November 6, about a week after October ended. Picture a spreadsheet with one column per series. Every column stops at a different row. The ragged edge is that jagged bottom of the sheet, and a nowcast is your best guess for the empty cells.

## Mixed frequencies: one number per month

Before any nowcast, you must put a weekly or daily series on the same footing as a monthly target. [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#changing-frequency-sum-average-or-last-value) gave the general rule: it depends on what the series measures. Nowcasting adds a twist, because months are not all the same length.

| What the series is | Example in this chapter | Sensible monthly number | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flow**: an amount *during* each week | Initial claims (`ICSA`): new filings that week | **Average week** (a rate). A monthly total only if you adjust for the number of weeks | A plain sum compared month to month |
| **Stock**: a level *at a point* in time | Continuing claims (`CCSA`): people on benefits that week | **End of month**, or the monthly average | Any sum: it counts the same people again every week |
| **Price or rate** | Gasoline price, oil price, interest rates | **Average** (what was paid during the month) or **end of month** (where it closed) | Any sum |

Here is the twist. The bridge lab assigns each week to the month that contains its week-ending Saturday. A month then holds four or five weeks. October 2026 has five Saturdays (the 3rd through the 31st). September has four.

Ask one question of every fast series: *is this an amount during the period, a level at a moment, or a price?* Then:
<ul>
<li><strong>Flows</strong> (claims, sales, card spending): use the average per week or per day. If you need a monthly total, divide by the number of weeks or days and multiply by a standard month, or compare each month only with the same month a year earlier.</li>

<li><strong>Prices and rates</strong> (gas, oil, yields, spreads): average, unless your question is "where did the month close?"</li>
</ul>

</Callout>

## Nowcasting with a bridge equation

The simplest nowcasting tool is a <Term def="A regression that links a low-frequency target (monthly or quarterly) to high-frequency indicators aggregated to the same frequency. It 'bridges' the frequencies, and it is re-run as each new high-frequency print arrives.">bridge equation</Term>. It has three steps:

1. **Aggregate** the fast indicator to the target's frequency. For example, average the weekly claims in each month.
2. **Regress** the target on the aggregated indicator, using past months.
3. **Update** the nowcast each time a new weekly print arrives, using the partial-month average.

Our target is the monthly change in payrolls (thousands of jobs, SA). The predictors are last month's change and the change in average weekly claims from last month. First, a prediction about the most famous month in the data.

The band barely narrows (about ±222,000 with no claims, ±210,000 with a full month). Weekly claims are a **turning-point** indicator: they shine when layoffs surge and add little when the labor market is steady.

<Callout type="math" title="The bridge equation, written out">
Let $y_t$ be the payroll change in month $t$ and $c_{t,k}$ the average of the first $k$ weekly claims prints whose week ends in month $t$. With $k$ weeks known, the nowcast comes from

$$
y_t = \alpha_k + \phi_k\, y_{t-1} + \beta_k \left(c_{t,k} - \bar c_{t-1}\right) + \varepsilon_{t,k},
$$

where $\bar c_{t-1}$ is last month's full average. There is one regression for each $k = 0, 1, \dots, K$. With $k = 0$ it is a plain AR(1) on payroll changes. The 90% band is $\hat y_t \pm 1.645\,\hat\sigma_k$. Mixed-data sampling (MIDAS) regressions and dynamic factor models handle many indicators and the ragged edge in one model;<sup>[7](#refs)</sup> the bridge equation is their transparent ancestor.
</Callout>

### Reading a nowcast as the data arrive

A nowcast is not one number. It is a sequence of numbers, one after each release, and professionals read the **changes**. Every change has two parts. The **news** is the part of the new print you did not expect. The **coefficient** says how many jobs each unit of news is worth. Read the bridge coefficient in plain words first: with two weeks of claims known, each extra 1,000 claims a week in the month's average goes with about **2,400 fewer jobs** that month, holding last month's payroll change fixed.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Read every revision as coefficient × surprise">
When a nowcast moves, write the move as a sentence with units: "Claims came in 20,000 above last week, which raised the month's average by 10,000; at about 2.4 jobs per claim, the nowcast fell by 24,000." Three checks follow from that sentence:
<ul>
<li><strong>Is the size plausible?</strong> If a modest surprise moves the nowcast by hundreds of thousands, look for a units error (claims in thousands vs ones) or a partial month treated as a full one.</li>
<li><strong>Is the sign right?</strong> More claims should lower the jobs nowcast. A wrong sign usually means a mis-merged month.</li>
<li><strong>Is it news?</strong> Only the unexpected part should move the nowcast. GDPNow and the New York Fed publish exactly this "news" breakdown after each update.<sup>[26](#refs)</sup></li>
</ul>
Here "expected" simply means "the same as last week". Professional systems use the model's own forecast of each indicator, but the logic is the same.
</Callout>

## Real nowcasts: GDPNow and the New York Fed

Two public nowcasts of U.S. GDP growth are watched by markets every week:

- **GDPNow** (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, public since 2014) builds GDP up from its components with bridge equations and related models, and updates as each source release arrives.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup> It is not an official forecast and involves no judgment.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup>
- **The New York Fed Staff Nowcast** (launched 2016) is a <Term def="A model in which many observed series are driven by a few unobserved common factors that evolve over time. The Kalman filter updates the factors as each new data point arrives, so it handles the ragged edge naturally.">dynamic factor model</Term>: it extracts the common movement of dozens of indicators, the idea of session 2.<sup>[10](#refs)</sup>

<Callout type="business" title="How professionals use a nowcast">
Traders use a nowcast to judge whether the official number will surprise; policymakers use it to see the economy weeks earlier. Analysts read the <em>changes</em>: which release moved it, and by how much. A nowcast is a tool for being less surprised, not for being right to the decimal.
</Callout>

How accurate GDPNow is depends on which vintage of GDP you score it against; see Beyond the basics below.

## Many series, one factor

You can't put 200 predictors into a regression with 400 observations and expect stable coefficients. The way out is an old observation: economic series move together. Jobs, output, spending and housing tend to rise in expansions and fall in recessions. If most of that shared movement comes from one or two hidden <Term def="An unobserved variable that drives the co-movement of many observed series. In macroeconomics the first factor usually looks like overall economic activity.">factors</Term>, you can estimate the factors and forecast with them instead.

The standard estimator is <Term def="A method that finds the weighted averages of standardized variables that capture the most variance. The first principal component is the single weighted average that explains the most shared variation.">principal components</Term> (PCA). Clipping 68 of the 8,100 values (47 of them in 2020) at ±4 standard deviations keeps the factor about the business cycle, not about one spring.

### Prepare the panel: transform, then standardize

The wall looked clean because two preparation steps happened before PCA. Every series was turned into a monthly growth rate or change, and every series was **standardized**: converted to a <Term def="A value expressed as standard deviations from the series' own mean: z = (x − mean) / sd. A z-score of −2 means 'two standard deviations below normal for this series'.">z-score</Term>, so 0 means "a normal month for this series" and −2 means "two standard deviations below normal". Skip either step and PCA quietly answers a different question.<sup>[27](#refs)</sup> Without standardizing, the factor is mostly the series with the biggest swings (claims swing by about 14% in a typical month, payrolls by 0.8%). In log levels, it is mostly a time trend. Beyond the basics below lets you try all four versions.

<Callout type="empirical" title="Make every series stationary before you combine them">
Series that all grow are correlated in levels just because they grow; in log levels PCA "finds" that shared trend and calls it 55% of the variance. Before PCA or any regression on many series:
<ul>
<li>Turn levels that grow (jobs, output, sales, prices) into <strong>growth rates</strong>: 100 × the change in the log, which is roughly the % change. See [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#transformations-every-analyst-uses) for the transformation and [Chapter 7](/chapters/arima/#the-cure-differencing) for why differencing removes a wandering (stochastic) trend.</li>
<li>Turn rates, spreads and indexes (hours, credit spreads, the NFCI) into <strong>changes</strong>.</li>
<li>Use <strong>seasonally adjusted</strong> data, or the factor may just be the calendar.</li>
<li>Then <strong>standardize</strong> and <strong>clip extreme months</strong> (±4 standard deviations here), so no single series or single month owns the factor.</li>
</ul>
</Callout>

<Callout type="empirical" title="Read loadings and “% of variance” in plain words">
<ul>
<li><strong>"% of variance explained."</strong> "Component 1 explains 36%" means: one weighted average of the 20 standardized series captures 36% of all their month-to-month movement. That is as much as about 7 of the 20 series (0.36 × 20) would carry on their own. The next component adds only 11%.</li>
<li><strong>Loadings.</strong> A loading is a weight. Retail sales excluding autos (+0.30) and vehicle sales (+0.30) carry the most weight; payrolls carry +0.14. A <em>negative</em> loading (initial claims −0.18, credit spreads −0.20) means the series rises when the factor falls, which is exactly what claims and spreads do in a downturn.</li>
<li><strong>The factor's units.</strong> The factor is in "standard units", not percent or jobs. Read it relative to zero: 0 is an average month.</li>
<li><strong>The sign is arbitrary.</strong> PCA can return the factor upside down. Flip it so that payrolls load positively, then "up" means "stronger economy".</li>
</ul>
A loading is not a causal effect. It only says how strongly a series moves with the common factor.
</Callout>

## Many series that must add up

The second "big" problem is not many predictors but many **targets**. A retailer forecasts every product in every store. The sum of those forecasts should equal the company forecast that finance uses, or two departments will plan for two different futures. Series that sum to totals form a <Term def="A collection of time series where some are sums of others, such as stores within regions within a country, or categories within total sales.">hierarchy</Term>. Forecasts that respect the sums are called <Term def="Forecasts in which every aggregate equals the sum of its components, just as the actual data do.">coherent</Term>.

U.S. retail sales form a real hierarchy. Total retail and food services (`RSAFS`) = motor vehicles & parts (`RSMVPD`) + everything else (`RSFSXMV`). Everything else splits into nine published categories plus "Other" (grocery, sporting goods and miscellaneous stores, computed as the remainder).

**Reconciliation in plain words.** Forecast each of the 13 series on its own and the numbers will not add up, because each model learned only from its own series. Reconciliation is the bookkeeping step that fixes this: nudge the forecasts until every total equals the sum of its parts, the way the real data do. The methods differ only in *who gets nudged*. **Bottom-up** trusts the parts and rewrites the total. **Top-down** trusts the total and splits it by historical shares. Reconciliation methods (OLS, WLS, MinT) move every forecast a little.<sup>[23](#refs)</sup>

Whatever method you use, check that the sums hold, and reconcile in dollars, not in logs: logs of parts do not add up to the log of the total.

## Chapter checklist

- "Big" means three things: **many predictors**, **many series**, and **mixed frequencies**. Each has its own tool.
- Know the **release calendar**. Every series has a **publication lag**, every nowcast lives on the **ragged edge**, and every evaluation must use only data available on the nowcast date.
- Before merging frequencies, decide **flow, stock or price**. Average a flow per week (a plain sum makes five-week months look about 22% bigger), take a stock at month end, average a price.
- A **bridge equation** aggregates fast data to the target frequency and is re-run as each print arrives. Read every revision as **coefficient × surprise**, in units: 20,000 extra claims in one week ≈ 24,000 fewer jobs. Claims help most at **turning points**.
- Before PCA, make each series **stationary** (growth rates or changes, SA), then **standardize** to z-scores and clip outliers. Otherwise the "factor" is a trend or the noisiest series.
- Read PCA in words: "% of variance" is how much of the panel's movement one index captures; **loadings** are weights, and a negative loading means the series rises when the factor falls. Flip the sign so "up" means stronger.
- Forecasts of a **hierarchy** must be **coherent**: the totals must add up. Bottom-up, top-down and reconciliation differ only in who gets nudged.

#### When the calendar breaks: the 2025 shutdown

The federal government shut down from October 1 to November 12, 2025, for 43 days. During a lapse in funding, BLS doesn't collect data. It never published an October 2025 CPI, and the October unemployment rate was never measured.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup> You can see the hole in the bundled data: `UNRATE.csv` and `CPIAUCSL.csv` have a blank for 2025-10. The national claims report stopped too, but the Labor Department kept posting state-level claims files. Private economists added them up each week, and those estimates became one of the few timely signals.<sup>[24](#refs)</sup> When official data stop, nowcasts become more valuable.

#### Line up the dates before you merge

Most nowcasting bugs are date bugs, not model bugs.

- **Know what a date means.** A claims date is the *last* day of a week (a Saturday). The week ending Saturday, August 1, 2026, is six days of July and one of August, yet it counts as an August week. A FRED monthly date such as 2026-10-01 means "the whole of October", not October 1.
- **Know the reference period.** The payroll survey counts jobs in the pay period that includes the **12th**. That is why claims from late March 2020 barely showed up in the March jobs report.
- **Count your weeks.** After aggregating, tabulate how many weeks each month got (the bridge lab's `weeks` column). A newest month with one week is a *partial* month, not a calm one.
- **Going from months to quarters?** GDP is a flow at an annual rate, so a quarter is the *average* of its three months, as [Chapter 1](/chapters/time-series-data/#changing-frequency-sum-average-or-last-value) explains for SAAR data.

For prices, the choice between **end of month** and **average** is real: in the flow/stock explorable, the end-of-month gasoline or oil price is jumpier, because it is one print, not an average of four or twenty. Consumer spending responds to the average price paid; a trader marking a position cares about the close.

The bundled real GDP growth series (`A191RL1Q225SBEA`) is the **latest vintage**: the advance estimate plus every revision since. Those are different targets, and that difference matters.

#### Principal components in four lines

<Callout type="math" title="Principal components in four lines">
Stack the standardized panel into a $T \times N$ matrix $Z$ (months × series). Its correlation matrix is $R = Z'Z/(T-1)$. PCA finds the eigenvectors $w_1, w_2, \dots$ of $R$, sorted by eigenvalue $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots$:

$$
f_{1t} = \sum_{j=1}^{N} w_{1j}\, z_{jt}, \qquad \text{share}_1 = \frac{\lambda_1}{\sum_j \lambda_j}.
$$

The weights $w_1$ are the **loadings**, and $f_1$ is the first factor. The sign of $w_1$ is arbitrary, so we flip it to make payrolls load positively. Stock and Watson showed that with many series, the principal components consistently estimate the true factors, even when each series is noisy.<sup>[14](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

#### Forecasting with factors: the diffusion index

A factor is useful for more than charts. Stock and Watson's <Term def="A forecasting regression that adds a few estimated factors from a large panel to a small benchmark model, such as an autoregression. Named for the way it 'diffuses' information from many series into a few predictors.">diffusion index</Term> forecast adds the first few factors to a benchmark autoregression.<sup>[16](#refs)</sup> In words: next month's growth = a constant + a share of this month's and last month's growth (the AR benchmark) + a weight on each of the first two factors this month + a surprise. If the factors carry no extra information, their weights are near zero and the forecast is just the AR.

<Callout type="math" title="The diffusion-index regression">
$$
y_{t+1} = \alpha + \phi_1 y_t + \phi_2 y_{t-1} + \gamma_1 \hat f_{1t} + \gamma_2 \hat f_{2t} + \varepsilon_{t+1},
$$

where $\hat f_{1t}$ and $\hat f_{2t}$ are the first two principal components of the panel, estimated with data through month $t$ only.
</Callout>

The honest test re-estimates the factors **at every forecast origin**, using only data available then. The lab does exactly that for next month's industrial production growth, with 179 forecast origins from January 2005 to November 2019 (so the forecasts cover February 2005 to December 2019).

#### Reconciliation in detail

In the code lab below, the total's own forecast for October 2025 is about \$0.9 billion *below* the sum of its 11 parts, and the gap grows to \$3.0 billion by December. Four ways to close it:

| Method | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bottom-up** | Forecast the bottom series, add them up | Keeps detail; nothing is lost | Bottom series are noisy |
| **Top-down** | Forecast the total, split by historical shares | Total is smooth and easy to forecast | Misses shifts in the mix (e.g. the move to online) |
| **OLS reconciliation** | Adjust all forecasts by the smallest equal-weighted amount that makes them add up | Uses every level | Treats a \$700 billion total and an \$8 billion category as equally noisy |
| **WLS / MinT** | Same, but weighted by how noisy each series' forecasts are | Often the most accurate in published comparisons<sup>[23](#refs)</sup> | Needs good estimates of the error variances |

In the code lab below (ETS base forecasts, 12 test months from October 2025 to September 2026), **bottom-up** gave the most accurate total, with a MAPE of 1.87% against 2.66% for the total's own model. **Top-down** did worst one level down (ex-autos MAPE 3.26%), and it missed the shift to online shopping: its nonstore MAPE was 5.59%, against 1.16% for the series' own model. **OLS** reconciliation hurt small categories such as electronics (MAPE 4.60% → 6.35%), because it spread the total's large errors evenly. The explorable uses a simpler damped-Holt model and finds the same ranking for the total.

<Callout type="math" title="Reconciliation and MinT in one formula">
Collect all 13 base forecasts in $\hat y$ and the 11 bottom-level series in $b$. The summing matrix $S$ (13 × 11) maps bottom to all: $y = Sb$. Any reconciliation method takes the form

$$
\tilde y = S G \hat y, \qquad G = (S' W^{-1} S)^{-1} S' W^{-1}.
$$

With $W = I$ this is OLS. With $W = \text{diag}(S\mathbf 1)$ (each node weighted by how many bottom series it contains) it is structural WLS. **MinT** (minimum trace) sets $W$ to the estimated covariance matrix of the base forecast errors. Wickramasuriya, Athanasopoulos and Hyndman proved that this choice minimizes the total forecast-error variance among all unbiased reconciliations of this form.<sup>[17](#refs)</sup> Bottom-up is $G = [\,0 \mid I\,]$, which simply ignores the upper-level forecasts.
</Callout>

#### Alternative data: Google searches, card swipes, satellites

Official data are slow because they come from careful surveys. Private "exhaust" data are fast because they are by-products of everyday activity:

- **Search data.** Choi and Varian added Google Trends search volumes to simple seasonal autoregressions and improved nowcasts of auto sales, initial unemployment claims, travel and consumer confidence.<sup>[18](#refs)</sup> People search for "unemployment benefits" before they file.
- **Card spending.** During COVID, Chetty and coauthors built a public tracker from anonymized card transactions and other private data. It showed spending falling in close to real time, weeks before the official monthly statistics.<sup>[19](#refs)</sup>
- **Satellites.** Night-light intensity seen from space tracks GDP growth. It is especially useful in countries where official statistics are weak.<sup>[20](#refs)</sup>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The parable of Google Flu Trends">
Google Flu Trends estimated flu cases from searches and was celebrated in 2009. In the 2012–13 season it roughly doubled the CDC's estimate of doctor visits for flu-like illness.<sup>[21](#refs)</sup> The causes were overfitting to search terms that happened to correlate with winter, and changes to Google's own search engine. Alternative data need the same discipline as any other data: out-of-sample tests against a benchmark, and suspicion when the data-generating process can change under you.
</Callout>

#### Working at scale

Most of "big data" forecasting is plain good engineering:

- **Write one function, apply it to a list.** The factor lab transforms 20 series with one `lapply()` call. The hierarchy lab fits 13 models with one `sapply()`. The same code works for 30,000 series. In Python, use dictionary comprehensions or `groupby().apply()`.
- **Vectorize.** Operate on whole columns at once, not element by element. Matrix algebra did all of reconciliation in two lines.
- **Use the right storage.** When data stop fitting comfortably in memory, R's `data.table`, `arrow`, and `duckdb` (an in-process SQL database) handle hundreds of millions of rows on a laptop. In Python, use `polars` or `duckdb`.
- **Parallelize the loop, not the model.** Forecasting many series is "embarrassingly parallel": each series is independent. `future.apply` in R and `joblib` in Python spread the loop across cores.
