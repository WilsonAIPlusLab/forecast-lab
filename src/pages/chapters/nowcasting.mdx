<p class="lede">It is mid-November 2026. Your company must decide this week whether to keep hiring through the holidays. The official November jobs report won't arrive until <strong>December 4</strong>. The first estimate of fourth-quarter GDP won't arrive until around the end of January. But the Labor Department published fresh jobless claims on Thursday, and it will do so again next Thursday. <strong>What is the economy doing this month, before the data are out?</strong></p>

Forecasting the present sounds like a contradiction. It isn't. Official statistics arrive weeks or months after the period they describe, and each one arrives on its own day. <Term def="Estimating the current (or just-finished) period's value of a slowly published statistic, such as GDP or payrolls, from faster data that are already available. A blend of 'now' and 'forecast'.">Nowcasting</Term> fills that gap with faster, noisier data. Central banks, investment banks and large retailers all run nowcasts. This chapter shows you how they work, what "big data" really changes in forecasting, and where the gains are smaller than the hype.

<KeyIdea>A nowcast is only as good as what it knows <em>on the day you make it</em>. Track exactly which data exist on that date, and judge the model only with information that was really available.</KeyIdea>

## Three kinds of "big"

"Big data" in forecasting usually means one of three problems. Each needs a different tool.

| The problem | Example | Why ordinary regression struggles | Tool in this chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Many predictors** (*p* large relative to *n*) | 20 or 200 monthly indicators, but only ~400 months of history | Too many coefficients to estimate. The fit chases noise. | Principal components and factor models |
| **Many series** | 13 retail series (11 categories and two totals), or 30,000 store × product series | You can't hand-build 30,000 models. Their forecasts must add up. | Loops over lists, hierarchical reconciliation |
| **High frequency, mixed timing** | Weekly claims, daily spreads, monthly payrolls, quarterly GDP | Series arrive on different days and at different frequencies | Bridge equations, the release calendar |

## The release calendar and the ragged edge

Every statistic has a release schedule. These are the ones that matter most for U.S. nowcasting:

| Statistic | Agency | Frequency | When it comes out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial jobless claims (`ICSA`) | Labor Department (DOL) | Weekly | Thursdays at 8:30 a.m. ET (Wednesday in Thanksgiving week), for the week ending the previous Saturday<sup>[1](#refs)</sup> |
| Nonfarm payrolls (`PAYEMS`) | Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) | Monthly | Usually the first Friday of the next month, for the pay period that includes the 12th<sup>[2](#refs)</sup> |
| Retail sales (`RSAFS`) | Census Bureau | Monthly | About ten business days after the month ends<sup>[3](#refs)</sup> |
| Real GDP growth (`A191RL1Q225SBEA`) | Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) | Quarterly | Advance estimate near the end of the month after the quarter ends, then a second and third estimate a month apart<sup>[4](#refs)</sup> |

Line them up on any given day, and the newest observation of each series falls in a different month. Claims are current to last Saturday. Payrolls are current to last month. GDP is current to last quarter. This uneven end of the dataset is called the <Term def="The uneven end of a dataset at any moment: because series are published on different schedules, the latest available observation falls in a different period for each one.">ragged edge</Term>, and every nowcasting model must handle it.

<Callout type="pitfall" title="When the calendar breaks: the 2025 shutdown">
The federal government shut down from October 1 to November 12, 2025, for 43 days. During a lapse in funding, BLS doesn't collect data. It never published an October 2025 CPI, and the October unemployment rate was never measured.<sup>[5](#refs)</sup> You can see the hole in the bundled data: `UNRATE.csv` and `CPIAUCSL.csv` have a blank for 2025-10. The national claims report stopped too, but the Labor Department kept posting state-level claims files. Private economists added them up each week, and those estimates became one of the few timely signals.<sup>[24](#refs)</sup> When official data stop, nowcasts become more valuable.
</Callout>

## Nowcasting with a bridge equation

The simplest nowcasting tool is a <Term def="A regression that links a low-frequency target (monthly or quarterly) to high-frequency indicators aggregated to the same frequency. It 'bridges' the frequencies, and it is re-run as each new high-frequency print arrives.">bridge equation</Term>. It has three steps:

1. **Aggregate** the fast indicator to the target's frequency. For example, average the weekly claims in each month.
2. **Regress** the target on the aggregated indicator, using past months.
3. **Update** the nowcast each time a new weekly print arrives, using the partial-month average.

[Chapter 8](/chapters/dynamic-regression/) found that claims add almost nothing to a one-month-ahead payroll forecast in calm years, but that a same-month nowcast cut the error by about 10% in the Great Recession. A bridge equation turns that nowcast into a tool you update every Thursday.

Our target is the monthly change in payrolls (thousands of jobs, SA). The predictors are last month's change and the change in average weekly claims from last month. Before you play with it, make a prediction about the most famous month in the data.

Play each month and watch the nowcast move:

- **Oct 2026 (calm).** The nowcast barely moves, from about +77,000 to +91,000 jobs as five weeks of claims arrive. The actual change was +165,000. In calm times claims hardly move, so they carry little news about payrolls.
- **Sep 2008 (Lehman).** Claims climbed through the month, and the nowcast slid from −159,000 to −257,000. Here the claims-based nowcast was closer to the eventual truth than the first official number.
- **Mar 2020 (COVID).** The nowcast collapses to about −6.1 million after the last two weeks, far outside its own 90% band. The actual was −1.4 million. The model's error was not bad luck. It was a timing mismatch between what claims measure (the whole month) and what the payroll survey measures (the pay period including the 12th).
- **Nov 2026 (live).** Only one week of claims exists so far. The nowcast is about +144,000 jobs, with a 90% band of roughly ±215,000. The answer arrives on December 4.

Notice how little the band narrows: from about ±222,000 with no claims to about ±210,000 with a full month. That is an honest result. Weekly claims are a **turning-point** indicator. They shine when layoffs surge and add little when the labor market is steady.

<Callout type="math" title="The bridge equation, written out">
Let $y_t$ be the payroll change in month $t$ and $c_{t,k}$ the average of the first $k$ weekly claims prints whose week ends in month $t$. With $k$ weeks known, the nowcast comes from

$$
y_t = \alpha_k + \phi_k\, y_{t-1} + \beta_k \left(c_{t,k} - \bar c_{t-1}\right) + \varepsilon_{t,k},
$$

where $\bar c_{t-1}$ is last month's full average. There is one regression for each $k = 0, 1, \dots, K$. With $k = 0$ it is a plain AR(1) on payroll changes. The 90% band is $\hat y_t \pm 1.645\,\hat\sigma_k$. Fancier systems, such as mixed-data sampling (MIDAS) regressions and dynamic factor models with a Kalman filter, handle many indicators and the ragged edge in one model.<sup>[7](#refs)</sup> The bridge equation is the transparent ancestor of both.
</Callout>

Now build it yourself. The lab aggregates the weekly file to months, fits the bridge, nowcasts November 2026 from the single week available, and then runs the honest test: re-fit using only past data and nowcast each month as it would have happened.

Two shortcuts in this lab make the evaluation look better than real life:
<ul>

A model that looks great on revised data can disappoint in real time. Say which kind of data you used.
</Callout>

## Real nowcasts: GDPNow and the New York Fed

Two public nowcasts of U.S. GDP growth are watched by markets every week:

- **GDPNow** (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, public since 2014, with a track record reconstructed back to 2011) builds GDP up from its components. It forecasts consumer spending, investment, government and net exports with bridge equations and related models, and updates as each source release arrives.<sup>[8](#refs)</sup> The Atlanta Fed stresses that it is not an official forecast and involves no judgment.<sup>[9](#refs)</sup>
- **The New York Fed Staff Nowcast** (launched 2016) is a <Term def="A model in which many observed series are driven by a few unobserved common factors that evolve over time. The Kalman filter updates the factors as each new data point arrives, so it handles the ragged edge naturally.">dynamic factor model</Term>. It reads dozens of indicators, extracts their common movements, and updates the GDP estimate with each release.<sup>[10](#refs)</sup>

How good are they? The bundled `GDPNOW` series holds GDPNow's **final** nowcast for each quarter, made just before BEA's advance estimate. The bundled real GDP growth series (`A191RL1Q225SBEA`) is the **latest vintage**: the advance estimate plus every revision since. Those are different targets, and that difference matters.

## Many series, one factor

GDPNow and the New York Fed read dozens of indicators. You can't put 200 predictors into a regression with 400 observations and expect stable coefficients. The way out is an old observation: economic series move together. Jobs, output, spending and housing tend to rise in expansions and fall in recessions. If most of that shared movement comes from one or two hidden <Term def="An unobserved variable that drives the co-movement of many observed series. In macroeconomics the first factor usually looks like overall economic activity.">factors</Term>, you can estimate the factors and forecast with them instead.

The standard estimator is <Term def="A method that finds the weighted averages of standardized variables that capture the most variance. The first principal component is the single weighted average that explains the most shared variation.">principal components</Term> (PCA). Below are 20 bundled monthly indicators, each turned into a monthly growth rate or change and standardized. Look at the wall first. Can you see the recessions?

One component explains **36%** of the variation across all 20 series. The second explains 11%, and the rest trail off. That pattern in the **scree plot** says the panel really does have one dominant common factor. Its 3-month average plunges in 2001, 2008–09 and 2020, and it sat at **0.12** in September 2026: close to average growth.

**What surprised you?** Uncheck "Clip outliers". The first component jumps to **49%** and the 2020 plunge dwarfs everything else. In spring 2020 some series sit up to 19 standard deviations from normal. Principal components minimize squared distances, so a single extreme month can hijack the whole analysis. Clipping 68 of the 8,100 values (47 of them in 2020) at ±4 standard deviations keeps the factor about the business cycle, not about one spring.

<Callout type="math" title="Principal components in four lines">
Stack the standardized panel into a $T \times N$ matrix $Z$ (months × series). Its correlation matrix is $R = Z'Z/(T-1)$. PCA finds the eigenvectors $w_1, w_2, \dots$ of $R$, sorted by eigenvalue $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots$:

$$
f_{1t} = \sum_{j=1}^{N} w_{1j}\, z_{jt}, \qquad \text{share}_1 = \frac{\lambda_1}{\sum_j \lambda_j}.
$$

The weights $w_1$ are the **loadings**, and $f_1$ is the first factor. The sign of $w_1$ is arbitrary, so we flip it to make payrolls load positively. Stock and Watson showed that with many series, the principal components consistently estimate the true factors, even when each series is noisy.<sup>[14](#refs)</sup>
</Callout>

## Forecasting with factors: the diffusion index

A factor is useful for more than charts. Stock and Watson's <Term def="A forecasting regression that adds a few estimated factors from a large panel to a small benchmark model, such as an autoregression. Named for the way it 'diffuses' information from many series into a few predictors.">diffusion index</Term> forecast adds the first few factors to a benchmark autoregression:<sup>[16](#refs)</sup>

$$
y_{t+1} = \alpha + \phi_1 y_t + \phi_2 y_{t-1} + \gamma_1 \hat f_{1t} + \gamma_2 \hat f_{2t} + \varepsilon_{t+1}.
$$

The honest test re-estimates the factors **at every forecast origin**, using only data available then. The lab does exactly that for next month's industrial production growth, with 179 forecast origins from January 2005 to November 2019 (so the forecasts cover February 2005 to December 2019).

## Many series that must add up

The second "big" problem is not many predictors but many **targets**. A retailer forecasts every product in every store. The sum of those forecasts should equal the company forecast that finance uses, or two departments will plan for two different futures. Series that sum to totals form a <Term def="A collection of time series where some are sums of others, such as stores within regions within a country, or categories within total sales.">hierarchy</Term>. Forecasts that respect the sums are called <Term def="Forecasts in which every aggregate equals the sum of its components, just as the actual data do.">coherent</Term>.

U.S. retail sales form a real hierarchy. Total retail and food services (`RSAFS`) = motor vehicles & parts (`RSMVPD`) + everything else (`RSFSXMV`). Everything else splits into nine published categories plus "Other" (grocery, sporting goods and miscellaneous stores, computed as the remainder).

| Method | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bottom-up** | Forecast the bottom series, add them up | Keeps detail; nothing is lost | Bottom series are noisy |
| **Top-down** | Forecast the total, split by historical shares | Total is smooth and easy to forecast | Misses shifts in the mix (e.g. the move to online) |
| **OLS reconciliation** | Adjust all forecasts by the smallest equal-weighted amount that makes them add up | Uses every level | Treats a \$700 billion total and an \$8 billion category as equally noisy |
| **WLS / MinT** | Same, but weighted by how noisy each series' forecasts are | Often the most accurate in published comparisons<sup>[23](#refs)</sup> | Needs good estimates of the error variances |

In the code lab below (ETS base forecasts, 12 test months from October 2025 to September 2026), **bottom-up** gave the most accurate total, with a MAPE of 1.87% against 2.66% for the total's own model. **Top-down** did worst one level down (ex-autos MAPE 3.26%), and it missed the shift to online shopping: its nonstore MAPE was 5.59%, against 1.16% for the series' own model. **OLS** reconciliation hurt small categories such as electronics (MAPE 4.60% → 6.35%), because it spread the total's large errors evenly. The explorable uses a simpler damped-Holt model and finds the same ranking for the total.

<Callout type="math" title="Reconciliation and MinT in one formula">
Collect all 13 base forecasts in $\hat y$ and the 11 bottom-level series in $b$. The summing matrix $S$ (13 × 11) maps bottom to all: $y = Sb$. Any reconciliation method takes the form

$$
\tilde y = S G \hat y, \qquad G = (S' W^{-1} S)^{-1} S' W^{-1}.
$$

With $W = I$ this is OLS. With $W = \text{diag}(S\mathbf 1)$ (each node weighted by how many bottom series it contains) it is structural WLS. **MinT** (minimum trace) sets $W$ to the estimated covariance matrix of the base forecast errors. Wickramasuriya, Athanasopoulos and Hyndman proved that this choice minimizes the total forecast-error variance among all unbiased reconciliations of this form.<sup>[17](#refs)</sup> Bottom-up is $G = [\,0 \mid I\,]$, which simply ignores the upper-level forecasts.
</Callout>

## Alternative data: Google searches, card swipes, satellites

Official data are slow because they come from careful surveys. Private "exhaust" data are fast because they are by-products of everyday activity:

- **Search data.** Choi and Varian added Google Trends search volumes to simple seasonal autoregressions and improved nowcasts of auto sales, initial unemployment claims, travel and consumer confidence.<sup>[18](#refs)</sup> People search for "unemployment benefits" before they file.
- **Card spending.** During COVID, Chetty and coauthors built a public tracker from anonymized card transactions and other private data. It showed spending falling in close to real time, weeks before the official monthly statistics.<sup>[19](#refs)</sup>
- **Satellites.** Night-light intensity seen from space tracks GDP growth. It is especially useful in countries where official statistics are weak.<sup>[20](#refs)</sup>

<Callout type="pitfall" title="The parable of Google Flu Trends">
Google Flu Trends estimated flu cases from searches and was celebrated in 2009. In the 2012–13 season it roughly doubled the CDC's estimate of doctor visits for flu-like illness.<sup>[21](#refs)</sup> The causes were overfitting to search terms that happened to correlate with winter, and changes to Google's own search engine. Alternative data need the same discipline as any other data: out-of-sample tests against a benchmark, and suspicion when the data-generating process can change under you.
</Callout>

## Working at scale

Most of "big data" forecasting is plain good engineering:

- **Write one function, apply it to a list.** The factor lab transforms 20 series with one `lapply()` call. The hierarchy lab fits 13 models with one `sapply()`. The same code works for 30,000 series. In Python, use dictionary comprehensions or `groupby().apply()`.
- **Vectorize.** Operate on whole columns at once, not element by element. Matrix algebra did all of reconciliation in two lines.
- **Use the right storage.** When data stop fitting comfortably in memory, R's `data.table`, `arrow`, and `duckdb` (an in-process SQL database) handle hundreds of millions of rows on a laptop. In Python, use `polars` or `duckdb`.
- **Parallelize the loop, not the model.** Forecasting many series is "embarrassingly parallel": each series is independent. `future.apply` in R and `joblib` in Python spread the loop across cores.

## Chapter checklist

- "Big" means three things: **many predictors**, **many series**, and **mixed frequencies**. Each has its own tool.
- Know the **release calendar**. Every nowcast lives on the **ragged edge**, and every evaluation must use only data available on the nowcast date.
- A **bridge equation** aggregates fast data to the target frequency and is re-run as each print arrives. Claims help most at **turning points**.
- **Principal components** compress many indicators into a few factors. Read the **scree plot**, standardize first, and tame outliers.
- **Diffusion-index** forecasts usually beat an AR benchmark modestly. Re-estimate factors in real time.
- Forecasts of a **hierarchy** must be **coherent**: bottom-up, top-down or reconciliation (OLS, WLS, MinT). The best method can differ by level.
- Always ask **which vintage** a forecast is scored against. Revisions are part of the "error".

**Nowcasting desk (30 minutes).** Teams of three each take one month in the ragged-edge explorable.

1. **Predict (5 min).** Before pressing Play, each team writes its nowcast for the payroll change and a 90% range.
2. **Play (10 min).** Step through the month day by day. Record the nowcast after each Thursday's claims print. Note when it moves most.
3. **Debrief (15 min).** Each team reports its path and the miss on jobs report day.

*Expected results (from the explorable's model):*
- **October 2026:** the nowcast drifts from about +77k to +91k as five weeks arrive; actual +165k.
- **September 2008:** −159k → −257k; actual (revised) −451k, first print −159k.
- **March 2020:** about +230k with no claims or one week, +157k after two weeks, then −2.2 million and −6.1 million after the last two weeks; actual −1.4 million (first print −701k).
- **November 2026:** +144k after one week, band about ±215k; the answer arrives December 4, 2026. Ask students to post their nowcast and check it in class after the release.

**Follow-up (20 minutes).** Run the hierarchy lab. Ask: *which method would you ship if finance only cares about the total, and which if store managers care about their own category?* Expected: bottom-up for the total (MAPE 1.87%); base or bottom-up for the categories (average 3.52%); OLS hurts small categories (average 4.00%).
